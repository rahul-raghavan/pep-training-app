## Section 1: How Experts Think Differently
**Slug:** how-experts-think-differently
**Estimated Minutes:** 15

### Content

# What's in an Expert's Head?

Think about a time you walked into a colleague's classroom and instantly knew something was off — a student about to disengage, a group losing focus, an activity falling flat. You knew it before you could articulate why.

Now think about a student teacher walking into the same room. They see the same things you see — students, materials, activity — but they don't read the situation the way you do. They notice individual details. You notice patterns.

That difference isn't intuition. It isn't experience alone. It's the result of something cognitive scientists call **schemas** — organised knowledge structures stored in long-term memory that allow you to recognise patterns, make connections, and respond efficiently.

# Schemas: How Knowledge Gets Organised

A schema is a mental framework that organises related pieces of knowledge into a single, connected structure. Think of it as a mental filing system — but one that doesn't just store information. It connects it, so you can access what you need quickly and see how things relate.

Here's a simple example. When you hear the word "dog," your brain doesn't just retrieve the definition. It activates a whole network: four legs, tail, barking, breeds, pets, loyalty, maybe a specific dog you've known. All of that is your "dog" schema. It's rich, connected, and instantly available.

A toddler hearing the word "dog" for the first time has no schema. The word means nothing until they connect it to experiences — seeing dogs, touching dogs, hearing dogs. Over time, through many encounters, their schema builds. Eventually, they can recognise a dog they've never seen before, because it fits the pattern their schema predicts.

This is exactly what happens with academic learning. A student who has a strong schema for fractions doesn't just know that 1/2 = 0.5. They understand how fractions relate to division, to decimals, to ratios, to sharing equally. When they encounter a new fraction problem, they can connect it to what they already know. The new information has somewhere to attach.

A student with no fraction schema sees each new problem as completely new. Nothing connects. Everything has to be processed from scratch in working memory.

> [!info]
> **Schemas are the difference between understanding and memorising.** A student who has memorised that 1/2 = 0.5 has a fact. A student who has a fraction schema understands *why* 1/2 = 0.5, can connect it to other ideas, and can apply it flexibly. Building schemas — not just delivering facts — is the real work of teaching.

# The Chess Master Study

One of the most famous studies in cognitive science illustrates this perfectly. Researchers showed chess positions to expert chess players and beginners for just five seconds, then asked them to recreate the positions from memory.

Expert players could recreate the entire board — 20 or more pieces placed accurately. Beginners could place only four or five.

Here's the twist: when the pieces were placed *randomly* (not in positions from a real game), experts performed no better than beginners. Both groups managed only four or five pieces.

What does this tell us? Experts don't have superior memories. They have superior schemas. When the positions came from real games, experts recognised meaningful patterns — "that's a Sicilian Defence setup" — and stored the whole configuration as a single chunk. Beginners, lacking those schemas, had to remember each piece individually.

The same thing happens in your classroom. When you look at a group of students working, you see patterns: "that student is copying without understanding," "that pair is genuinely discussing." A new teacher sees individual actions without the schema to interpret them.

# Chunking: Making the Most of Working Memory

This connects directly to what you learned in LS 101 about working memory holding only 3-5 new items. Schemas let you cheat the system — not by expanding working memory, but by packing more information into each slot.

Think about the letters F, B, I. If you don't know the abbreviation, that's three items in working memory. If you know what FBI stands for, it's one item — one chunk that contains all three letters plus their meaning. You've freed up two working memory slots.

This is called **chunking** — grouping individual items into meaningful units based on existing knowledge. The more schemas you have, the more you can chunk, and the more efficiently you can think.

For your students, this has enormous practical implications:

| Student knowledge | What they see | Working memory load |
|-------------------|---------------|---------------------|
| No schema for multiplication | 7 x 8 = three separate numbers plus an operation | 4 items (heavy) |
| Has multiplication schema | 7 x 8 = a single known fact | 1 item (light) |
| No grammar schema | Subject, verb, object — three separate rules to apply | 3+ items (heavy) |
| Has sentence schema | "A sentence needs someone doing something" — one pattern | 1 item (light) |

# What This Means for Teaching

Here's the practical takeaway: **building expertise is building schemas.** A student who is becoming an expert in any subject is building richer, more connected mental structures that allow them to process new information efficiently and see patterns where novices see chaos.

This means you can't skip the knowledge-building phase. We sometimes talk about wanting students to "think deeply" or "be creative" — and those are worthy goals. But deep thinking requires a foundation of well-organised knowledge. You can't think critically about something you know nothing about. You can't be creative with tools you haven't mastered.

In elementary, this is why the Montessori materials work in a specific sequence — each material builds a schema that the next material relies on. The golden beads build a schema for place value that the stamp game then extends. Skip the beads, and the stamp game is just a confusing activity.

In middle school, this is why prerequisite knowledge matters so much. If students don't have a solid schema for basic operations, they can't build a schema for algebraic thinking — the new information has no structure to attach to.

# You Can't Just Tell Them the Schema

One more crucial point. You can't simply hand students a schema by explaining it clearly. Schemas are built through experience — through encountering many examples, making connections, getting feedback, and gradually organising knowledge into patterns.

You can certainly help students build schemas faster (that's what good teaching does). Clear explanations, worked examples, and well-sequenced activities all support schema construction. But the student's brain has to do the building. You provide the materials and the scaffolding. They build the structure.

# Beyond the Basics

In LS 101, you learned the fundamental constraint: working memory holds about 3-5 new items at a time. Everything you teach has to get through this narrow door before it can reach long-term memory.

You also learned the basic distinction between intrinsic load (genuine complexity of the material) and extraneous load (unnecessary difficulty from poor presentation). Now we're going to go deeper — because understanding cognitive load more precisely gives you much more control over your lesson design.

Cognitive load theory, developed by John Sweller and refined over decades of research, actually identifies three types of load. The third type is the one that matters most for learning.

# Intrinsic Load: The Real Complexity

Intrinsic load is the genuine difficulty of the material itself — the number of elements that need to be processed simultaneously and how they interact with each other.

Some things are inherently simple. Learning the name of a shape has low intrinsic load — there's one element (the shape) and one label. Some things are inherently complex. Understanding why the area of a triangle is half the area of the rectangle it fits inside has high intrinsic load — you need to hold the triangle, the rectangle, the relationship between them, and the concept of area all at once.

Here's the key insight: **you can't eliminate intrinsic load without dumbing down the content.** If a concept genuinely requires understanding four interacting elements, there's no way to make it require only one. The complexity is real.

But you CAN manage it. The most effective strategy is **sequencing** — breaking complex material into a series of steps, each of which is within working memory capacity, and building up to the full complexity gradually.

Think of it like building a wall. You can't place the top row of bricks without the bottom rows. And you can't ask working memory to hold all the rows at once. But you can build one row at a time, let each row set (move to long-term memory), and then add the next.

> [!info]
> **Managing intrinsic load is not dumbing down.** Breaking a complex concept into smaller steps doesn't reduce what students learn — it changes the *sequence* in which they learn it. The end result is the same understanding, built on a foundation rather than attempted all at once.

# Extraneous Load: The Unnecessary Tax

Extraneous load is mental effort wasted on things that have nothing to do with the actual content. It's a tax on working memory that produces no learning.

Three of the most common sources of extraneous load:

**Split attention:** When students have to mentally integrate information from two separate sources. A diagram on one page with its explanation on another. A map on the wall that students need to look at while reading instructions on their desk. A teacher talking about one thing while text on the board says something different. Every time a student's attention has to split and then reassemble, working memory pays the price.

The fix: integrate the sources. Put labels directly on diagrams. Keep verbal and visual information aligned. If you're pointing to something, make sure the explanation matches what students are looking at.

**Redundancy:** When the same information is presented in multiple ways simultaneously, and the student has to process both. The classic example: a teacher reads aloud exactly what's written on the board, word for word. Now the student is processing the visual text AND the spoken words AND checking whether they're the same. That's three cognitive tasks instead of one.

The fix: present information through one channel at a time. Either show it on the board and let students read it, or say it while showing a supporting image — but don't read aloud what they can already read.

**Seductive details:** Fun, interesting, tangential information that captures attention but doesn't contribute to the learning goal. A fascinating story about the explorer's childhood when the lesson is about map coordinates. A beautiful but complex infographic when the key data is just two numbers.

The fix: this one is hard, because seductive details often feel like they're making the lesson more engaging. They might be. But engagement that consumes working memory without building the target schema is a net negative for learning. Save the stories for moments when working memory isn't being asked to do heavy lifting.

| Extraneous load source | Example | Fix |
|----------------------|---------|-----|
| Split attention | Diagram on board, explanation on handout | Put explanation directly on the diagram |
| Redundancy | Reading aloud what's written on the board | Say it OR show it, not both simultaneously |
| Seductive details | Fascinating tangent during a complex explanation | Save it for a less loaded moment |

# Germane Load: The Good Kind

Here's the type of load that changes the picture completely. **Germane load** is the productive mental effort of building and strengthening schemas. It's the cognitive work you WANT students doing.

When a student is comparing two examples to find the pattern, that's germane load. When a student is connecting a new idea to something they already know, that's germane load. When a student is reorganising their understanding because new information doesn't fit their existing schema, that's germane load.

Germane load is what learning feels like from the inside — effortful, sometimes uncomfortable, but productive. It's the student thinking hard about the right things.

The relationship between the three types of load is like a budget:

**Intrinsic load + Extraneous load + Germane load = Total working memory capacity**

This is a fixed budget. You can't increase total capacity. So every unit of extraneous load you eliminate is a unit freed up for germane load — for actual learning. And if intrinsic load is high (the material is genuinely complex), you need to eliminate even more extraneous load to leave room for germane effort.

> [!warning]
> **The practical formula:** Reduce extraneous load (clean up your presentation), manage intrinsic load (sequence carefully), and maximise germane load (make students think hard about the content, not about the instructions). If your students are working hard but not learning, they might be spending their cognitive effort in the wrong place.

# Putting It Together: A Diagnostic Tool

When a lesson isn't working — when students seem confused or overwhelmed — you can now diagnose the problem more precisely:

1. **Is intrinsic load too high?** Am I asking students to process too many new, interacting elements at once? → Break it into smaller steps.
2. **Is extraneous load stealing working memory?** Are my materials, instructions, or presentation creating unnecessary mental work? → Simplify the format, integrate the sources, cut the tangents.
3. **Is germane load too low?** Are students busy but not actually thinking about the content? → Redesign the activity so the thinking is about the right things.

This three-part diagnostic is something you can apply to any lesson, any activity, any material. It's one of the most practically useful tools in all of learning science.

> [!tip]
> **Monday morning application:** Before your next lesson, look at your materials through the three-load lens. Where is the extraneous load? (Anything that makes students think about the format rather than the content.) Can you cut it? Where is the germane load? (Where are students actually thinking about the content?) Is that where most of their mental effort will go?

## Section 3: The Expertise Reversal Effect

# The Same Lesson Can Help AND Hurt

In LS 101, we briefly introduced the expertise reversal effect: what helps novices hurts experts. Now we're going to explore why this happens, what it means for your mixed-stage classrooms, and what to do about it.

Here's the core idea, and it's not just a theoretical curiosity — it's one of the most practically important findings in all of cognitive load research.

When a student is a **novice** in a topic — they have no schema, no organised prior knowledge — they need maximum support. Worked examples, step-by-step modelling, clear scaffolding. Without this support, the student's working memory is overwhelmed by the sheer novelty of everything.

When a student is **competent** in a topic — they've built schemas, they have organised knowledge in long-term memory — that same support becomes a burden. The scaffolding that helped the novice now forces the competent student to process information they already know, adding extraneous load and interfering with their own more efficient mental processes.

This isn't a minor effect. Research shows that worked examples can actually produce WORSE learning outcomes for students who already understand the material compared to just giving them problems to solve independently.

> [!warning]
> **This is critical for mixed-stage classrooms.** If you're teaching a group where some students are new to a concept and others have been working with it for months, there is literally no single approach that is optimal for everyone. The support that your novices need is actively harmful for your more advanced students. This isn't a failure of teaching — it's a fundamental feature of how learning works.

# Why Scaffolding Becomes a Problem

To understand why, think about what happens in a competent student's brain when you present a worked example they don't need.

They already have a schema for this type of problem. Their brain tries to activate and use that schema — which is efficient and effective. But the worked example forces them to also process external information (your steps, your method, your explanation) and reconcile it with their existing schema. This reconciliation is extraneous load — mental work that produces no new learning.

It's like giving detailed driving directions to someone who already knows the route. They can't ignore the directions entirely (they're being presented), so they have to process them AND navigate from memory simultaneously. The directions don't help — they interfere.

For novices, the worked example IS the navigation. Without it, they have no schema to guide them, and they're lost.

# The Fading Strategy

So what do you do? The answer is **fading** — gradually removing support as students gain competence.

The fading sequence looks like this:

1. **Full worked example:** Every step is shown and explained. For true novices. "Watch how I do this."
2. **Partially completed example:** Some steps are provided, others are left for the student to fill in. For students who are starting to build a schema. "I've done the first two steps — you do the next two."
3. **Problem with hints:** The student works independently but has access to hints if needed. For students who have a developing schema but may still need support. "Try it on your own. If you get stuck, here's a clue."
4. **Independent problem-solving:** No scaffolding. For students who have built a working schema. "Here's a new problem. Solve it."

The beauty of fading is that it matches support to the student's current level of expertise. It's not one-size-fits-all. And the transitions between stages are driven by the student's growing competence, not by a fixed schedule.

> [!info]
> **In elementary (Montessori):** Fading is already built into the method. The three-period lesson moves from "This is..." (full demonstration) to "Show me..." (guided practice with partial support) to "What is...?" (independent recall). Material progressions do the same thing — concrete to semi-abstract to abstract. The principle is the same: reduce support as schemas develop.

# Fading in Middle School

In middle school, fading looks slightly different because instruction is more teacher-led, but the principle is identical.

Consider a sequence for teaching students to write a topic sentence:

- **Day 1:** Teacher shows three examples of strong topic sentences. Points out what makes each one work. Students identify the pattern. (Full worked example.)
- **Day 2:** Teacher shows the beginning of a topic sentence and students complete it. Then students write one with a sentence starter provided. (Partially completed.)
- **Day 3:** Students write topic sentences for given paragraphs with a checklist to self-assess. (Hints available.)
- **Day 4:** Students write topic sentences for their own paragraphs. No supports. (Independent.)

The critical question at each stage is: can this student do it with less support? If yes, remove some. If no, keep the current level. Moving too fast overwhelms them. Moving too slowly bores them and adds extraneous load.

# The Diagnostic Challenge

Here's the practical problem: how do you know when a student has crossed from novice to competent? How do you know when to start fading?

The answer connects directly to what you learned about formative assessment in LS 101. **Diagnostic questions** are the key tool.

A well-designed diagnostic question doesn't just tell you whether a student can get the right answer. It tells you whether they have a schema — whether they understand the underlying structure or are just following memorised steps.

For example, after teaching column subtraction, you might ask:

"Without calculating, which of these has the largest answer? (a) 845 - 399 (b) 845 - 401 (c) 845 - 400"

A student who has built a schema for subtraction understands that subtracting a smaller number gives a larger result, so (a) is largest. A student who is following memorised steps without a schema needs to calculate each one and might get confused.

When diagnostic questions reveal that a student has the schema, you can start fading. When they reveal that the student doesn't, you keep the scaffolding in place.

> [!tip]
> **The one-size-fits-all trap:** The most common mistake in mixed-stage classrooms is giving everyone the same worksheet with the same level of scaffolding. For the students who need it, the scaffolding is helpful. For the students who don't, it's actively harmful — it adds extraneous load and prevents them from using their own schemas efficiently. This is one of the strongest arguments for differentiating materials, not just differentiating difficulty.

# A Practical Framework for Your Classroom

Here's a simple framework you can use:

1. **Identify where each student is.** Use diagnostic questions at the start of a topic.
2. **Match support to level.** Novices get worked examples. Developing students get partially completed examples. Competent students get open problems.
3. **Reassess regularly.** As students learn, they move through the fading stages. What was right yesterday may be wrong today.
4. **When in doubt, check.** If you're not sure whether to fade, give a diagnostic question. The student's response tells you.

This isn't about creating thirty different worksheets. It's often as simple as having two or three versions of the same activity at different scaffold levels, and knowing which students need which version.

- Suggest that experienced students receive a different version — either unscaffolded problems, open-ended challenges, or problems that require applying the method in new contexts
- Mention diagnostic questions as the way to determine which students need which version
- Acknowledge this isn't about creating more work — it can be as simple as having two versions of the same activity

## Section 4: Retrieval Practice — Going Deeper
**Slug:** retrieval-practice-deeper
**Estimated Minutes:** 15

### Content

> [!tip]
> **Quick retrieval:** From LS 101 — what is retrieval practice, and why is it more effective than re-reading? Think about the mechanism — what does the act of retrieval actually do to memory?

# Beyond "Quiz Them More"

In LS 101, you learned the fundamental principle: pulling information out of memory (retrieval) strengthens the memory more than putting information in again (re-studying). The act of remembering is itself a learning event.

That's the "what." Now we need the "how." Because the difference between retrieval practice that transforms learning and retrieval practice that wastes time comes down to design.

# The Testing Effect in Detail

Let's get precise about what research actually shows.

In a landmark series of studies, researchers compared two conditions. Group A studied material and then studied it again. Group B studied material and then took a test on it (with no feedback). Both groups spent the same total time. On a test one week later, Group B dramatically outperformed Group A — even though Group B spent less time studying and more time being tested.

This is the testing effect: retrieval produces better long-term retention than re-studying, even when total time is equal.

But here's where it gets even more interesting. When you add **feedback** after retrieval — telling students what they got right and correcting what they got wrong — the effect is even larger. Retrieval with feedback is the most powerful combination in the learning science toolkit.

Why does this work? Every time you try to retrieve something, you activate and strengthen the neural pathways to that memory. If you succeed, the pathway gets stronger. If you fail but then receive feedback, you get two benefits: the attempt itself (which activates related schemas) and the correction (which fixes or builds the right connection).

> [!info]
> **Retrieval WITH feedback beats retrieval alone.** Always include a checking step after retrieval practice. Students write what they remember, THEN check against their notes. Students answer a question, THEN hear the correct answer with explanation. The retrieval attempt plus the correction is the winning combination.

# Three Types of Retrieval

Not all retrieval is created equal. Understanding the types helps you design more effective practice.

**Free recall** is the most powerful and most demanding form. "Write down everything you remember about photosynthesis." No prompts, no cues, no options to choose from. The student must search their memory and produce information entirely from their own mental structures.

**Cued recall** provides a prompt or question. "What are the three stages of the water cycle?" The cue narrows the search — the student doesn't have to decide what to retrieve, but they do have to produce the answer from memory.

**Recognition** provides the answer along with distractors. "Which of these is a stage of the water cycle? (a) evaporation (b) erosion (c) oxidation." The student doesn't produce the answer — they recognise it. This is the weakest form of retrieval because the memory doesn't have to be generated, only matched.

| Retrieval type | What the student does | Learning power | Example |
|---------------|----------------------|----------------|---------|
| Free recall | Produces everything from memory | Strongest | "Write everything you know about..." |
| Cued recall | Answers a specific question from memory | Strong | "What are the three types of rock?" |
| Recognition | Picks the right answer from options | Weakest | Multiple choice questions |

For maximum learning, design retrieval activities that lean toward free recall and cued recall. Recognition (like multiple choice) has its place — especially for checking understanding quickly — but it shouldn't be your primary retrieval tool.

> [!tip]
> **Design for production, not recognition.** Whenever possible, ask students to produce answers from memory rather than recognise them from a list. "Tell me what you remember" is more powerful than "Pick the right answer." The harder the retrieval, the stronger the learning.

# Retrieval Grids: A Practical Tool

A retrieval grid is a simple, versatile tool that builds retrieval practice into your routine. Here's how it works:

Create a grid with topics across the top and sessions down the side. At the start of each session, students fill in one or two cells from memory — writing what they remember about a particular topic without looking at any materials.

The power of the grid is cumulative. Students revisit the same topics at increasing intervals. The first time they fill in a cell, it might be one day after learning the topic. The next time, it might be a week later. Then two weeks. Each retrieval gets harder (because more time has passed) and more effective (because the difficulty is what strengthens memory).

A simple retrieval grid might look like this:

| | Topic A | Topic B | Topic C | Topic D |
|---|---------|---------|---------|---------|
| Week 1 | Fill in | | | |
| Week 2 | | Fill in | Fill in | |
| Week 3 | Fill in | | | Fill in |
| Week 4 | | Fill in | | Fill in |

After students fill in a cell from memory, they check against their notes or materials. This is the feedback step — crucial for correcting errors and strengthening correct memories.

# Brain Dumps: The Simplest Retrieval Practice

If retrieval grids feel like too much structure, brain dumps are even simpler.

At the start of a session, before opening any materials, students write down everything they remember about the previous session's topic. No prompts, no cues — just "What do you remember?"

This is free recall — the most powerful form of retrieval. It takes three to five minutes and requires no preparation from you.

The checking step is crucial. After the brain dump, students open their materials and compare what they wrote with what they should know. They mark what they got right, add what they missed, and correct any errors. This self-checking is where much of the learning happens — it provides immediate feedback on the retrieval attempt.

Over time, brain dumps also build metacognitive awareness. Students start to notice patterns: "I always forget the third step" or "I remember the examples but not the definitions." This awareness helps them direct their own study more effectively.

> "The act of retrieving is the act of learning. Every time students pull information from memory — even imperfectly — they're making that memory stronger."

# When Retrieval Fails: The Encoding Problem

Here's a common mistake: a teacher starts using retrieval practice and discovers that students can't retrieve anything. They sit with blank pages during brain dumps. They can't answer cued recall questions. The teacher concludes that "retrieval practice doesn't work."

But retrieval practice has a prerequisite: **the information must have been encoded in the first place.** Retrieval strengthens existing memories — it doesn't create them from nothing.

If students can't retrieve anything, the problem isn't with retrieval practice. The problem is with initial instruction. The information never made it into long-term memory to begin with — perhaps because initial learning was too rushed, cognitive load was too high, or students weren't paying attention.

The fix: go back and teach the material again (managing cognitive load properly), and then use retrieval practice to strengthen the new encoding.

Think of it this way: retrieval practice is exercise for memories that exist. You can't exercise a muscle you don't have. Build the muscle first (initial learning), then strengthen it (retrieval practice).

> [!warning]
> **Retrieval practice is not a substitute for good initial teaching.** If students can't retrieve, check whether initial encoding happened. Did they understand the material when it was first taught? Was cognitive load managed? Were they paying attention? Fix the encoding problem first, then use retrieval to strengthen what was learned.

# The Mindspark Connection

Every problem a student solves on Mindspark is retrieval practice. They're not re-reading explanations or watching demonstrations — they're producing answers from memory.

And Mindspark does several things that make this retrieval especially effective:

- **Adaptive difficulty:** Problems are pitched at the right level — hard enough to require genuine retrieval, not so hard that the student has no schema to retrieve from
- **Immediate feedback:** Students see whether they're right or wrong after each problem, getting the retrieval + feedback combination
- **Spaced revisiting:** Topics return at intervals, building in the spacing effect
- **Varied formats:** The same concept appears in different problem types, building flexible schemas

When you understand the science behind retrieval practice, you can see why Mindspark is effective — and why "just do more Mindspark" isn't a complete answer. Mindspark builds retrieval and spacing into maths and English practice. But retrieval practice for science, social studies, language, and every other subject needs to be designed by you.

# The Science of When to Revisit

In LS 101, you learned that spacing — distributing practice over time — produces dramatically better long-term retention than massed practice. You learned the core principle: revisit material after a delay, when memory has started to fade, and the retrieval at that point of forgetting stretches the memory further.

Now let's get practical. HOW do you actually build spacing into your teaching? What does a spacing system look like in a real classroom?

# The Forgetting Curve and the Spacing Sweet Spot

The forgetting curve is one of the most replicated findings in psychology. After learning something, memory begins to fade almost immediately. Without any review, most people forget about 70% of new material within 24 hours.

But here's the key insight that makes spacing so powerful: each time you retrieve something at the point of forgetting, the forgetting curve flattens. The memory fades more slowly after each retrieval. The intervals between necessary reviews get longer and longer.

This means:

- First review: 1 day after initial learning (memory is fading fast)
- Second review: 1 week later (memory fades more slowly now)
- Third review: 2-3 weeks later (even slower fading)
- Fourth review: 1 month later (the memory is now quite durable)

Each review is shorter than the last — it's just retrieval, not re-teaching. A five-minute brain dump or a few cued recall questions is enough. You're not covering the material again. You're asking students to pull it from memory, which strengthens the neural pathways.

> [!info]
> **The spacing formula in practice:** Revisit at 1 day, 1 week, 2-3 weeks, 1 month. Each revisit is just a retrieval activity — not a full re-teach. Five minutes at the start of a session is enough. Over time, the memory becomes self-sustaining and requires less maintenance.

# Spacing Calendars: A Practical Planning Tool

The hardest part of spacing isn't understanding why it works — it's remembering to do it. When you're focused on next week's content, it's easy to forget to revisit what you taught three weeks ago.

A spacing calendar solves this. It's a simple planning tool where you map out when you'll revisit each topic across a term.

Here's a simplified example for a four-week stretch:

| Week | New content | 1-day review | 1-week review | 2-3 week review |
|------|------------|--------------|---------------|-----------------|
| Week 1 | Topic A | Topic A (next day) | | |
| Week 2 | Topic B | Topic B (next day) | Topic A | |
| Week 3 | Topic C | Topic C (next day) | Topic B | Topic A |
| Week 4 | Topic D | Topic D (next day) | Topic C | Topics A & B |

Notice how topics accumulate in the review column. By Week 4, you're reviewing Topics A, B, C, and D at different intervals. This sounds like a lot, but each review is just a few minutes of retrieval — not full lessons.

The practical rule: **dedicate the first five minutes of each session to spaced retrieval of previous content.** Rotate through topics using your spacing calendar. This is the simplest, most effective change most teachers can make.

> [!tip]
> **Start small.** You don't need a perfect spacing calendar for every topic. Start with the three most important things you've taught in the last month. Add them to a sticky note. At the start of each session, pick one and do a quick retrieval activity. That's spacing. You can systematise it later.

# The Cumulative Review Principle

Here's a pattern that many effective schools use: cumulative review. Instead of treating each unit as a self-contained block that's finished once you move on, you build review of previous units into every subsequent one.

This means that by the middle of the year, a typical session might start with three minutes of retrieval on content from earlier in the term, followed by the day's new teaching. The review isn't separate from the teaching — it's woven into the fabric of every session.

Cumulative review works because it naturally creates spacing. Topics you taught in September get retrieved in October, November, December — at increasing intervals. Each retrieval strengthens the memory. By the end of the year, core content has been retrieved so many times that it's deeply embedded in long-term memory.

This is exactly what EI ASSET rewards — students need to retain and apply knowledge from throughout the year, not just recall what they learned most recently.

# Interleaving: The Power of Mixing

Spacing is about WHEN you revisit. Interleaving is about HOW you structure practice. And like spacing, it goes against every instinct.

In LS 101, you learned the basic principle: mixing different problem types during practice (interleaving) produces better retention than practising one type at a time (blocking), even though blocking produces better performance during the practice itself.

Now let's understand WHY, so you can design interleaving more effectively.

When students do blocked practice — ten addition problems, then ten subtraction problems — they know what operation to use before they even look at the problem. The practice is about executing the procedure, not about deciding which procedure to use.

When students do interleaved practice — addition and subtraction mixed randomly — they must make an additional decision for each problem: what type is this? Which approach do I need? This **discrimination** is the extra mental work that produces superior learning.

In real life, problems don't come pre-sorted. A student solving word problems in maths has to first figure out whether it's an addition, subtraction, multiplication, or division problem. A student writing has to decide what type of sentence to use. The discrimination skill is essential — and blocked practice never develops it.

> "Interleaving forces learners to repeatedly reload different strategies, compare them, and select the right one. This extra effort is exactly what builds flexible knowledge."

# When NOT to Interleave

Interleaving is not always appropriate. There's a critical prerequisite: **students must have initial competence with each individual skill before you interleave them.**

If a student hasn't yet learned how to add fractions, mixing fraction addition with fraction subtraction will just confuse them. They need blocked practice first — enough to build a basic schema for each operation — before interleaving asks them to discriminate between them.

Think of it in two phases:

1. **Blocked practice first:** Students learn each skill or concept individually. Enough repetition to build a basic schema. ("Here are ten addition problems. Get comfortable with the method.")
2. **Interleaved practice after:** Once students can execute each skill individually, mix them together. ("Here are ten problems — some addition, some subtraction. Figure out which is which.")

This sequencing is important. Interleaving before students have basic schemas is an undesirable difficulty — it creates confusion without learning. Interleaving after basic schemas are built is a desirable difficulty — it creates the discrimination that produces durable, flexible knowledge.

> [!warning]
> **Don't interleave too early.** If students are still building their initial schema for a skill, they need blocked practice. Interleaving works by forcing discrimination between developed skills — not by confusing novices. Make sure students can do each thing individually before you ask them to sort between things.

# Mindspark and Interleaving

Mindspark naturally interleaves across topics within a subject, but it also offers the option of focused practice on specific areas. Understanding the science behind interleaving helps you make better decisions about when to use each mode:

- When students are learning a brand-new concept on Mindspark, focused (blocked) practice on that concept is appropriate.
- When students have basic competence in multiple concepts, mixed assignments across topics build the discrimination skills that make knowledge flexible and durable.

You can set up Mindspark assignments strategically — using focused practice for new skills and mixed practice for consolidation.

# Why Some Things Must Become Automatic

Think about what happens when you read this sentence. You don't sound out each letter. You don't consciously identify each word. You don't think about grammar rules. Reading has become automatic — your brain does it without conscious effort, leaving your working memory free to focus on what the sentence means.

Now think about a student who is still sounding out words. Every word consumes working memory. By the time they finish a sentence, they've forgotten the beginning. They can decode the words, but they can't understand the paragraph — because their working memory is completely consumed by the mechanics of reading.

This is why **automaticity** matters. Some foundational skills must become so fast and effortless that they require zero working memory. When they're automatic, working memory is free for higher-order thinking. When they're not automatic, working memory is trapped in the basics.

The skills that need to become automatic are the ones that are used as building blocks for more complex tasks:

- **Reading:** Sight word recognition, phonics patterns, fluent decoding
- **Writing:** Letter formation, spelling of common words, basic sentence structure
- **Maths:** Number bonds, multiplication facts, basic operations, counting patterns
- **General:** Following classroom routines, using reference materials, basic procedures

> [!info]
> **Automaticity is not about being "robotic."** It's about freeing up mental resources. A musician who has to think about where each finger goes can't think about musical expression. A student who has to think about how to form each letter can't think about what to write. Automaticity in the basics enables creativity and depth in everything else.

# The Automaticity Threshold

There's a threshold — a tipping point — where a skill shifts from consuming working memory to being essentially free.

Below the threshold, the skill requires conscious attention. The student can do it, but it takes effort and time. Each use of the skill draws from the working memory budget, leaving less capacity for other tasks.

Above the threshold, the skill is automatic. It happens without conscious effort, quickly and reliably. It draws nothing from the working memory budget. The student can perform the skill AND think about something else simultaneously.

Consider a student doing long division. If basic multiplication facts aren't automatic — if the student has to think about what 7 x 8 is — then each step of the long division problem includes a sub-problem that consumes working memory. The student is solving multiplication AND division simultaneously, and their working memory is overwhelmed.

If multiplication facts ARE automatic — the student knows instantly that 7 x 8 = 56 — then the multiplication takes no working memory at all. All cognitive resources are available for the actual division problem.

The difference isn't just convenience. It's the difference between being able to learn the new skill and not being able to.

# Deliberate Practice vs Mere Repetition

Here's a critical distinction: just doing something repeatedly doesn't build automaticity. A student who practises multiplication facts by writing them out 50 times may still not have automatic recall. A student who reads the same passage aloud ten times may not become a fluent reader.

**Mere repetition** is doing the same thing the same way over and over. It can feel like practice, but it doesn't reliably produce automaticity.

**Deliberate practice** has specific features that make it effective:

1. **It's focused on a specific sub-skill.** Not "practise maths" but "practise the 7-times table."
2. **It provides immediate feedback.** The student knows right away whether they were correct.
3. **It pushes at the edge of current ability.** Not too easy (no learning), not too hard (overwhelming). Just hard enough.
4. **It's sustained over time.** Short, frequent sessions beat long, infrequent ones.

This is exactly what well-designed flashcard practice looks like, what Mindspark's adaptive engine provides, and what the Montessori material repetitions build toward — focused, feedback-rich, appropriately challenging practice sustained over time.

> [!tip]
> **"Drill" isn't a dirty word — IF it's well-designed.** Mindless repetition without feedback or adjustment is indeed a waste of time. But focused, feedback-rich practice at the right difficulty level is one of the most powerful tools in education. The research is clear: there is no shortcut to automaticity. It requires practice.

# How Much Practice? More Than You Think

Research on expertise suggests that fluency requires **overlearning** — practising beyond the point of initial mastery. When a student gets their multiplication facts right for the first time, they're not fluent. They can produce the answer, but with effort and delay.

True automaticity — instant, effortless recall — requires continuing practice past the point where the student "knows" the answer. It's the continued practice that shifts the skill from working-memory-dependent to automatic.

How much practice depends on the skill, but a rough guide:

- If a student can produce the answer correctly but slowly (2-3 seconds of thinking): they know it but it's not automatic. Continue practising.
- If a student can produce the answer correctly and quickly (under 1 second, without hesitation): it's approaching automaticity. Continue practising to maintain it.
- If a student produces the answer instantly and reliably over weeks: it's automatic. Maintain with occasional review.

The practical implication: don't stop number facts practice the moment students can answer correctly. Correct is the beginning, not the end. Automatic and instant is the goal.

# The Fluency-Understanding Tension

You may hear (or feel) a tension in your school: "Should students understand it or just be fluent? We want understanding, not rote memorisation."

This is a false choice. Research shows that fluency and understanding are not opposites — they're partners.

**Fluency without understanding** is brittle. A student who has memorised that 7 x 8 = 56 without understanding what multiplication means can produce the answer but can't apply it to new situations, can't use it to check other answers, and can't connect it to division or repeated addition. If they forget the fact, they have no way to reconstruct it.

**Understanding without fluency** leaves working memory overloaded. A student who understands what multiplication means but has to count by sevens to figure out 7 x 8 can't use that knowledge efficiently in more complex problems. Their working memory is consumed by basic computation, leaving nothing for higher-order thinking.

The research-supported approach: **build understanding first, then build fluency on top of it.** Teach what multiplication means (using concrete materials, real-world contexts, arrays and groups). Then, once students understand, build speed and automaticity through focused practice.

Understanding without fluency is a bridge half-built. Fluency without understanding is a bridge without foundations. You need both.

> "Memorising without understanding is fragile. Understanding without fluency is slow. The combination — understanding made automatic through practice — is what expertise looks like."

# Desirable Difficulties

In LS 101, you learned the core distinction: performance is what a student can do right now; learning is what they can do later, independently. Now we're going to explore the mechanism behind this — and it has a name that captures its paradoxical nature: **desirable difficulties.**

The term was coined by Robert Bjork, one of the most influential memory researchers. A desirable difficulty is any condition that makes learning harder in the moment but produces stronger long-term retention and transfer.

You've already met several desirable difficulties in this course:

- **Spacing** (Section 5): Spreading practice over time feels worse than massing it together — you forget more between sessions, performance is lower during practice — but retention is dramatically better.
- **Interleaving** (Section 5): Mixing problem types feels chaotic — students make more errors, work more slowly — but discrimination and long-term retention are superior.
- **Retrieval** (Section 4): Pulling from memory is harder than re-reading — it feels effortful and uncertain — but it strengthens memory far more than re-exposure.

Here are two more:

- **Generation:** Producing an answer before being told it (even if you get it wrong) produces better learning than being given the answer first. Making a guess activates relevant schemas and prepares the brain to receive the correct information.
- **Variation:** Practising the same skill in different contexts (different examples, different formats, different settings) produces better transfer than practising in a single context. The variability feels confusing but forces the learner to extract the underlying principle rather than memorising one version.

> [!info]
> **The paradox of desirable difficulties:** The conditions that produce the best learning feel the worst during practice, and the conditions that feel the best produce the weakest learning. This is why student satisfaction and teacher intuition can both be misleading indicators of learning.

# Undesirable Difficulties

Not all difficulty is desirable. This is a crucial distinction, because the concept of desirable difficulties can be misused to justify any kind of struggle: "Well, they're struggling, but struggle is good for learning!"

No. Difficulty is only desirable when it forces the learner to engage more deeply with the **content**. Difficulty that forces the learner to engage with the **presentation** or the **format** is not desirable — it's extraneous load.

Here's the test:

**Desirable difficulty:** The difficulty makes the student think harder about the CONTENT.
- Retrieving from memory (thinking about the content)
- Spacing practice (re-engaging with the content after forgetting)
- Interleaving (discriminating between different types of content)
- Generating answers (constructing knowledge from content)

**Undesirable difficulty:** The difficulty makes the student think harder about the PRESENTATION.
- Illegible handwriting on worksheets (struggling to read, not to learn)
- Confusing instructions (struggling to understand what to do, not to understand the content)
- Unnecessary complexity in the format (navigating the task, not engaging with the ideas)
- Background noise or distractions (competing for attention, not deepening thought)

A simple question to ask: "Is this difficulty making my students think harder about the right things?" If yes, it's desirable. If no, eliminate it.

> [!warning]
> **Don't justify all struggle as "desirable."** A student who is struggling because the worksheet is confusing, the instructions are unclear, or the environment is distracting is NOT experiencing desirable difficulty. They're wasting cognitive effort on the wrong things. Desirable difficulty means difficulty in engaging with the CONTENT — not difficulty in navigating the presentation.

# The Short-Term Performance Trap

Here's why this matters so much for how we evaluate teaching and learning.

Desirable difficulties reduce short-term performance. Students who space their practice perform worse during practice than students who mass it. Students who interleave perform worse during practice than students who block. Students who retrieve perform worse than students who re-read (because retrieval is harder and produces more errors).

If you judge by short-term performance — by how students look during the lesson, by how smoothly the session goes, by how many problems students get right during practice — you will systematically prefer the methods that produce LESS learning.

This has profound implications:

| What you see in the moment | What it might mean for learning |
|---------------------------|-------------------------------|
| Students getting everything right | Could mean strong learning, OR could mean the practice is too easy and is building fluency without challenge |
| Students making many errors | Could mean the task is too hard, OR could mean they're in the middle of desirable difficulty that's building durable knowledge |
| A smooth, well-flowing lesson | Could mean excellent teaching, OR could mean cognitive load is low and students aren't being challenged enough |
| A messy, stop-and-start lesson | Could mean poor planning, OR could mean the teacher is responding to real-time evidence and adjusting |

The point isn't that messy is always better than smooth. It's that you can't tell from the surface. You need to look at what students can do LATER — after a delay, without support — to judge whether learning happened.

# What This Means for How We Judge Lessons

This has important implications for how teachers evaluate each other's lessons — and how they evaluate their own.

If you observe a colleague's lesson and see students working smoothly, getting answers right, and appearing confident, your instinct says: "Good lesson." And it might be. But it might also be a lesson where cognitive load was so low that students were performing without learning.

If you observe a lesson where students are struggling, making errors, and looking uncertain, your instinct says: "This needs improvement." And it might. But it might also be a lesson where desirable difficulties are building the kind of durable, flexible knowledge that will outperform the smooth lesson a month from now.

The better question — the learning science question — is not "How does this look right now?" but "What will students be able to do with this material a week from now? A month from now?"

This doesn't mean we should judge lessons by test scores. It means we should look for evidence of desirable difficulties being used intentionally: retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, generation, variation. And we should be suspicious of lessons that look too easy — because ease in the moment often means weakness in the long term.

> [!tip]
> **The two-week test:** When evaluating a lesson or a teaching strategy, ask yourself: "If I tested students on this material two weeks later with no warning and no preparation, how much would they remember?" If the answer is "a lot," the strategy produced learning. If the answer is "not much," the strategy produced performance. This thought experiment helps cut through the illusion of smooth lessons.

# Implications for Your Own Teaching

This section isn't about making your lessons deliberately confusing or poorly planned. It's about being willing to build in productive struggle — and being able to tell the difference between productive and unproductive struggle.

Practically, this means:

1. **Don't shy away from retrieval practice** because students find it hard or uncomfortable. The discomfort is the learning happening.
2. **Don't abandon interleaving** because students make more errors with mixed practice. The errors are evidence that discrimination is being developed.
3. **Don't interpret smooth performance as proof of learning.** Check later. If students can still do it after a delay, the learning was real.
4. **DO eliminate undesirable difficulties** — confusing instructions, cluttered materials, split attention. These produce struggle without learning.

# Two Phases of Every Lesson

Everything you've learned in this course — and in LS 101 — comes together in a simple, powerful model for lesson design. Every effective lesson has two phases:

**Phase 1: Help students understand.** Manage cognitive load. Build new schemas. This is where intrinsic load must be carefully sequenced, extraneous load must be eliminated, and the teacher provides maximum support for new learning.

**Phase 2: Make it stick.** Create durable memories. This is where desirable difficulties come in — retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, generation, variation. The teacher gradually removes support and increases productive challenge.

These two phases aren't always separate sessions. They can happen within a single lesson. But they serve fundamentally different purposes, and confusing them is one of the most common mistakes in teaching.

Phase 1 is about comprehension. Phase 2 is about retention and transfer.

Phase 1 should feel supported and clear. Phase 2 should feel effortful and challenging.

Phase 1 reduces difficulty (so students can understand). Phase 2 increases difficulty (so students can remember).

> [!info]
> **Why both phases matter:** A lesson that's all Phase 1 (clear explanation, guided practice, everything makes sense) produces understanding in the moment but fragile memories. A lesson that's all Phase 2 (retrieval, challenge, struggle) without adequate initial instruction produces confusion and frustration. Effective teaching is knowing when to support and when to challenge.

# Phase 1: Understanding (Rosenshine's Principles)

Phase 1 maps directly to what you learned about Rosenshine's Principles in LS 101. This is the "I do, we do, you do" arc:

1. **Begin with a review** of previous learning (this is actually also Phase 2 for the previous topic — retrieval practice)
2. **Present new material in small steps** (manage intrinsic load)
3. **Provide models and worked examples** (reduce cognitive load for novices)
4. **Guide practice** with high success rate (build the initial schema)
5. **Check for understanding** at each step (formative assessment — did the schema form?)
6. **Provide scaffolds** for difficult tasks (support that will be faded as competence grows)

Phase 1 is where the teacher does the most active work. You're managing cognitive load, sequencing carefully, checking understanding, and adjusting in real time.

The goal of Phase 1 is to get the new information into long-term memory — to build the initial schema. Without this, Phase 2 has nothing to work with (remember from Section 4: retrieval practice strengthens existing memories; it doesn't create them).

# Phase 2: Durability (Making It Stick)

Phase 2 is where most teaching goes wrong — not because teachers don't know about it, but because Phase 1 takes so long that Phase 2 gets squeezed out. Understanding happened (Phase 1 complete), but durability never got built (Phase 2 skipped).

Phase 2 includes:

1. **Independent practice** with reduced scaffolding (fading, from Section 3)
2. **Retrieval practice** — recalling what was learned without looking at materials (Section 4)
3. **Spaced review** — revisiting after a delay (Section 5)
4. **Interleaved practice** — mixing this topic with previous topics (Section 5)
5. **Building automaticity** — for foundational skills that need to become effortless (Section 6)

Phase 2 is where desirable difficulties live (Section 7). It should feel harder than Phase 1 — because that productive difficulty is what builds durable, transferable knowledge.

The goal of Phase 2 is to move knowledge from fragile initial understanding to robust, flexible, durable memory.

> "Phase 1 gets the knowledge in. Phase 2 keeps it there. Most teachers are excellent at Phase 1 and skip Phase 2 — which is why students understand today and forget by next month."

# How This Maps to Elementary (Montessori)

In elementary, the Montessori lesson cycle already contains both phases — though the language is different.

**Phase 1: The presentation (lesson)**
The three-period lesson IS Phase 1. The teacher introduces new material — names it, demonstrates it, shows the child how to use it. This is Rosenshine in action: small steps, modelling, guided practice, checking understanding.

The presentation is typically short and focused — just enough for the child to understand the concept and begin working. This is excellent cognitive load management: intrinsic load is kept low, extraneous load is minimal, and the child's working memory isn't overwhelmed.

**Phase 2: The work cycle**
After the presentation, the child works independently — often returning to the same material repeatedly over days or weeks. This is Phase 2: independent practice, retrieval (using the material from memory), spacing (returning over time), and building automaticity through repetition.

The Montessori system naturally builds in Phase 2 because children have extended work cycles and freedom to return to materials. But there's a gap: the system relies on the child to naturally space and retrieve, which doesn't always happen. Some children move on too quickly. Others stay with familiar materials too long.

**Where to strengthen Phase 2 in elementary:**
- Add brief retrieval activities at the start of group gatherings (brain dumps, quick questions from previous lessons)
- Track which concepts need revisiting and actively guide children to return to materials they haven't used in a while
- Use retrieval grids or simple checklists to ensure spacing happens systematically, not randomly

# How This Maps to Middle School

In middle school, the teacher-led small-group format maps more directly to the two-phase model.

**Phase 1: The lesson**
The teacher explains, models, and guides practice. In a typical session, this might be 15-20 minutes: explain the concept, show worked examples, guide students through practice problems, check understanding with diagnostic questions.

This is where Rosenshine's Principles are most directly applicable: small steps, high success rate during guided practice, scaffolding for difficult tasks, checking understanding at each point before moving on.

**Phase 2: Independent practice and review**
After guided practice, students work independently. This is where Phase 2 begins — but it's also where it often ends. Students do the day's practice, the teacher moves to the next topic, and the material from today is never revisited until it appears on the EI ASSET months later.

**Where to strengthen Phase 2 in middle school:**
- Dedicate the first 5 minutes of every session to spaced retrieval of previous content (use your spacing calendar from Section 5)
- Design homework and independent practice to include interleaved problems (not just today's topic)
- Build retrieval routines (brain dumps, retrieval grids) into the weekly schedule
- When assigning Mindspark, use mixed-topic assignments for material students have already learned individually

> [!tip]
> **The five-minute fix:** Most teachers don't need to redesign their lessons from scratch. The single most effective change you can make is to spend the first five minutes of every session on Phase 2 activities for previous content: retrieval from memory, spaced review, mixed-topic questions. Five minutes. Every session. The cumulative effect over a term is enormous.

# A Lesson Design Template

Here's a simple template you can use to plan any lesson through the two-phase lens:

**Before the lesson:**
- [ ] What previous content will I retrieve at the start? (Phase 2 for earlier material)
- [ ] What new content am I introducing? (Phase 1 focus)
- [ ] What is the minimum students need to understand today? (Manage intrinsic load)
- [ ] What extraneous load can I eliminate from my materials? (Cut split attention, redundancy)

**Phase 1 — Understanding:**
- [ ] Present in small steps (one idea at a time)
- [ ] Model/demonstrate with worked examples (for novices) or open problems (for competent students)
- [ ] Guide practice — students try it with support
- [ ] Check understanding — diagnostic question before moving on

**Phase 2 — Durability:**
- [ ] Independent practice with reduced scaffolding
- [ ] Include at least one retrieval opportunity without materials
- [ ] Plan when this content will be retrieved again (add to spacing calendar)
- [ ] Include in interleaved practice once initial schema is built

This isn't a rigid prescription. It's a thinking tool — a way to make sure both phases are present in your teaching, not just Phase 1.

# Taking Stock

This final section is different. We're not introducing new theory. Instead, we're going to use everything you've learned — across both LS 101 and this course — to take an honest look at your own practice.

This isn't an evaluation. There's no score. It's a reflection exercise designed to help you identify what you're already doing well and where you have the biggest opportunity to improve.

Let's walk through the key ideas from both courses and ask: where does your current practice align, and where are the gaps?

# What You're Probably Already Doing Well

Before we look at gaps, let's recognise what most teachers in our schools are already doing right. If you're reading this, chances are:

**You're already managing cognitive load** — even if you didn't use that language before. When you break a concept into steps, when you demonstrate before asking students to try, when you simplify your materials — that's cognitive load management. The three-period lesson in elementary and the "explain-practise-apply" arc in middle school both reflect this.

**You're already building schemas** — every time you connect a new idea to something students already know, every time you use concrete materials before abstract notation, every time you give multiple examples of the same concept. Schema-building is at the heart of good teaching, and most teachers do it instinctively.

**You're probably already using some formative assessment** — checking whether students understand before moving on, noticing when someone is confused, adjusting your teaching based on what you see. You may not call it formative assessment, but the instinct to check is there.

**You're already committed to student understanding** — not just coverage. The fact that you're working through this course says something about your commitment to getting better at helping students learn.

> [!tip]
> **Start with what's working.** It's easy to read a course like this and feel overwhelmed by everything you "should" be doing. Resist that. You're already doing many things well. The goal isn't to overhaul your teaching — it's to make strategic additions to what's already working.

# Where the Gaps Usually Are

Based on what learning science tells us, most teachers — even good ones — have predictable gaps. See if any of these resonate:

**Gap 1: Phase 2 is weak or missing.** This is the most common gap by far. Teachers teach well (Phase 1) but don't systematically build in retrieval practice, spacing, or interleaving (Phase 2). Content is taught, "covered," and moved on from. Students understand in the moment but forget within weeks. If you recognise this, you're in good company — and the fix is straightforward.

**Gap 2: Same scaffolding for everyone.** The expertise reversal effect (Section 3) means that what helps your newer students hurts your more advanced ones. If you're giving everyone the same worksheet, the same level of support, the same worked examples — your novices may be well-served but your advanced students are being held back. The fix: two or three versions of materials at different scaffold levels.

**Gap 3: Fluency practice isn't systematic.** Many teachers understand the importance of number facts, sight words, and other foundational skills but don't have a systematic, daily routine for building automaticity. Practice happens when there's time, not as a non-negotiable daily activity. If your students still count on fingers for basic facts, this gap is worth closing.

**Gap 4: Retrieval practice isn't happening.** The simplest, most evidence-based change any teacher can make is to start every session with a retrieval activity. Yet many sessions begin with "open your books to page..." or "today we're going to learn about..." without any attempt to retrieve previous content. If this describes your typical session start, this is a high-impact gap to close.

> [!info]
> **These gaps aren't criticisms.** They're predictable patterns that emerge from how teaching has traditionally been designed — and they're fixable. Every gap above has a simple, practical solution that takes minutes per day, not a complete restructure.

# The One-Change Principle

Here's the most important practical advice in this entire course: **don't try to change everything at once.**

The knowing-doing gap from LS 101 applies to you as much as to your students. You now KNOW about schemas, cognitive load, retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, automaticity, desirable difficulties, and two-phase lesson design. But knowing doesn't mean doing.

If you try to implement everything at once, you'll be overwhelmed — your own working memory will be overloaded with too many new teaching behaviours — and within two weeks, you'll likely revert to your default practice. Not because you're lazy or uncommitted, but because that's how habit change works.

Instead: **pick one thing.** Just one. The one change that feels most relevant to your classroom right now. Try it for two weeks. Refine it. Make it part of your routine. When it feels natural — when it's become part of your schema for teaching — then add another.

Here are common "one changes" that teachers find high-impact:

| If your biggest gap is... | Your one change could be... |
|--------------------------|---------------------------|
| Phase 2 is missing | Start every session with 5 minutes of retrieval from previous content |
| No spacing system | Create a simple spacing calendar for your three most important recent topics |
| Same scaffolding for all | Create two versions of your next worksheet — one scaffolded, one open |
| Fluency practice is inconsistent | Add 3 minutes of number facts (or sight words) practice to the start of every session |
| Checking understanding is informal | Write one diagnostic question for your next lesson and use it before moving on |

# Peer Accountability

Research on habit change shows that public commitment and accountability dramatically increase the likelihood of follow-through. Telling someone what you plan to change — and agreeing to check in — is one of the most effective supports for behaviour change.

So here's the challenge: **who will you tell about your one change?** A colleague. A teaching partner. Your team lead. Your coordinator. Someone who will ask you in two weeks: "How's it going with that thing you said you'd try?"

And set a date. Not "sometime." A specific date when you'll check in. Two weeks from today is ideal — long enough to have given it a fair try, short enough that you haven't forgotten.

The check-in doesn't need to be formal. A five-minute conversation: "What did I try? What worked? What do I need to adjust?" That's it.

- B) She'll successfully implement all changes because she's motivated
- C) She'll implement the easiest changes and skip the harder ones
- D) The changes will be effective immediately because she understands the science — with responsibility placed mainly on the student rather than the environment
**Explanation:** The knowing-doing gap applies to teachers just as much as to students. Trying to change multiple teaching habits simultaneously overloads working memory (yes, yours too!) and makes it nearly impossible to sustain any of the changes. #### Q1: Applying cognitive load to a confusing lesson
A teacher presents a science concept using a video on one screen while students follow along with a different text summary on paper. Students seem lost and retain very little. The primary problem is:
- A) The video was too long
- B) Split attention ✓
- C) Students need better note-taking skills
- D) The text summary was at the wrong reading level
**Explanation:** Split attention occurs when learners must mentally integrate two or more sources of information presented separately. The video says one thing while the text says another (or the same thing differently), and students must reconcile both — consuming working memory for navigation rather than for understanding the science concept. The fix: use one integrated source or align both sources so they support rather than compete with each other.

- A) Benefit from the reinforcement of seeing the demonstration again

- C) Learn new aspects of the material they hadn't discovered on their own

- D) Students are studying more at home because they want to perform well
**Explanation:** This is the testing effect in action. The act of pulling information from memory — even if imperfect — strengthens the memory itself. It's not just that students are more attentive (though that might be a side benefit). The retrieval event is directly strengthening the neural pathways. #### Q4: The fluency-understanding relationship

**Explanation:** Fluency without understanding is brittle. The student can produce facts quickly but can't apply them flexibly because there's no underlying schema connecting multiplication to its meaning. #### Q5: Desirable vs undesirable difficulty
A teacher tries to create "productive struggle" by giving students a worksheet with tiny, hard-to-read font and complex formatting. Students spend most of their time trying to read the worksheet rather than thinking about the maths. This is:
- A) Desirable difficulty — the struggle will make learning stronger
- B) Undesirable difficulty ✓
- C) A good strategy for building resilience
- D) Neutral — font size doesn't affect learning
**Explanation:** The test for desirable difficulty: does the struggle make students think harder about the CONTENT? In this case, students are struggling with the PRESENTATION (tiny font, complex formatting), not with the maths. That's extraneous load, not germane load. Desirable difficulties (spacing, interleaving, retrieval, generation) make students think harder about the content itself — not harder about the format.

#### Q6: The spacing calendar in practice
A teacher teaches a grammar concept in Week 1 and plans to revisit it. Based on the spacing formula, the most effective review schedule would be:
- A) Review once in Week 2 and then never again
- B) Review every day for the next month to maximise repetition
- C) Wait until the end-of-term review to revisit it all at once
- D) Review in Week 2, Week 3, and Week 5 ✓
**Explanation:** The spacing formula suggests expanding intervals: approximately 1 day, 1 week, 2-3 weeks, 1 month. This means reviewing in Week 2 (one week later), Week 3 (two weeks), and around Week 5 (one month). Each review is brief — just retrieval practice, not re-teaching.
