# Narrative for "Collaborative Learning and Peer Thinking"

## Core Thesis

Collaborative learning works when group work is designed for learning, not just interaction.

This deck is useful because it is balanced. Wiliam shows that collaboration can help learning through peer explanation, distributed cognition, motivation, and feedback, but it can also fail through social loafing, unequal participation, coordination costs, and weak task design.

## The Story in One Paragraph

The deck begins by distinguishing cooperative and collaborative learning, especially who sets the goal. It then connects peer learning to formative assessment: students can become resources for one another. Wiliam reviews evidence and mechanisms, including cross-age tutoring, cognitive load, distributed cognition, retrieval, and group accountability. The practical section focuses on structures that make peer work productive: group goals, individual accountability, pair routines, peer assessment, best composite responses, and metacognitive group work.

## The Four Acts of the Deck

### Act 1: Collaboration Needs a Clear Purpose

Relevant slides: 1-8

Key message:

> Do not use group work just because students are sitting together.

Narration:

"Wiliam asks why we are using collaboration. Is the goal to produce the best solution, improve collaboration skills, maximize learning for the group, or maximize learning for each member? Different goals require different designs."

Teacher question:

> "What can this group do better than individuals, and what should every individual learn?"

### Act 2: Peer Learning Is Part of Formative Assessment

Relevant slides: 9-17

Key message:

> Students can be learning resources for one another.

Narration:

"The formative assessment framework places peers in the learning system. Peer work is not mainly about saving teacher time. It gives students access to explanations, comparisons, errors, feedback, and models of quality."

Equity link:

> Structured peer learning can help more students access feedback and thinking opportunities.

### Act 3: Collaboration Can Help or Harm Learning

Relevant slides: 18-40

Key message:

> The benefits of collaboration must outweigh the transaction costs.

Narration:

"The deck is honest about failure modes. Groups can create disguised inactivity, unequal loading, social loafing, fear of evaluation, and memory coordination costs. Collaboration helps most when tasks are complex enough to benefit from shared reasoning and when roles, goals, and accountability are clear."

Practical criteria for using collaboration:

- task is conceptually rich;
- students have relevant knowledge to contribute;
- group interaction is necessary;
- individual accountability is visible;
- roles reduce coordination costs;
- product requires reasoning, not just dividing labor.

### Act 4: Start With Pairs, Then Move to Groups

Relevant slides: 41-57

Key message:

> Peer learning needs routines.

Narration:

"The practical section gives routines teachers can use immediately: ladder of feedback, pre-flight checklists, two stars and a wish, think-pair-share, peer improvement of homework, choose-swap-choose, group questions, best composite response, and students leading parts of lessons."

Good progression:

1. Anonymous peer work.
2. Pair comparison.
3. Structured feedback.
4. Group reasoning.
5. Student-led review.

## Suggested Opening Script

"Collaboration is not automatically good. Students can sit in groups and learn less. The question is: when does interaction create thinking that would not happen otherwise?"

## Suggested Closing Script

"Use collaboration when it helps students think, explain, retrieve, compare, and improve. Design it with group goals and individual accountability. Otherwise, individual practice may be better."

## Workshop Activity

Teachers audit one group task:

1. What is the individual learning goal?
2. Why is collaboration useful here?
3. What is the group goal?
4. How is individual accountability visible?
5. What role or protocol reduces coordination costs?
6. How will the teacher know what each student learned?

The final message:

> Group work is not the strategy. Structured peer thinking is the strategy.
