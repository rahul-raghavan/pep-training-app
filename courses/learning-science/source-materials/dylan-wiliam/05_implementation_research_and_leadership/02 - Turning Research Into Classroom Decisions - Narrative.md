# Narrative for "Turning Research Into Classroom Decisions"

## Core Thesis

Evidence-informed practice means translating research into local, costed, implementable decisions.

This deck is more applied than the general research-informed teaching deck. It gives leaders a practical filter for deciding whether a research-backed idea should become school practice.

## The Story in One Paragraph

The deck starts with change theory: understand the current system and its trade-offs before changing it. It then explains why teaching is a wicked learning environment: performance is not learning, observation signals are noisy, short-term results can mislead, and practices may not transfer across settings. Wiliam then turns to the role of research and the five adoption questions. The second half is a detailed warning about meta-analysis, effect sizes, RCTs, implementation, and generalizability. The closing message is that educators need to be critical consumers of research.

## The Five Acts of the Deck

### Act 1: Start With the Existing System

Relevant slides: 3-5

Key message:

> Before changing a practice, understand what it currently does.

Narration:

"Chesterton's fence is the opening discipline. Leaders often see the weaknesses of current practice more clearly than its hidden functions. Removing or replacing a practice without understanding those functions creates unintended consequences."

Decision prompt:

> What will be worse if we make this change?

### Act 2: Teaching Is a Wicked Learning Environment

Relevant slides: 7-25

Key message:

> Short-term classroom signals can mislead.

Narration:

"Wiliam builds the case that teaching is hard to learn from informally. Performance is not learning. Observation ratings are biased by student prior achievement. Short-term success can produce weaker long-term learning. Interventions can work in one setting and fail in another."

Use this to explain why teacher learning needs:

- evidence;
- theory;
- delayed measures;
- peer discussion;
- cycles of testing and refinement.

### Act 3: Use Research as a Decision Aid

Relevant slides: 26-30

Key message:

> Research can guide focus, but local judgement decides action.

Narration:

"Research can tell us where not to waste energy, what moderators matter, how large effects might be, and what costs to expect. But the adoption decision has to be local."

The five questions:

1. Does it solve a problem we have?
2. How much faster will students learn?
3. What will it cost?
4. Can we implement it here?
5. Do we know what to do?

### Act 4: Treat Effect Sizes With Caution

Relevant slides: 31-56

Key message:

> A big average effect does not automatically mean a good local bet.

Narration:

"The deck explains why meta-analysis can mislead: inappropriate comparisons, publication bias, implementation variation, outcome sensitivity, age differences, and population variability. This does not mean ignore meta-analysis. It means interpret it carefully."

Teacher/leader rule:

> An effect size is not an implementation plan.

### Act 5: Implementation Is Part of the Evidence

Relevant slides: 57-64

Key message:

> "What worked" is not the same as "what will work here."

Narration:

"The Tennessee STAR and RCT slides show that strong evidence still requires careful implementation and generalization. The final slides ask for adequate theory, standardized interventions, and intention-to-treat analysis. For schools, the practical equivalent is: be clear about the mechanism, define the practice, and evaluate everyone exposed to the initiative, not just successful adopters."

## Suggested Opening Script

"This session is about moving from research says to should we do this here. Those are not the same question. A practice can be evidence-supported and still be the wrong priority for our school right now."

## Suggested Closing Script

"Evidence-informed schools are not those with the longest bibliography. They are schools that make disciplined bets: clear problem, plausible mechanism, manageable cost, realistic implementation, and evidence of impact."

## Workshop Activity

Use this as an initiative review protocol. For each proposed initiative, leaders fill in:

- problem statement;
- evidence source;
- expected learning gain;
- opportunity cost;
- implementation conditions;
- mechanism of action;
- first leading indicator;
- stopping or scaling rule.

The final message:

> Evidence matters most when it disciplines our choices.
