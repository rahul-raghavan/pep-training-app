# 1. Teacher Expertise Is the Improvement Lever

_Section: Foundations and Motivation_

## Core Thesis

This deck is not really about ranking teachers.

It is about why a serious school improvement strategy cannot depend on finding, ranking, rewarding, or replacing "good" and "bad" teachers. Teacher quality matters enormously, but the usual ways of identifying teacher quality are too noisy, too biased, or too slow to be the main improvement strategy.

Therefore, the only scalable strategy is to invest in the teachers we already have.

Use this deck to make the case for sustained professional learning.

## Better Title

If you present this to teachers, I would retitle it:

**Why Teacher Learning Is the Improvement Strategy**

The current file title, `Teacher Expertise Is the Improvement Lever`, is accurate, but the narrative should emphasize teacher learning rather than teacher judgement.

## The Story in One Paragraph

Schools can improve curriculum, class size, resources, planning time, and systems, but the quality of day-to-day teaching remains one of the biggest in-school factors affecting student learning. The problem is that teacher quality is real but hard to measure precisely. Qualifications do not tell us enough. Brief observations are unreliable and biased. Test-score measures are unstable and model-dependent. Even combined systems require years of data to become reliable. And when systems try to improve by removing low-performing teachers, the average system-wide gains are small. So the practical conclusion is clear: if we want better student learning at scale, we must build better teacher learning at scale.

## The Emotional Framing

Be careful with this deck. If presented badly, it can sound like:

> "Teachers are the problem."

That is not the right message.

The better message is:

> "Teaching is complex, teacher expertise matters, and the system owes teachers better conditions for getting better."

This is a pro-teacher deck. It argues against simplistic accountability and for serious investment in professional expertise.

## The Four Acts of the Deck

### Act 1: Teacher Quality Matters

Relevant slides: 1-5

The first act establishes that teaching quality is not the only thing that matters, but it matters a great deal.

Key message:

> Students' learning opportunities are strongly shaped by the quality of teaching they receive.

How to narrate it:

"We should not pretend that teachers control everything. Curriculum, class size, time, resources, leadership, family background, and peer effects all matter. But once students are in classrooms, the skill of the teacher is a major driver of what happens next. The point is not to blame teachers. The point is to recognize that teacher expertise is one of the few improvement levers schools can actually develop."

Use slide 5 as the most memorable evidence:

- Among 50 teachers, the difference between the most effective and least effective teacher can be very large.
- The equity point is especially important: strong teaching can help disadvantaged students learn at rates comparable to advantaged students.

Interpretation:

"If teacher expertise has this much impact, then professional development cannot be treated as an occasional event. It has to be part of the school improvement model."

### Act 2: But We Cannot Simply Identify the Good Teachers

Relevant slides: 6-27

This is the longest act. It can feel like disconnected data, but the narrative is simple:

> We keep hoping there is an easy way to identify teacher quality. There is not.

The deck tests several possible answers.

#### Attempt 1: Use qualifications

Relevant slides: 7-8

Message:

> Qualifications tell us something, but not enough.

Narration:

"It would be convenient if we could identify teacher quality from degrees, courses, credentials, or aptitude scores. But the evidence is mixed. Some forms of preparation matter, especially pedagogical content knowledge, but qualifications are not a strong enough guide to tell us who will reliably produce better learning."

Do not overstate this as "training does not matter." The better point is:

> Credentials are not the same as classroom expertise.

#### Attempt 2: Use observation

Relevant slides: 9-18

Message:

> Observations are useful for development, but weak for precise judgement.

Narration:

"Most of us think we know good teaching when we see it. Wiliam challenges that intuition. When people watch short video clips, even experienced educators are not very accurate at identifying which teachers produce stronger student learning. Training helps only partly. And to get highly reliable observation judgements, you would need far more observations and raters than most schools can realistically provide."

Slide 16 is the key:

> Reliable judgement would require observing a teacher teaching six different classes, with each lesson judged by five independent observers.

What this means:

"A single observation, or even a few observations, should not be treated as a precise measurement of teacher quality. Observation is better used as a tool for professional conversation, coaching, and improvement."

Slide 17 adds an important caution:

> Teachers can receive higher observation ratings when they teach higher-achieving students.

Narration:

"This matters because observation can confuse the students' starting points with the teacher's effectiveness. A calm, high-attaining class can make teaching look better. A class with more unfinished learning can make teaching look worse. So if we use observation carelessly, we may reward context rather than expertise."

#### Attempt 3: Use test-score gains

Relevant slides: 19-22

Message:

> Test-score measures contain useful information, but they are unstable and model-dependent.

Narration:

"Maybe we should stop relying on human judgement and use student progress data. That sounds objective, but it is still complicated. Different statistical models can classify the same teacher differently. Short-term gains may not match long-term learning. And some teachers may produce higher immediate scores while producing weaker future performance."

Slide 22 is important because it connects to learning science:

> Immediate performance is not the same as durable learning.

Narration:

"This is one of the strongest bridges to the rest of our program. A teacher can produce short-term performance without producing lasting learning. So our training has to focus not just on whether students can perform today, but whether learning endures and transfers."

#### Attempt 4: Combine multiple measures

Relevant slides: 23-27

Message:

> Combining measures helps, but still does not solve the problem.

Narration:

"Maybe we can combine value-added data, observations, and student surveys. That is better than any one measure alone, but the deck shows that even combined systems can require years of data to produce highly reliable predictions. And the correlation pictures show why this matters: even a decent correlation leaves a lot of room for error when judging individuals."

Slide 25 is the key:

> To get a 90 percent reliable prediction of a teacher's quality, you would need data for each teacher for nine years.

Interpretation:

"If a school has to wait nine years to be confident about a teacher-quality label, then ranking teachers cannot be the main strategy for improving next year's learning."

### Act 3: Replacing Teachers Is Not Enough

Relevant slides: 28-30

Message:

> Removing less effective teachers produces surprisingly small system-wide gains.

Narration:

"If teacher quality matters and measurement is imperfect, perhaps we should still remove the weakest teachers. Wiliam looks at this too. The result is sobering. Even fairly aggressive removal policies produce very small average gains in learning across the system."

Slide 30 is the key:

> The estimated gains are around 0.0 to 0.7 extra weeks of learning per student per year, depending on the policy.

Interpretation:

"This does not mean no teacher should ever be removed. It means removal cannot be the main improvement strategy. It is too blunt, too slow, and too small to deliver the scale of improvement we need."

### Act 4: Therefore, Invest in Teacher Learning

Relevant slide: 31

This is the conclusion of the deck.

Key message:

> The only way to improve student achievement at scale is to invest in the teachers we already have.

Narration:

"The argument has now narrowed. Teacher quality matters. But we cannot reliably identify it quickly. We cannot easily buy it through credentials. We cannot accurately judge it through occasional observations. We cannot depend on test-score measures alone. And replacement strategies do not produce enough improvement. So the only strategy with the required scale is to help the teachers already in the building become more expert over time."

This is where you connect to the rest of the training program:

"That is why we are studying learning science, cognitive load, formative assessment, feedback, retrieval practice, metacognition, and responsive teaching. These are not random techniques. They are ways of making teacher learning more precise, more evidence-based, and more connected to student learning."

## Suggested Opening Script

"I want to begin with a claim that is both obvious and often mishandled: teachers matter. But when schools say teachers matter, they often turn too quickly to judgement. Who is good? Who is weak? Who should be rewarded? Who should be replaced?

Dylan Wiliam's argument is more useful and more humane. Teacher expertise matters enormously, but it is difficult to measure precisely. Qualifications tell us only part of the story. Observations are useful but noisy and biased. Test scores contain information but are unstable and can confuse short-term performance with long-term learning. And if we try to improve mainly by replacing low-performing teachers, the gains are much smaller than people expect.

So the conclusion is not 'judge teachers harder.' The conclusion is 'help teachers learn better.' If we want student learning to improve at scale, then teacher learning has to become a core function of the school."

## Suggested Closing Script

"The takeaway from this deck is not that teacher quality is mysterious or that evidence is useless. The takeaway is that teacher quality is too important to leave to crude measurement and occasional professional development.

We need a better improvement model. That model starts with learning science: how memory works, how practice works, how feedback works, how students become more independent, and how teachers can collect evidence of learning while there is still time to respond.

So this deck is the doorway into the rest of the program. It tells us why the work matters and why the strategy has to be sustained teacher learning."

# 2. Why Formative Assessment Matters

_Section: Foundations and Motivation_

## Core Thesis

Formative assessment should be a priority because teaching is uncertain.

Students do not reliably learn what we teach. They bring different prior knowledge, interpret tasks differently, forget quickly, and can perform today without retaining the learning. Formative assessment is the system teachers use to find out what students are actually thinking, then adapt instruction while there is still time to make a difference.

This deck is the best opening argument for why responsive teaching belongs at the center of the professional development program.

## The Story in One Paragraph

The deck begins with a challenge: schools need higher achievement, but cannot get there by narrowing the curriculum or making learning joyless. The solution is not more testing, but better use of evidence during learning. Formative assessment matters because students' prior knowledge is the biggest influence on what they learn next, and teachers cannot assume students learned what was taught. Wiliam then gives four arguments: the intuitive argument, the functional argument, the equity argument, and the empirical argument. Together they show that formative assessment is not a technique or a test; it is a disciplined way of making teaching responsive.

## The Emotional Framing

Present this deck as:

> "We are not adding more assessment. We are improving the quality of decisions during teaching."

Avoid framing it as:

> "Teachers need to collect more data."

The word "assessment" can make teachers think of marking, tests, accountability, and workload. The narrative should keep returning to evidence, decisions, and action.

## The Four Acts of the Deck

### Act 1: The Challenge Is Bigger Than Test Scores

Relevant slides: 1-3

Key message:

> We need better learning without narrowing the curriculum or destroying the desire to keep learning.

Narration:

"The problem is not just that students need higher scores. They need deeper achievement, broader capabilities, and the desire to keep learning. Any improvement strategy that raises test performance by narrowing the curriculum is too small. We need approaches that improve learning inside the normal work of teaching."

This is a useful bridge from teacher quality to learning science: if teachers are the improvement lever, they need practical ways to make better instructional decisions.

### Act 2: The Intuitive Case - Students Do Not Learn What We Teach

Relevant slides: 4-10

Key message:

> If prior knowledge drives learning, teachers need evidence of students' current thinking.

Narration:

"The intuitive argument is simple. What students already know shapes what they can learn next. But students do not simply absorb what we teach. They misunderstand, partially understand, forget, overgeneralize, or appear to understand while only performing in the moment. So teachers need plan B built into plan A."

Slides 5 and 6 are the center of this act.

Use the phrase:

> "Build plan B into plan A."

That is the memorable practical idea. A formative lesson is not a fixed script. It is a lesson designed with decision points.

### Act 3: The Functional Case - Formative Assessment Is a Decision System

Relevant slides: 11-13, 25-41

Key message:

> Formative assessment means using evidence to adapt teaching and learning.

Narration:

"Wiliam's framework asks three questions: where is the learner going, where is the learner now, and how do we get the learner there? These questions can be answered by the teacher, peers, or the learner. That produces the five key strategies: clarify learning intentions, elicit evidence, give feedback, activate peers, and activate students as owners of learning."

This is the framework to reuse across the whole PD program.

The practical sequence is:

1. Clarify the learning destination.
2. Elicit evidence from all students.
3. Interpret the evidence.
4. Decide what to do next.
5. Help students use feedback, peers, and self-assessment to close the gap.

### Act 4: The Equity and Empirical Case - It Is High Impact and Low Cost

Relevant slides: 14-24

Key message:

> Formative assessment is one of the most practical equity strategies because it helps teachers respond before gaps widen.

Narration:

"The equity argument is not just about closing the gap at the end of the year. It is about not allowing gaps to grow unnoticed during lessons and units. If teachers know what students understand now, they can respond now. That is why feedback, peer tutoring, and metacognition appear as cost-effective approaches in the evidence summaries."

The empirical argument matters, but keep it short:

"There is enough evidence to justify serious investment, but the main reason formative assessment matters is that it changes daily classroom decisions."

## Suggested Opening Script

"I want to separate formative assessment from the idea of more tests. Formative assessment is not a test type. It is a way of teaching under uncertainty.

Every lesson contains uncertainty. We do not know exactly what students bring with them. We do not know exactly what they understood from our explanation. We do not know which students are performing fluently but will forget next week. So the question is: how do we make better decisions during learning?

That is the purpose of formative assessment."

## Suggested Closing Script

"The reason formative assessment matters is that teaching is responsive work. If we teach without evidence, we are guessing. If we collect evidence but do nothing with it, it is not formative. The power is in the loop: evidence, interpretation, action.

This is why the rest of the program focuses on learning intentions, questioning, feedback, peer learning, self-regulation, and teacher learning communities. They are all parts of the same system."

## Slides To Prioritize

For a 20-30 minute teacher-facing version, use:

1. Slide 2 - the challenge.
2. Slide 3 - four perspectives.
3. Slide 5 - prior knowledge and "students do not learn what we teach."
4. Slide 6 - plan B into plan A.
5. Slide 12 or 13 - the five key strategies framework.
6. Slides 15-20 - equity and cost-effectiveness.
7. Slide 24 - direct experimental evidence.
8. Slides 31-33 - learning intentions and success criteria.
9. Slides 36, 38-41 - eliciting evidence and feedback.
10. Slides 43-61 - peers and students as owners of learning.

## Discussion Questions

1. Where in our current lessons do we already build plan B into plan A?
2. Where are we still waiting too long to find out what students understood?
3. Which evidence-gathering routines would give us information from all students, not just the most confident?
4. How can feedback be redesigned so students have to think and act?
5. What would make formative assessment feel like better teaching rather than extra workload?

## Link to the Training Program

This deck should introduce the whole formative assessment strand:

- Learning intentions: students need to know what quality looks like.
- Eliciting evidence: teachers need to know what students are thinking.
- Feedback: information must lead to learner action.
- Peer learning: students can become resources for each other.
- Self-regulation: students can learn to monitor and improve their own work.

The final message:

> Formative assessment is not more assessment. It is better teaching under conditions of uncertainty.

# 3. Cognitive Load Theory in Practice

_Section: Learning, Memory, and Cognitive Load_

## Core Thesis

Cognitive load theory is a design constraint for teaching.

It does not tell teachers everything about instruction, but it explains why some tasks create activity without learning. Students learn when their working memory is focused on the right content, with enough support to connect new information to long-term memory and not so much unnecessary load that the learning collapses.

## The Story in One Paragraph

The deck begins by defining learning as a change in long-term memory and separating learning from immediate performance. It then shows that expertise is built from organized knowledge in long-term memory, not generic thinking skill alone. From there, Wiliam explains working memory limits and cognitive load: intrinsic load comes from the complexity of what is being learned, while extraneous load comes from how instruction is designed. The practical conclusion is that teachers should reduce unnecessary load, manage necessary load, use worked examples and integrated materials, and remember that novices and experts need different kinds of support.

## The Four Acts of the Deck

### Act 1: Learning Is Not the Same as Performance

Relevant slides: 3-13

Key message:

> A student can look successful during a task without retaining the learning.

Narration:

"Before talking about cognitive load, Wiliam resets the definition of learning. Learning is a change in long-term memory. That means we cannot judge learning only by whether students complete today's task. Performance is visible now; learning is what remains later."

Use this act to challenge poor proxies for learning:

- students are busy;
- students seem engaged;
- students complete the task;
- students say they understand.

The implication:

> Instruction should be designed for durable learning, not just smooth performance.

### Act 2: Expertise Depends on Organized Knowledge

Relevant slides: 14-25

Key message:

> Experts do not just think better. They know more, and their knowledge is organized for use.

Narration:

"The chess and memory examples show why knowledge matters. Experts see meaningful patterns that novices cannot see. What looks like better general thinking is often better domain-specific knowledge in long-term memory."

This matters because teachers sometimes ask novices to behave like experts:

- solve open problems too early;
- infer patterns without enough examples;
- discover procedures without enough prior knowledge;
- monitor their own learning before they know what quality looks like.

The bridge to instruction:

> We build thinking by building usable knowledge.

### Act 3: Working Memory Is the Bottleneck

Relevant slides: 26-34

Key message:

> Working memory is limited; long-term memory is the main resource for learning.

Narration:

"Cognitive load theory starts from a simple architecture: working memory is limited, long-term memory is vast. When students face too many interacting elements at once, working memory overloads. When that happens, students may be active, but not learning the intended content."

Use the distinction:

- Intrinsic load: the complexity built into the material.
- Extraneous load: the unnecessary load created by presentation, task format, or poor sequencing.

The teacher's job is not to reduce all load. It is to:

1. reduce unnecessary load;
2. manage necessary load;
3. keep attention on the learning goal.

### Act 4: Design Instruction for Novices

Relevant slides: 35-58

Key message:

> Novices need worked examples, integrated explanations, and carefully sequenced practice.

Narration:

"The final section turns theory into design. Worked examples help novices because they reduce search. Integrated diagrams help because students do not waste working memory matching labels to parts. Dual coding helps when verbal and visual channels support one another. But extra information can become redundancy, and supports that help novices can get in the way for experts."

Practical implications:

- Use worked examples before independent problem solving.
- Fade support gradually.
- Integrate text and diagrams where students would otherwise split attention.
- Remove decorative or redundant material.
- Pre-teach vocabulary and prerequisite knowledge when it reduces intrinsic load.
- Change support as expertise grows.

## Suggested Opening Script

"Cognitive load theory is not a style of teaching. It is a warning about human cognitive architecture. Students have limited working memory. If our explanations, diagrams, tasks, or questions overload working memory, students may appear busy but fail to learn what we intended."

## Suggested Closing Script

"The practical takeaway is not 'make everything easy.' The takeaway is 'make the right things hard.' Students should think hard about the content, not about confusing instructions, split diagrams, missing vocabulary, or tasks that ask novices to search blindly. Good instruction protects working memory for learning."

## Teacher Workshop Activity

Ask teachers to bring one explanation, worksheet, slide, or task. In pairs, they identify:

1. What is the intended learning?
2. What prior knowledge does the task assume?
3. What interacting elements must be held in working memory?
4. What extraneous load can be removed?
5. Where would a worked example or partial example help?

## Link to Rosenshine

Cognitive load theory gives a learning-science explanation for several Rosenshine principles:

- Small steps reduce overload.
- Models and worked examples reduce search.
- Guided practice lets students rehearse with support.
- Checks for understanding reveal overload early.
- High success rates help teachers calibrate challenge.
- Independent practice comes after enough guidance.

The final message:

> Good teaching makes students think hard about the right things.

# 4. Learning Memory and Retrieval

_Section: Learning, Memory, and Cognitive Load_

## Core Thesis

This is the broadest learning science deck in the curated set.

Its central message is that durable learning depends on building, organizing, retrieving, and regulating long-term memory. The deck also warns teachers not to be seduced by neuroscience, immediate performance, student feelings of fluency, or generic motivation language.

Use this deck as the main source for the learning science chapter of the manual.

## The Story in One Paragraph

Wiliam begins by arguing that neuroscience is interesting but rarely gives direct guidance for pedagogy. The more useful level is cognitive science: expertise, memory, practice, retrieval, and self-regulation. The deck then develops a chain of ideas: expertise is mostly built through practice and organized knowledge; learning and performance are different; cognitive load explains why novices need support; retrieval, spacing, interleaving, and testing make learning more durable; students often misjudge their own learning; and self-regulated learning requires metacognition, motivation, and accurate beliefs about effort and strategy.

## The Six Acts of the Deck

### Act 1: Do Not Start With Neuroscience

Relevant slides: 3-18

Key message:

> Brain images are not the same as teaching guidance.

Narration:

"Neuroscience can identify correlates of cognitive activity, and it can help diagnose some learning difficulties. But most teaching decisions are better guided by cognitive science: what students attend to, remember, retrieve, misunderstand, and transfer."

This act is useful for preventing neuromyths and science theater.

Teacher-facing phrasing:

> "We do not need brain scans to improve tomorrow's lesson. We need a better model of learning."

### Act 2: Expertise Is Built, Not Simply Found

Relevant slides: 19-33

Key message:

> Talent matters, but practice and knowledge matter more than schools often assume.

Narration:

"The examples from music and surgery show that high performance depends on deliberate practice, feedback, and accumulated experience. Wiliam is careful: talent is not irrelevant. But treating aptitude as a fixed ceiling is the wrong model. A better model is that aptitude often means time needed to learn."

Use this to set a professional culture:

- students differ in starting points and speed;
- teachers differ in current expertise;
- both can improve with the right practice conditions.

### Act 3: Learning and Performance Are Different

Relevant slides: 34-40

Key message:

> What looks good now may not last.

Narration:

"Students can perform well during blocked practice, copied examples, or heavily scaffolded tasks, but that does not prove durable learning. Some conditions that feel harder now produce better retention and transfer later."

This is the bridge to retrieval, spacing, and interleaving.

Use this phrase repeatedly:

> "Fluency today is not the same as learning for later."

### Act 4: Cognitive Load Explains Why Novices Need Design Support

Relevant slides: 41-136

Key message:

> New learning runs through limited working memory, so instruction must manage load.

Narration:

"The deck spends a long section on cognitive load because it explains why students can complete activities but miss the intended learning. Novices do not have the schemas experts have. They need explicit instruction, worked examples, integrated materials, and reduced extraneous load."

Key practical ideas:

- pre-teach prerequisite knowledge;
- break complex ideas into manageable chunks;
- use worked examples and completion problems;
- reduce split attention and redundancy;
- fade guidance as expertise grows.

### Act 5: Make Learning Stick

Relevant slides: 137-208

Key message:

> Durable learning depends on retrieval, spacing, interleaving, and desirable difficulty.

Narration:

"This is the most directly useful part for lesson and curriculum design. Students learn more when they retrieve rather than simply restudy, when practice is distributed rather than massed, when problem types are interleaved rather than always blocked, and when review is timed to make retrieval effortful but possible."

This section aligns very strongly with The Math Academy Way.

Pull out these classroom principles:

- Begin lessons with short review.
- Revisit material across weeks and months.
- Use low-stakes quizzes.
- Mix old and new problem types.
- Use pre-tests or preview questions when they focus attention.
- Treat retrieval as learning, not just checking.

### Act 6: Students Need Help Managing Their Own Learning

Relevant slides: 209-349

Key message:

> Self-regulation is teachable, but only when it is grounded in subject knowledge and good learning strategies.

Narration:

"Students do not reliably know when they are learning. They often choose strategies that feel fluent rather than strategies that build durable memory. So self-regulated learning requires explicit instruction in planning, monitoring, evaluating, retrieval practice, resource use, and attribution."

Important caution:

> Do not turn metacognition into generic thinking skills lessons.

Better approach:

- model subject-specific thinking;
- teach students how to plan, monitor, and evaluate within real tasks;
- help them attribute success and failure to controllable causes: effort, strategy, and practice quality.

## Suggested Opening Script

"This session is about what learning actually is. We will not begin with brain scans, because most classroom decisions do not need neuroscience. They need a practical model of memory: how knowledge gets into long-term memory, how it becomes retrievable, and how students learn to manage that process."

## Suggested Closing Script

"The big message is that learning is not the same as performance. Our job is not just to help students succeed in today's activity. It is to build knowledge and skill that students can retrieve, use, and transfer later. That means we need retrieval, spacing, interleaving, feedback, and explicit support for self-regulated learning."

2. Slides 45-60 - long-term memory, working memory, and cognitive load.

# 5. Designing for Durable Learning

_Section: Learning, Memory, and Cognitive Load_

## Core Thesis

This deck connects cognitive load theory to durable learning.

It argues that teachers need to design instruction around two constraints: working memory is limited, and long-term retention requires retrieval, spacing, and interleaving. In other words, lessons must first make new material learnable, then make it stick.

## The Story in One Paragraph

The deck begins by separating learning from performance. It then introduces cognitive load theory through an evolutionary account of learning, showing why novices need instruction that builds long-term memory rather than expecting them to discover everything. The main middle section explains intrinsic and extraneous load, split attention, redundancy, worked examples, and expertise reversal. The final section turns from "getting things in" to "making things stick" through retrieval practice, spaced learning, and interleaved practice.

## The Four Acts of the Deck

### Act 1: Design for Learning, Not Smooth Performance

Relevant slides: 3-8

Key message:

> Some activities create successful performance without durable learning.

Narration:

"The deck starts by warning us that performance and learning can move in different directions. A task can feel smooth, and students can get answers right, while the intended learning does not transfer to long-term memory."

This is the reason the rest of the deck matters:

> We need design principles that improve later retention and transfer, not just today's task completion.

### Act 2: Human Cognitive Architecture Matters

Relevant slides: 9-25

Key message:

> Students learn school knowledge most efficiently when they can borrow organized knowledge from others.

Narration:

"Wiliam distinguishes biologically primary knowledge from biologically secondary knowledge. Some things humans learn naturally through everyday interaction. School knowledge, like reading, mathematics, science, and history, usually requires teaching."

This supports explicit instruction without making the training ideological:

> The argument is not that students should never inquire. The argument is that novices need enough knowledge and structure before inquiry becomes productive.

### Act 3: Manage Cognitive Load

Relevant slides: 26-83

Key message:

> Reduce unnecessary load and manage necessary load.

Narration:

"Intrinsic load comes from the complexity of the material for a particular learner. Extraneous load comes from design choices that make learning harder than it needs to be. Good teaching reduces extraneous load and optimizes intrinsic load."

Key examples:

- split-attention effect: integrate diagrams and labels;
- redundancy effect: remove information that duplicates rather than supports;
- goal-free effect: reduce means-ends search for novices;
- worked-example effect: show solution structures before expecting independent problem solving;
- expertise reversal: withdraw supports as students become more expert.

Teacher-facing rule:

> Do not ask working memory to do what long-term memory should do.

### Act 4: Make Learning Stick

Relevant slides: 84-95

Key message:

> Once material is understandable, students still need retrieval, spacing, and interleaving.

Narration:

"Cognitive load theory helps with initial learning, but initial learning is not enough. The final section shows why retrieval practice, distributed practice, and interleaving are essential for durable learning. Restudy can feel good, but retrieval strengthens memory more. Blocked practice can look fluent, but interleaved practice often improves later discrimination and transfer."

This is the bridge to curriculum and homework design:

- plan review across time;
- mix old and new material;
- use quizzes as learning events;
- space practice so students have to retrieve;
- do not wait until the end of a unit to revisit key ideas.

## Suggested Opening Script

"This deck is about designing learning that lasts. First, we need to help students understand new material without overloading working memory. Then we need to make sure the learning survives beyond the lesson. Cognitive load helps with the first problem. Retrieval, spacing, and interleaving help with the second."

## Suggested Closing Script

"The practical challenge for teachers is to design a sequence, not just a lesson. We introduce new material with enough support to make it learnable. We reduce unnecessary load. Then we revisit it through retrieval, spacing, and interleaving until students can use it later."

# 6. Learning Intentions and Useful Feedback

_Section: Formative Assessment Core_

## Core Thesis

Students can only improve their work when they understand what quality means and have feedback they can act on.

This deck connects three ideas that are often taught separately: learning intentions, success criteria, and feedback. The narrative is that clarity is not about writing objectives on the board. ## The Story in One Paragraph

Wiliam distinguishes the intended learning from the task context, warns against weak rubrics and over-specified process criteria, and emphasizes examples as a route into quality. The second half turns to feedback: feedback is only feedback if it is used, grades can block learning, and the best feedback causes thinking rather than doing the work for the student.

## The Four Acts of the Deck

### Act 1: Clarity Starts With Learning, Not Activity

Relevant slides: 3-14

Key message:

> The task is not the learning.

Narration:

"Wiliam begins by reminding us that students can be busy, engaged, and successful in the moment without durable learning. That means the teacher has to be clear about the intended change in long-term memory, not just the task students will complete."

Use the distinction:

- Task: what students do.
- Learning intention: what students are supposed to learn through doing it.

### Act 2: Learning Intentions Should Detach Learning From Context

Relevant slides: 15-40

Key message:

Important cautions:

- Do not confuse learning intentions with success criteria.
- Do not differentiate learning intentions when the intended learning should be common.
- Do not turn success criteria into mechanical recipes that block thinking.
- Use examples before rubrics when students need to understand quality.

### Act 3: Feedback Must Be Used To Count as Feedback

Relevant slides: 41-58

Key message:

> Information is feedback only if it helps close the gap.

Narration:

"Wiliam returns to the engineering meaning of feedback: information about a gap that is used to alter the gap. In classrooms, we often call comments, marks, grades, praise, or corrections 'feedback' even when students do nothing useful with them. The deck challenges that habit."

Use the key principles:

- Feedback should improve the student, not just the work.
- Feedback should cause thinking.
- Feedback should provide guidance without doing the work.
- Feedback should be more work for the recipient than the donor.

### Act 4: Feedback Lives Inside Motivation and Self-Regulation

Relevant slides: 59-99

Key message:

> Students need feedback, but they also need the beliefs and routines that let them use it.

Narration:

"The later slides connect feedback to memory, retrieval, learning progressions, mindset, wise feedback, and student agency. This is important because feedback can fail for emotional and cognitive reasons. Students may ignore it, reject it, become dependent on it, or focus on protecting self-worth rather than improving."

The practical conclusion:

> Feedback works best when it is part of a culture where students expect to revise, retrieve, compare, monitor, and improve.

# 7. Responsive Teaching in Action

_Section: Formative Assessment Core_

## Core Thesis

Responsive teaching means designing lessons with evidence-based decision points.

This deck is compact and practical. It takes the big idea of formative assessment and turns it into a classroom sequence: clarify the destination, elicit evidence from all students, interpret the evidence, give feedback that causes thinking, and adapt what happens next.

## The Story in One Paragraph

The deck begins with the central reason for formative assessment: students do not learn exactly what teachers teach. Because learning depends on prior knowledge and because performance today may not last, teachers need to build plan B into plan A. The deck then introduces the five formative assessment strategies, focusing especially on eliciting evidence and feedback. It shows how diagnostic and hinge questions can reveal student thinking quickly, then closes with principles for feedback that moves learning forward.

## The Four Acts of the Deck

### Act 1: Teaching Requires Contingency

Relevant slides: 1-6

Key message:

> A good lesson is planned, but not rigid.

Narration:

"The opening argument is that students do not always learn what we teach. So responsive teaching means planning for decision points. We do not just plan the explanation and activity. We plan how we will find out what students understood and what we will do with that evidence."

Use the memorable phrase:

> "Build Plan B into Plan A."

### Act 2: Elicit Evidence From All Students

Relevant slides: 7-19

Key message:

> The teacher needs evidence from the whole room, not volunteers.

Narration:

"Questioning has two purposes: to cause thinking and to collect evidence. If only confident students respond, the teacher is not seeing the class. Wiliam's practical alternatives include no hands up, all-student response systems, ABCD cards, mini-whiteboards, and hinge questions."

The crucial design principle:

> A correct response should indicate correct thinking.

That is why distractor-driven questions matter. The wrong answers should reveal likely misconceptions.

### Act 3: Use Hinge Questions To Decide What Happens Next

Relevant slides: 10-18

Key message:

> A hinge question is not a quiz item. It is a teaching decision tool.

Narration:

"A hinge question sits at a point in the lesson where the teacher needs to know whether to move on, reteach, give more practice, or change the task. Every student answers quickly, and the teacher can interpret responses quickly."

Practical criteria:

- based on a critical concept;
- answerable by every student within two minutes;
- interpretable by the teacher in about 30 seconds;
- includes distractors based on real misconceptions;
- leads to a planned instructional response.

### Act 4: Feedback Should Make Students Think

Relevant slides: 20-29

Key message:

> Feedback is useful only when it changes what students do next.

Narration:

"The feedback section warns that feedback is not automatically beneficial. Grades can stop students reading comments. Comments can be too directive. Feedback can lower achievement if it shifts attention away from the task or convinces students the goal is out of reach."

Use the general principles:

- improve the student, not just the work;
- focus on action;
- cause thinking;
- give students time to respond;
- make feedback more work for the learner than the teacher.

## Suggested Opening Script

"Responsive teaching is the daily version of formative assessment. It asks: what do students understand right now, and what should I do next? The key is to design lessons with moments where evidence changes the path of instruction."

## Suggested Closing Script

"The simplest version of responsive teaching is a loop: plan, elicit, interpret, respond. If evidence does not change anything, it is not formative. If feedback does not lead to student action, it is not useful."

# 10. Eliciting Evidence of Thinking

_Section: Feedback, Metacognition, and Student Ownership_

## Core Thesis

Teachers cannot respond to thinking they cannot see.

This deck is about designing tasks, questions, and routines that reveal student understanding from the whole class, not just from volunteers. It also connects questioning to retrieval practice: good questions both cause thinking and give the teacher evidence for action.

## The Story in One Paragraph

The deck begins with Wiliam's formative assessment framework and zooms in on eliciting evidence. It challenges hand-raising and volunteer-based questioning because they give teachers a distorted view of the class. It then uses memory research to show why retrieval matters and gives examples of diagnostic questions across subjects. The practical center of the deck is hinge-question design: identify a critical point in the lesson, anticipate misconceptions, create distractors, collect responses from everyone, and decide what to do next.

## The Four Acts of the Deck

### Act 1: Evidence Must Come From Everyone

Relevant slides: 1-6

Key message:

> Volunteer answers are not class evidence.

Narration:

"If only a few students answer, the teacher may think the class understands when many students do not. Eliciting evidence means creating routines where every student has to think and the teacher can see enough responses to make a decision."

Core practices:

- no hands up except to ask a question;
- random selection;
- no opting out;
- all-student response systems;
- mini-whiteboards, ABCD cards, exit passes.

### Act 2: Retrieval Is Learning, Not Just Checking

Relevant slides: 7-13

Key message:

> Asking students to retrieve strengthens memory.

Narration:

"The deck connects formative assessment to memory. Retrieval practice does two jobs: it shows what students can access now, and it strengthens future access. The new theory of disuse explains why effortful retrieval is powerful: retrieval strength rises and falls, while storage strength grows through successful retrieval."

Teacher implication:

> Low-stakes questions are not interruptions to learning. They are learning.

Relevant slides: 14-25

Key message:

> A good question reveals why students are wrong, not just whether they are wrong.

Narration:

"The subject examples show that diagnostic questions are carefully designed. The wrong answers are not random. They represent likely misconceptions or partial understandings. That is why a multiple-choice question can be more powerful than an open question when it is designed well."

- distractor-driven multiple choice;
- multiple-correct-response questions;
- quick classification questions;
- questions that reveal overgeneralization;
- questions that separate similar concepts.

Relevant slides: 26-29

Key message:

> The point of evidence is to decide what happens next.

Narration:

"A hinge question is placed where the lesson could go in different directions. If students are ready, the teacher moves on. If not, the teacher reteaches, gives more practice, changes grouping, or uses another representation."

# 12. Metacognition and Self-Regulation

_Section: Feedback, Metacognition, and Student Ownership_

## Core Thesis

Students become better learners when they can plan, monitor, evaluate, and adjust their own learning inside real subject tasks.

This deck is a careful treatment of metacognition. It avoids the common mistake of treating metacognition as a generic thinking skill. Wiliam connects it to memory, cognitive load, feedback, beliefs, motivation, and domain knowledge.

## The Story in One Paragraph

The deck begins by reviewing learning and memory: learning is long-term memory change, retrieval matters, and cognitive load must be managed. It then defines metacognition and self-regulated learning as forms of regulation: goal setting, monitoring, feedback interpretation, and adjustment. Wiliam shows that self-regulated learning is associated with achievement, but novices cannot self-regulate like experts because they lack schemas and automated skills. The deck then moves into practical teaching: explicit metacognitive strategies, wise feedback, planning-monitoring-evaluating routines, motivation, attribution, and growth mindset as a means to better learning behavior.

## The Five Acts of the Deck

### Act 1: Metacognition Builds on Learning Science

Relevant slides: 3-5

Key message:

> Students can regulate learning only if they understand something about how learning works.

Narration:

"The deck reviews memory, retrieval, cognitive load, and expertise first because self-regulation is not magic. If students believe rereading feels better than retrieval, they may choose weak strategies. If they lack prior knowledge, they may not know what to monitor."

### Act 2: Regulation Means Goal, Monitor, Interpret, Adjust

Relevant slides: 6-14

Key message:

> Metacognition is active regulation in pursuit of a goal.

Narration:

"Wiliam defines metacognition as more than 'thinking about thinking.' It is monitoring and regulating thought and action in relation to a goal. Sadler's idea is central: students need a concept of quality, the ability to monitor their work, and a repertoire of moves to improve it."

Use this three-part model:

1. Know what quality looks like.
2. Monitor current performance against that quality.
3. Choose a strategy to close the gap.

### Act 3: Novices Need Support To Self-Regulate

Relevant slides: 15-20

Key message:

> Self-regulation is harder for novices because they lack knowledge.

Narration:

"Experts can work forward because they have schemas and automaticity. Novices often work backward and overload working memory. So telling students to 'be metacognitive' is not enough. Teachers must model thinking, teach strategies explicitly, and embed metacognitive prompts in subject tasks."

Practical implication:

> Teach metacognition through the curriculum, not as a separate thinking-skills add-on.

### Act 4: Feedback and Beliefs Shape Student Agency

Relevant slides: 21-46

Key message:

> Students' response to feedback depends on trust, attribution, and beliefs about ability.

Narration:

"The wise feedback studies show that critical feedback works better when students believe the teacher has high standards and believes they can meet them. The mindset evidence is treated carefully: effects are not huge, and posters are not enough. The value of growth mindset is that it can support better strategy choice and persistence."

Teacher moves:

- frame feedback as high standards plus support;
- focus on strategies students can change;
- avoid praise that makes ability the focus;
- create classroom norms where challenge-seeking is normal.

### Act 5: Teach Planning, Monitoring, and Evaluating

Relevant slides: 47-59

Key message:

> Metacognition improves when teachers teach concrete questions and routines.

Narration:

"The metacognitive training examples show how students can be taught to ask comprehension, connection, strategy, and reflection questions. This is the practical heart of the deck."

Examples:

- Planning: What do I know? What strategy will I use?
- Monitoring: Is this working? Do I need to adjust?
- Evaluating: What worked? What will I change next time?

"Metacognition is not a poster that says 'think about your thinking.' It is the ability to manage learning while learning is happening. That requires knowledge, strategies, feedback, and practice."

## Suggested Closing Script

"The goal is not independent learners on day one. The goal is gradual transfer of responsibility. Teachers model, guide, prompt, and then reduce support as students become more able to regulate their own learning."

# 13. Feedback That Moves Learning Forward

_Section: Feedback, Metacognition, and Student Ownership_

## Core Thesis

Feedback should change the learner, not merely comment on the work.

This deck is about wise feedback: feedback that causes thinking, protects learner agency, and leads to action. It is especially useful because Wiliam shows that feedback is powerful but risky; it can improve learning, do nothing, or make things worse.

## The Story in One Paragraph

The deck begins by unpacking the term feedback and showing that classroom feedback is often much less precise than engineering feedback. It reviews evidence that grades can block learning and that feedback effects vary widely. Wiliam then connects feedback to memory, retrieval, rubrics, learning progressions, and motivation. The main practical conclusion is that feedback should be designed as work for the learner: detective work, revision work, comparison work, or planning work.

## The Five Acts of the Deck

### Act 1: Feedback Is Not One Thing

Relevant slides: 1-5

Key message:

> Feedback can be descriptive, evaluative, immediate, delayed, specific, generic, oral, written, positive, or corrective. Those differences matter.

Narration:

"Teachers often ask whether feedback works. Wiliam's answer is: what feedback, for whom, when, and what do students do with it? Feedback is not automatically beneficial."

### Act 2: Grades Can Block Learning

Relevant slides: 6-8

Key message:

> Students often attend to judgement rather than improvement.

Narration:

"The Butler study is central. Comments improved learning, grades did not, and grades plus comments did not produce the same benefit as comments alone. The likely reason is that grades shift attention from the task to the self."

Teacher implication:

> If the goal is improvement, do not let judgement crowd out action.

### Act 3: Feedback Connects to Memory

Relevant slides: 9-17

Key message:

> Feedback and retrieval are linked.

Narration:

"The deck moves into spaced learning and retrieval because feedback is not just correction. Students need opportunities to retrieve, compare, and adjust. Feedback works best when it is part of a learning cycle, not a final comment."

Use this to design:

- low-stakes retrieval;
- delayed review;
- correction after effortful recall;
- feedback followed by another attempt.

### Act 4: Quality Cannot Be Fully Captured in Rubrics

Relevant slides: 18-32

Key message:

> Rubrics can help, but examples and judgement are essential.

Narration:

"Wiliam warns that descriptions of quality are always incomplete. Rubrics can under-represent complex performance and create mechanical compliance. Students need to see, discuss, compare, and internalize examples of quality."

Practical rule:

> Use rubrics as tools, not substitutes for judgement.

### Act 5: Make Feedback Into Learner Action

Relevant slides: 33-63

Key message:

> Feedback should be more work for the recipient than the donor.

Narration:

"The strongest practical idea is feedback as detective work. Instead of telling students exactly what is wrong and how to fix it, the teacher gives enough information for students to locate, diagnose, and improve the work themselves."

Examples of better feedback:

- "Find the two examples where this rule breaks down."
- "One paragraph needs stronger evidence. Identify it and add the missing support."
- "Three calculations use the wrong order of operations. Find and correct them."
- "Compare your answer to the exemplar and mark one difference that matters."

## Suggested Opening Script

"Feedback is one of the highest-potential and highest-risk things teachers do. It is not enough to ask whether we gave feedback. We have to ask: what did the learner do because of it?"

## Suggested Closing Script

"The best feedback does not make students dependent on the teacher. It builds their ability to notice, diagnose, revise, and improve."

## Slides To Prioritize

Use:

1. Slides 2-4 - what feedback means.
2. Slides 6-8 - grades and comments.
3. Slides 10-17 - spacing, testing, and memory.
4. Slides 18-24 - rubrics and models of quality.
5. Slides 33-43 - wise feedback and detective work.
6. Slides 44-48 - peer feedback routines.
7. Slides 53-61 - student ownership.

## Workshop Activity

Teachers bring three comments they commonly write. Rewrite each comment so that:

1. the student has to think;

The final message:

> Feedback is not what the teacher says. Feedback is what the learner does next.

# 17. Research-Informed Teaching

_Section: Implementation, Research, and Leadership_

## Core Thesis

Research should inform professional judgement, not replace it.

This deck is about becoming a critical consumer of educational research. Wiliam's argument is that evidence matters, but school improvement always involves trade-offs, context, implementation, and opportunity cost.

## The Story in One Paragraph

The deck begins with a framework for thinking about change: understand why existing practices exist before removing them, and recognize that every improvement creates trade-offs. Wiliam then argues that research can help identify promising areas, likely moderators, possible effects, and costs, but "evidence-based" is a continuum rather than a binary category. The middle of the deck is a caution against naive meta-analysis and average effect sizes. The practical heart is five questions leaders and teachers should ask before adopting an idea: does it solve our problem, how much faster will students learn, what will it cost, can we implement it here, and do we know what to do?

## The Four Acts of the Deck

### Act 1: Change Always Has Trade-Offs

Relevant slides: 1-10

Key message:

> There are no perfect solutions, only different trade-offs.

Narration:

"The deck starts with Chesterton's fence: before removing a practice, understand why it exists. Wiliam is warning against reform energy that only sees what the current approach does badly. Every change also removes something the old approach did well."

Use these two questions:

1. What will be better if we change?
2. What will be worse if we change?

If the answer to the second question is "nothing," the plan is probably underthought.

### Act 2: Research Can Guide, But Not Dictate

Relevant slides: 10-16

Key message:

> Evidence-based is a continuum, not a category.

Narration:

"Research can help us avoid weak bets, understand conditions, estimate likely impact, and think about costs. But it cannot remove professional judgement. Teaching is a wicked learning environment: performance is not learning, signals are noisy, and practices that work in one setting may not transfer cleanly."

Practical stance:

> Use research to improve the quality of decisions, not to outsource decisions.

### Act 3: Be Careful With Effect Sizes and Meta-Analysis

Relevant slides: 19-40

Key message:

> Average effects can mislead.

Narration:

"Wiliam's critique of meta-analysis is not anti-research. It is pro-careful-research. Effect sizes depend on comparison groups, publication bias, study quality, age, population variability, outcome measures, and implementation quality."

Important cautions:

- "What works" often means "what worked somewhere."
- Outcome measures close to the intervention show larger effects.
- Studies with younger or restricted populations may show larger effect sizes.
- Average effects may hide who benefits and under what conditions.

### Act 4: Ask Better Adoption Questions

Relevant slides: 41-49

Key message:

> The adoption question is not "Does research support this?" but "Should we do this here now?"

Narration:

"The five questions are the most useful part of the deck for leaders and teachers. They turn research into a local decision process."

Use them as a checklist:

1. Does this solve a problem we actually have?
2. How much faster will students learn if it works?
3. What will it cost in money, time, attention, and workload?
4. Can we implement it here?
5. Do we know exactly what to do?

## Suggested Opening Script

"Evidence-informed practice is not about obeying research. It is about using research to make better local decisions. Research can tell us what is promising, risky, costly, or context-dependent. It cannot remove the need for judgement."

## Suggested Closing Script

"The best schools are not research faddish or research dismissive. They are disciplined. They ask whether an idea solves their problem, whether the likely benefit is worth the cost, whether implementation is realistic, and what evidence would show it is working."

## Slides To Prioritize

Use:

1. Slides 4-5 - Chesterton's fence and trade-offs.
2. Slides 7-10 - evaluation vs improvement and role of research.
3. Slides 12-16 - wicked environments and five questions.
4. Slides 20-27 - meta-analysis cautions.
5. Slides 31-39 - effect size and outcome measure issues.
6. Slides 41-49 - implementation and trustworthiness.

## Workshop Activity

Use a current school initiative. Apply the five-question test:

1. Problem it solves.

> Research does not tell us what to do. It helps us ask better questions before we act.

_Section: Implementation, Research, and Leadership_

## Core Thesis

Evidence-informed practice means translating research into local, costed, implementable decisions.

This deck is more applied than the general research-informed teaching deck. It gives leaders a practical filter for deciding whether a research-backed idea should become school practice.

## The Story in One Paragraph

The deck starts with change theory: understand the current system and its trade-offs before changing it. It then explains why teaching is a wicked learning environment: performance is not learning, observation signals are noisy, short-term results can mislead, and practices may not transfer across settings. Wiliam then turns to the role of research and the five adoption questions. The second half is a detailed warning about meta-analysis, effect sizes, RCTs, implementation, and generalizability. The closing message is that educators need to be critical consumers of research.

## The Five Acts of the Deck

### Act 1: Start With the Existing System

Relevant slides: 3-5

Key message:

> Before changing a practice, understand what it currently does.

Narration:

"Chesterton's fence is the opening discipline. Leaders often see the weaknesses of current practice more clearly than its hidden functions. Removing or replacing a practice without understanding those functions creates unintended consequences."

Decision prompt:

> What will be worse if we make this change?

### Act 2: Teaching Is a Wicked Learning Environment

Relevant slides: 7-25

Key message:

> Short-term classroom signals can mislead.

Narration:

"Wiliam builds the case that teaching is hard to learn from informally. Performance is not learning. Observation ratings are biased by student prior achievement. Short-term success can produce weaker long-term learning. Interventions can work in one setting and fail in another."

Use this to explain why teacher learning needs:

- evidence;
- theory;
- delayed measures;
- peer discussion;
- cycles of testing and refinement.

### Act 3: Use Research as a Decision Aid

Relevant slides: 26-30

Key message:

> Research can guide focus, but local judgement decides action.

Narration:

"Research can tell us where not to waste energy, what moderators matter, how large effects might be, and what costs to expect. But the adoption decision has to be local."

The five questions:

1. Does it solve a problem we have?
2. How much faster will students learn?
3. What will it cost?
4. Can we implement it here?
5. Do we know what to do?

### Act 4: Treat Effect Sizes With Caution

Relevant slides: 31-56

Key message:

> A big average effect does not automatically mean a good local bet.

Narration:

"The deck explains why meta-analysis can mislead: inappropriate comparisons, publication bias, implementation variation, outcome sensitivity, age differences, and population variability. This does not mean ignore meta-analysis. It means interpret it carefully."

Teacher/leader rule:

> An effect size is not an implementation plan.

### Act 5: Implementation Is Part of the Evidence

Relevant slides: 57-64

Key message:

> "What worked" is not the same as "what will work here."

Narration:

"The Tennessee STAR and RCT slides show that strong evidence still requires careful implementation and generalization. The final slides ask for adequate theory, standardized interventions, and intention-to-treat analysis. For schools, the practical equivalent is: be clear about the mechanism, define the practice, and evaluate everyone exposed to the initiative, not just successful adopters."

## Suggested Opening Script

"This session is about moving from research says to should we do this here. Those are not the same question. A practice can be evidence-supported and still be the wrong priority for our school right now."

## Suggested Closing Script

"Evidence-informed schools are not those with the longest bibliography. They are schools that make disciplined bets: clear problem, plausible mechanism, manageable cost, realistic implementation, and evidence of impact."

- mechanism of action;

> Evidence matters most when it disciplines our choices.

# 19. Leading Teacher Learning Over Time

_Section: Implementation, Research, and Leadership_

## Core Thesis

This is the leadership capstone deck.

Its central argument is that school excellence requires leaders to create the conditions for sustained teacher learning. That means preparing students for an uncertain future, de-implementing low-value work, understanding how learning works, improving teaching through formative assessment, building teacher learning communities, and using evidence wisely.

## The Story in One Paragraph

The deck is organized around six pillars. It starts with the future students are entering, including AI and the changing nature of work. It then argues for de-implementation: schools must remove or reduce low-value work to make room for higher-impact practice. The middle sections revisit learning science, cognitive load, retrieval, formative assessment, motivation, and feedback. The leadership heart of the deck is the knowing-doing gap: teachers do not change simply because they hear good ideas. They need structures, time, peer accountability, and support. The final sections focus on assessment literacy, research use, logic models, and evidence of impact.

## The Six Acts of the Deck

### Act 1: Lead for an Uncertain Future

Relevant slides: 10-83

Key message:

> Schools cannot prepare students by only teaching for the past.

Narration:

"The opening asks how schools prepare young people for a future we cannot fully imagine. AI and automation make this urgent. But the answer is not to chase every trend. The answer is to strengthen deep learning, adaptive expertise, curriculum clarity, and students' capacity to keep learning."

Leadership implication:

> Innovation should sharpen the mission, not distract from it.

### Act 2: Make Room for Impact Through De-Implementation

Relevant slides: 84-150

Key message:

> Leaders must remove lower-value work before adding more improvement work.

Narration:

"This is one of the most important leadership moves. Schools are full of initiatives, routines, reports, meetings, and inherited practices. If leaders keep adding, teachers cannot focus. De-implementation is not neglect. It is disciplined removal, reduction, replacement, or redesign."

Useful tools:

- initiative stocktake;
- policy-to-practice map;
- time study;
- HEAT scoring: harm, ease, acceptability, time saved;
- pre-mortem;
- sprint/run/walk pacing.

### Act 3: Lead With a Shared Model of Learning

Relevant slides: 151-309

Key message:

> Professional development needs a common model of how learning works.

Narration:

"The deck revisits cognitive load theory, memory, retrieval, spacing, interleaving, motivation, and formative assessment. This is not random content. It gives leaders and teachers a shared language for judging teaching choices."

Leadership implication:

> Do not start with techniques. Start with principles that help teachers decide when techniques are appropriate.

Core learning principles:

- learning is long-term memory change;
- performance is not learning;
- novices need support;
- reduce extraneous load;
- use retrieval, spacing, and interleaving;
- formative assessment makes learning visible.

### Act 4: Close the Knowing-Doing Gap

Relevant slides: 349-398

Key message:

> Teachers need structures for changing habits, not just information.

Narration:

"The knowing-doing gap is the leadership center of the deck. Teachers often agree with ideas but struggle to change routines. Wiliam's model for teacher learning combines content and process: evidence and ideas, plus choice, flexibility, small steps, accountability, and support."

Teacher learning communities are the practical structure:

- run for two years;
- 10 to 12 teachers;
- common agenda each month;
- feedback on classroom trials;
- new learning in small doses;
- personal action planning;
- peer observation run to the agenda of the observed teacher.

### Act 5: Know Whether It Is Working

Relevant slides: 399-425

Key message:

> Leaders need assessment literacy to interpret evidence of improvement.

Narration:

"This section reminds leaders that evidence can mislead. Validity depends on the inference being made. Reliability is part of validity. Performance assessments, observations, and learning walks require careful interpretation."

Leadership question:

> What evidence would convince us that teaching and learning are actually improving?

### Act 6: Use Research and Logic Models for Local Improvement

Relevant slides: 426-461

Key message:

> Improvement needs a theory of action.

Narration:

"The final section connects research use to logic models and implementation evidence. A program should specify inputs, activities, outputs, outcomes, and mechanisms. Without a logic model, leaders may collect data without knowing what it means."

Practical leadership move:

> For every initiative, write the mechanism: if we do X, then Y should change because Z.

## Suggested Opening Script

"This session is about leadership for teacher learning. The central problem is not that teachers lack goodwill. It is that schools often ask teachers to improve while keeping every existing demand in place. If we want better teaching, we must create time, focus, structures, and evidence for teacher learning."

## Suggested Closing Script

"The leadership task is to make improvement possible. That means choosing a few high-value priorities, removing lower-value work, building shared learning science, supporting teacher learning communities, and checking evidence carefully."
