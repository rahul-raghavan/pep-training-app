# Narrative for "Learning Intentions and Useful Feedback"

## Core Thesis

Students can only improve their work when they understand what quality means and have feedback they can act on.

This deck connects three ideas that are often taught separately: learning intentions, success criteria, and feedback. The narrative is that clarity is not about writing objectives on the board. It is about helping students develop a concept of quality, monitor their own work, and take better next steps.

## The Story in One Paragraph

The deck starts with learning science: learning is a change in long-term memory, not just task completion. It then moves into formative assessment, especially learning intentions and success criteria. Wiliam distinguishes the intended learning from the task context, warns against weak rubrics and over-specified process criteria, and emphasizes examples as a route into quality. The second half turns to feedback: feedback is only feedback if it is used, grades can block learning, and the best feedback causes thinking rather than doing the work for the student.

## The Four Acts of the Deck

### Act 1: Clarity Starts With Learning, Not Activity

Relevant slides: 3-14

Key message:

> The task is not the learning.

Narration:

"Wiliam begins by reminding us that students can be busy, engaged, and successful in the moment without durable learning. That means the teacher has to be clear about the intended change in long-term memory, not just the task students will complete."

Use the distinction:

- Task: what students do.
- Learning intention: what students are supposed to learn through doing it.
- Success criteria: what good performance on the task looks like.

### Act 2: Learning Intentions Should Detach Learning From Context

Relevant slides: 15-40

Key message:

> A good learning intention names the transferable learning, not the surface activity.

Narration:

"A learning intention should help teachers and students see what is meant to transfer beyond the immediate task. 'Write instructions for changing a bicycle tire' is a task. 'Write clear instructions' is closer to the learning. The more the learning intention is trapped inside the context, the less likely students are to transfer it."

Important cautions:

- Do not confuse learning intentions with success criteria.
- Do not differentiate learning intentions when the intended learning should be common.
- Do not turn success criteria into mechanical recipes that block thinking.
- Use examples before rubrics when students need to understand quality.

### Act 3: Feedback Must Be Used To Count as Feedback

Relevant slides: 41-58

Key message:

> Information is feedback only if it helps close the gap.

Narration:

"Wiliam returns to the engineering meaning of feedback: information about a gap that is used to alter the gap. In classrooms, we often call comments, marks, grades, praise, or corrections 'feedback' even when students do nothing useful with them. The deck challenges that habit."

Use the key principles:

- Feedback should improve the student, not just the work.
- Feedback should cause thinking.
- Feedback should provide guidance without doing the work.
- Feedback should be more work for the recipient than the donor.

### Act 4: Feedback Lives Inside Motivation and Self-Regulation

Relevant slides: 59-99

Key message:

> Students need feedback, but they also need the beliefs and routines that let them use it.

Narration:

"The later slides connect feedback to memory, retrieval, learning progressions, mindset, wise feedback, and student agency. This is important because feedback can fail for emotional and cognitive reasons. Students may ignore it, reject it, become dependent on it, or focus on protecting self-worth rather than improving."

The practical conclusion:

> Feedback works best when it is part of a culture where students expect to revise, retrieve, compare, monitor, and improve.

## Suggested Opening Script

"Today is not about putting objectives on the board. It is about helping students understand what they are trying to get better at, what quality looks like, and what to do next. If students do not understand quality, feedback becomes correction. If they do understand quality, feedback becomes a tool for improvement."

## Suggested Closing Script

"Learning intentions, success criteria, and feedback form one system. The learning intention names the destination. Success criteria and examples make quality visible. Feedback helps students close the gap. If any part is weak, students may complete tasks without getting better."

## Workshop Activity

Ask teachers to bring one lesson objective and one piece of feedback they often give. Have them revise both:

1. Strip the learning intention away from the task context.
2. Create two examples of quality before writing success criteria.
3. Rewrite feedback as a prompt that makes the student diagnose or improve something.
4. Decide what class time students will use to act on the feedback.
