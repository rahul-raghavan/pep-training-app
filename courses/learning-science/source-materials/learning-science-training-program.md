# Learning Science for Teachers: Training Program Blueprint

Working title: **Learning Science for Teachers: From Evidence to Classroom Practice**

Purpose: create a sustained professional learning program for teachers that brings together Dylan Wiliam's work, the learning-science ideas in *The Math Academy Way*, and Rosenshine's principles of instruction. The goal is a practical "one-stop shop" that motivates teachers, gives them a coherent model of learning, and helps them change classroom routines over time.

This document is a program design blueprint. It can be used to create:

- live launch workshops
- LMS modules
- embedded assessments
- printable manual chapters
- teacher reflection tasks
- teacher learning community agendas
- implementation checklists

## 1. Program Rationale

### The Problem This Program Solves

Most teacher professional development fails because it gives teachers ideas without changing classroom routines. Teachers often leave a workshop agreeing with the principles but return to the same habits, classroom pressures, assessment structures, and pacing demands.

This program should not be framed as "here is more theory." It should be framed as:

> We are building a shared model of how learning works, and then using that model to improve everyday teaching decisions.

Teachers need to understand:

- what learning actually is
- why short-term performance can mislead us
- why students often feel they are learning when they are not
- why some effective learning strategies feel effortful or uncomfortable
- why feedback, questioning, practice, and review must be deliberately designed
- why teacher improvement is best done through small changes, evidence, collaboration, and repetition

### Core Thesis

The core thesis of the program:

> Learning improves when teachers use evidence about students' current understanding to design instruction, practice, feedback, and review in ways that respect how memory, attention, motivation, and expertise actually work.

This thesis joins the three major source traditions:

- **Dylan Wiliam**: formative assessment, feedback, teacher quality, responsive teaching, teacher learning communities
- **The Math Academy Way**: cognitive learning strategies, mastery learning, automaticity, cognitive load, spacing, interleaving, retrieval practice
- **Rosenshine**: daily review, small steps, modelling, questioning, guided practice, checks for understanding, scaffolding, independent practice, weekly and monthly review

### What Teachers Should Believe By The End

By the end of the program, teachers should believe:

1. Learning is not the same as coverage, activity, enjoyment, confidence, or short-term performance.
2. Learning means durable change in long-term memory.
3. Students need strong prior knowledge and automaticity to think deeply.
4. Working memory is limited, so instruction must be carefully sequenced and scaffolded.
5. Students are often poor judges of their own learning.
6. Effective practice often feels harder than ineffective practice.
7. Retrieval, spacing, interleaving, and feedback should be built into normal teaching.
8. Formative assessment is not a test type. It is the process of using evidence to adapt teaching and learning.
9. Feedback is only formative if students use it to improve.
10. Teacher improvement requires sustained routines, peer accountability, and deliberate practice.

### What Teachers Should Be Able To Do By The End

Teachers should be able to:

- distinguish performance evidence from learning evidence
- diagnose prior knowledge and misconceptions
- reduce unnecessary cognitive load in explanations, worksheets, slides, and tasks
- use worked examples, modelling, guided practice, and scaffolds effectively
- plan retrieval practice and spaced review
- use interleaving after initial understanding has been established
- create hinge questions and all-student response routines
- give feedback that causes thinking and requires action
- build student self-assessment using criteria and exemplars
- identify missing prerequisites and plan targeted remediation
- participate in a teacher learning community using action plans and classroom evidence

## 2. Source Integration Map

### Dylan Wiliam's Contribution

Wiliam gives the program its professional development spine:

- teacher quality matters enormously
- the classroom is the key improvement unit
- formative assessment is a high-leverage, low-cost improvement strategy
- feedback must move learning forward
- students must become owners of their own learning
- teachers improve through structured, sustained, collaborative professional learning

Important Wiliam ideas to preserve:

- "An effective school is a school full of effective classrooms."
- Use evidence about learning to adapt teaching and learning.
- The five formative assessment strategies.
- Feedback should cause thinking.
- Assessment is formative only if the information is used to improve learning.
- We cannot improve by replacing all teachers; we must improve the teachers we have.
- The knowing-doing gap: knowing what works does not mean doing it consistently.
- Teacher Learning Communities need choice, flexibility, small steps, accountability, and support.

### Math Academy's Contribution

*The Math Academy Way* gives the program its learning-science engine:

- learning is change in long-term memory
- working memory is a bottleneck
- active learning beats passive consumption
- deliberate practice is effortful and targeted
- mastery learning requires students to demonstrate prerequisite knowledge before moving forward
- automaticity frees working memory for higher-order thinking
- spaced repetition makes learning durable
- interleaving improves retention, discrimination, and transfer
- retrieval practice strengthens memory more than re-reading
- students often prefer easier strategies that create illusions of competence
- technology can support personalization, but the principles are not limited to technology

Important caution:

Do not import Math Academy's full tone directly into teacher PD. Some claims are strongly platform-specific and may feel accusatory to teachers. Translate the ideas into a supportive professional-learning tone:

- avoid "teachers are doing it wrong"
- use "the system makes this hard"
- emphasize "these principles help us make better instructional trade-offs"
- distinguish what a platform can automate from what a teacher can approximate manually

### Rosenshine's Contribution

Rosenshine gives the classroom routine layer. His ten principles are a highly practical bridge between learning science and lesson design:

1. Begin a lesson with a short review of previous learning.
2. Present new material in small steps with student practice after each step.
3. Ask a large number of questions and check the responses of all students.
4. Provide models.
5. Guide student practice.
6. Check for student understanding.
7. Obtain a high success rate.
8. Provide scaffolds for difficult tasks.
9. Require and monitor independent practice.
10. Engage students in weekly and monthly review.

How Rosenshine connects:

- daily review connects to retrieval, spacing, and automaticity
- small steps connect to cognitive load
- questioning connects to formative assessment
- models connect to worked examples
- guided practice connects to scaffolding
- checking understanding connects to responsive teaching
- high success rate connects to motivation, mastery, and appropriate challenge
- scaffolds connect to cognitive load management
- independent practice connects to fluency, retention, and transfer
- weekly/monthly review connects to spaced retrieval

## 3. Recommended Program Architecture

### Full Program Shape

Recommended sequence:

1. **Live Workshop 1: Why This Matters**
2. **Live Workshop 2: What Evidence-Informed Teaching Looks Like**
3. **LMS Module Series: 10-12 self-paced modules**
4. **Teacher Learning Community cycles every 3-4 weeks**
5. **Final implementation showcase**
6. **Ongoing revision of classroom routines**

### Suggested Timeline

Option A: 12-week version

- Week 1: Live Workshop 1
- Week 2: Live Workshop 2
- Weeks 3-12: One LMS module per week
- Weeks 5, 8, 11: Teacher Learning Community meetings
- Week 13: Sharing and next-step planning

Option B: 16-week version

- Week 1: Live Workshop 1
- Week 2: Live Workshop 2
- Weeks 3-14: One LMS module every week or every 10 days
- Weeks 6, 10, 14: Teacher Learning Community meetings
- Week 16: Final implementation showcase

Option C: Semester version

- Two launch workshops in the first month
- One module every two weeks
- One teacher learning community meeting each month
- End-of-semester reflection and evidence portfolio

### Recommended First Implementation

For the first rollout, use 10 core LMS modules and keep the leadership/teacher learning community chapter as a live process rather than an extra module.

Core modules:

1. Why Teacher Learning Matters
2. What Learning Is
3. Working Memory and Cognitive Load
4. Prior Knowledge, Misconceptions, and Diagnosis
5. Explicit Instruction, Modelling, and Guided Practice
6. Retrieval, Spacing, and Interleaving
7. Automaticity, Fluency, and Higher-Order Thinking
8. Formative Assessment and Responsive Teaching
9. Feedback That Moves Learning Forward
10. Self-Regulated Learning and Metacognition

Optional extension modules:

11. Mastery Learning and Targeted Remediation
12. Teacher Learning Communities and Sustained Change
13. Using Research Wisely
14. Technology, AI, and Personalized Practice

## 4. Live Workshop 1: Why This Matters

### Purpose

Create motivation, urgency, and shared purpose. Teachers should leave understanding why the program exists and why it is worth their time.

### Core Message

> If we want students to learn more, the highest-leverage place to work is the quality of classroom decisions. This program is about improving those decisions using what we know about learning.

### Duration

Recommended: 2-3 hours.

### Intended Outcomes

Teachers will:

- understand the case for focusing on classroom practice
- distinguish school-level improvement from classroom-level improvement
- see professional learning as habit change, not information transfer
- identify a real learning problem from their own classroom
- understand why this program will combine live workshops, LMS modules, assessments, and implementation tasks

### Agenda

#### Segment 1: Opening Provocation

Prompt:

> Think of a student who was present, compliant, and doing the work, but still did not learn what you expected. What do you think was happening?

Discussion goal:

Move teachers away from simplistic explanations such as "they did not try" or "they are weak" toward instructional questions:

- What prior knowledge was missing?
- Did they only perform well during supported practice?
- Did they forget because there was no review?
- Did the feedback help them improve?
- Did we gather evidence early enough?

#### Segment 2: Why Classroom Practice Matters

Use Wiliam's argument:

- education quality matters for students' life outcomes
- teacher quality is a major lever
- classroom-level variation is large
- replacing teachers is not a realistic improvement strategy
- improving existing teachers is the practical and ethical strategy

Key discussion:

> If teacher quality matters so much, what is the fairest and most useful response?

Desired answer:

Improve the systems, routines, and professional learning structures that help teachers get better.

#### Segment 3: The Knowing-Doing Gap

Explain:

Teachers often already know many important ideas:

- ask more questions
- check for understanding
- revisit prior learning
- give useful feedback
- use examples
- support struggling students

The issue is consistency under classroom pressure.

Use this framing:

> Our goal is not just to know more. Our goal is to do the right things more reliably.

#### Segment 4: The Learning Problem

Each teacher identifies one recurring learning problem.

Examples:

- students forget after the unit test
- students can follow examples but cannot solve independently
- students do not act on feedback
- students misunderstand word problems
- students rely too much on teacher prompts
- students know facts but cannot apply them
- students seem confident but perform poorly later
- students struggle because prerequisite knowledge is weak

Teachers complete:

- My recurring learning problem is...
- I usually notice it when...
- I think one possible cause is...
- Evidence I currently have...
- Evidence I need to gather...

#### Segment 5: Program Overview

Show the program map:

1. how learning works
2. how instruction supports learning
3. how practice makes learning durable
4. how assessment guides teaching
5. how feedback and metacognition help students improve
6. how teachers improve together

### Workshop 1 Output

Each teacher leaves with a written "learning problem statement."

Template:

```text
My classroom learning problem:

Students currently...

I know this because...

This matters because...

I suspect the issue may involve:
[ ] prior knowledge
[ ] cognitive load
[ ] insufficient practice
[ ] weak retrieval/review
[ ] ineffective feedback
[ ] low student self-monitoring
[ ] unclear success criteria
[ ] another factor

During the program, I will investigate...
```

## 5. Live Workshop 2: What Evidence-Informed Teaching Looks Like

### Purpose

Give teachers a coherent preview of the learning science and classroom routines that the LMS modules will develop in depth.

### Core Message

> Evidence-informed teaching is not a bag of tricks. It is a set of decisions about memory, attention, practice, evidence, feedback, and student independence.

### Duration

Recommended: 2-3 hours.

### Intended Outcomes

Teachers will:

- understand learning as long-term memory change
- distinguish performance from learning
- understand working memory limitations
- connect Rosenshine's principles to learning science
- understand Wiliam's five formative assessment strategies
- choose one classroom routine to improve

### Agenda

#### Segment 1: Learning Is Not Performance

Explain:

- performance is what students can do now
- learning is what remains later and transfers to new contexts
- students can perform well during a lesson and forget later
- students can feel fluent because the practice is too blocked or too supported

Demonstration options:

- show a worked example, then ask teachers to solve a near-identical item
- later, remove the scaffold and ask for a transfer item
- discuss how immediate success can mislead

Key teacher question:

> What evidence do I usually collect: immediate performance or durable learning?

#### Segment 2: Working Memory and Cognitive Load

Explain:

- working memory is limited
- long-term memory is vast
- novices rely heavily on working memory
- experts rely on organized knowledge in long-term memory
- too much new information overwhelms students

Classroom implications:

- small steps
- clear modelling
- worked examples
- reduce split attention
- remove unnecessary complexity
- check prerequisites
- build automaticity

#### Segment 3: Rosenshine as Classroom Routine

Present Rosenshine's ten principles as a lesson-design checklist, not a script.

Ask:

> Which of these already happens in your classroom? Which happens inconsistently? Which would make the biggest difference?

#### Segment 4: Wiliam's Five Strategies

Introduce the formative assessment framework:

1. clarify learning intentions and success criteria
2. elicit evidence of learning
3. provide feedback that moves learning forward
4. activate students as learning resources for one another
5. activate students as owners of their own learning

Emphasize:

> Formative assessment is not about more tests. It is about better decisions.

#### Segment 5: Choose One Routine

Each teacher chooses one routine to improve during the LMS course.

Examples:

- daily retrieval starter
- hinge question
- mini-whiteboard check
- worked example plus faded practice
- feedback action time
- student self-check against criteria
- spaced review plan
- misconception diagnostic

### Workshop 2 Output

Each teacher completes an implementation commitment:

```text
The routine I will improve:

Why this routine matters:

What I currently do:

What I will try first:

What evidence I will collect:

When I will review the evidence:
```

## 6. LMS and Manual Module Design

Each LMS module should be designed for about 2 hours.

Recommended structure for each module:

1. **Hook**: short problem, classroom scenario, or misconception
2. **Core Explanation**: 15-25 minutes of reading/video
3. **Research Anchor**: Wiliam, Math Academy, Rosenshine, or another cited researcher
4. **Classroom Translation**: what this means in teaching
5. **Worked Examples**: strong and weak classroom examples
6. **Check for Understanding**: embedded quiz
7. **Application Task**: teacher adapts something from their own classroom
8. **Reflection**: short submission
9. **Implementation Evidence**: optional upload, note, or classroom artifact

Manual chapters should mirror LMS modules so the manual is not separate from the course. The manual should be a field guide teachers can annotate.

Each manual chapter should include:

- Big idea
- Why it matters
- Research summary
- Classroom implications
- Common mistakes
- Examples
- Try this next week
- Reflection prompts
- LMS assessment checklist
- Space for notes

## 7. Module 1: Why Teacher Learning Matters

### Manual Chapter Title

**The Case for Improving Teaching**

### Module Purpose

Teachers should understand why the program focuses on classroom practice and teacher learning rather than one-off interventions, technology, or generic motivation.

### Big Idea

The most powerful improvement work happens when teachers improve the quality of everyday classroom decisions.

### Key Concepts

- teacher quality as a major lever
- classroom-level variation
- the limits of structural reform
- the impossibility of replacing our way to better teaching
- professional learning as practice change
- the knowing-doing gap
- teacher learning communities

### Source Alignment

- Wiliam: teacher quality, "love the one you're with," teacher learning communities
- Rosenshine: effective teacher practices as observable routines
- Math Academy: accountability for actual learning, not just activity or grades

### Core Content

Teachers should encounter the argument in this order:

1. Student learning matters enormously.
2. Many reforms target structures: class size, schedules, technology, accountability systems.
3. These may matter, but classroom practice remains central.
4. Teacher effectiveness varies, and this variation affects students.
5. We cannot simply select, replace, or incentivize our way to excellent teaching.
6. We need to improve the teachers we already have.
7. That requires more than workshops. It requires sustained changes in habits.

### Classroom Translation

This module should not make teachers feel blamed. The message should be:

> Teaching is complex. The system often asks teachers to improve without giving them enough time, structure, evidence, or support. This program is designed to provide those things.

### Common Mistakes

- treating teacher quality as a fixed trait
- assuming experience alone leads to improvement
- assuming professional development works because teachers heard good ideas
- focusing only on motivation rather than routines
- blaming teachers instead of improving systems for teacher learning

### LMS Assessment Ideas

Question types:

- scenario classification
- multiple choice
- short reflection

Example prompts:

1. A school wants to improve student achievement. Which option is most aligned with this program's theory of action?
   - buy a new app for homework
   - reduce all classes by two students
   - build shared routines for checking understanding and adapting teaching
   - increase the number of assemblies on effort

2. Why is "knowing what works" not enough?
   - because research is never useful
   - because teachers need structures to change habits under real classroom conditions
   - because students do not respond to teaching
   - because professional development should be optional

### Teacher Application Task

Teachers write a learning problem statement and identify one classroom routine that may be contributing to the problem.

### Manual Reflection Prompts

- What is one area of my teaching that I already know matters but do not do consistently?
- What classroom pressure makes this difficult?
- What evidence would show that this routine is improving?

## 8. Module 2: What Learning Is

### Manual Chapter Title

**Learning, Memory, and Performance**

### Module Purpose

Teachers should understand that learning is durable change in long-term memory and that immediate classroom performance can be misleading.

### Big Idea

Learning is not what students can do while the teacher is guiding them. Learning is what students can still do later, independently, and in the right context.

### Key Concepts

- learning as long-term memory change
- performance vs learning
- fluency illusions
- familiarity vs mastery
- desirable difficulties
- students' inaccurate judgments of learning
- why easy learning often does not last

### Source Alignment

- Wiliam: performance is not enough; use evidence to adapt teaching
- Math Academy: learning as long-term memory change; illusion of comprehension; desirable difficulty
- Rosenshine: review and practice to strengthen recall

### Core Content

Teachers should learn:

- A student can understand an explanation in the moment and still not learn it.
- A student can copy a procedure successfully and still not know when or why to use it.
- A student can perform well on blocked practice and fail on a mixed review.
- A student can feel confident because the material feels familiar.
- Durable learning requires retrieval, spacing, application, feedback, and review.

### Classroom Translation

Teachers should ask:

- Did my students learn this, or did they only perform it today?
- What will they remember next week?
- Can they do it without cues?
- Can they choose the right method when the problem type is not signposted?
- Can they explain why the method works?

### Examples

Weak evidence of learning:

- students nod during explanation
- students complete ten identical problems immediately after a model
- students say they understand
- students highlight or re-read notes
- students perform well on a worksheet where every problem is the same type

Stronger evidence of learning:

- students retrieve the idea days later
- students solve a problem without the method being identified
- students explain reasoning
- students correct an error
- students transfer the idea to a new example
- students succeed in mixed review

### Common Mistakes

- confusing engagement with learning
- confusing confidence with learning
- overvaluing neat books or completed worksheets
- moving on after immediate success
- assuming re-reading is effective study

### LMS Assessment Ideas

Example question:

> A class completes a worksheet of 20 nearly identical problems immediately after the teacher models the method. Most students score 18/20. What can the teacher conclude?

Best answer:

> Students have shown supported short-term performance, but the teacher still needs delayed, mixed, or independent evidence before concluding durable learning.

### Teacher Application Task

Teachers select one recent lesson and identify:

- what evidence they collected
- whether that evidence measured performance or learning
- how they could collect stronger evidence next time

### Manual Reflection Prompts

- Where in my subject do students often appear to understand but forget later?
- What classroom routines may be creating illusions of learning?
- How can I check durable learning without over-testing?

## 9. Module 3: Working Memory and Cognitive Load

### Manual Chapter Title

**Designing Lessons for Limited Working Memory**

### Module Purpose

Teachers should understand working memory as a major constraint in learning and use that understanding to improve explanations, tasks, worksheets, slides, and lesson sequences.

### Big Idea

Students cannot think deeply if their working memory is overloaded by too much new information, unclear presentation, weak prerequisites, or unnecessary distractions.

### Key Concepts

- working memory
- long-term memory
- cognitive load
- intrinsic load
- extraneous load
- germane processing
- element interactivity
- split attention
- redundancy
- worked examples
- expertise reversal

### Source Alignment

- Math Academy: working memory bottleneck, cognitive load, micro-scaffolding
- Wiliam: prior knowledge and responsive teaching
- Rosenshine: small steps, models, guided practice, scaffolds

### Core Content

Teachers should learn:

- Working memory is limited.
- Long-term memory supports thinking by reducing working memory load.
- Novices and experts experience the same task differently.
- Good instruction reduces unnecessary load.
- Good sequencing manages necessary load.
- Worked examples are especially useful for novices.
- Too much discovery too early can overload students.
- Scaffolds should eventually be faded.

### Classroom Translation

Teachers should inspect:

- board work
- slides
- worksheets
- explanations
- task instructions
- problem sets
- independent work
- homework

Questions to ask:

- What is the new idea?
- What prior knowledge is required?
- How many elements must students coordinate?
- Is any information split across multiple places?
- Is there redundant text or unnecessary decoration?
- Are students being asked to problem-solve before they have enough schema?
- Where should I model?
- Where should I fade support?

### Common Mistakes

- giving too many instructions at once
- putting dense text on slides while talking
- separating diagrams from labels
- asking novices to discover what they do not yet have the tools to discover
- assuming struggle always means productive challenge
- removing scaffolds too early or never removing them

### LMS Assessment Ideas

Show teachers examples of:

- overloaded slide
- split-attention worksheet
- unclear task instruction
- too-large step in explanation

Ask them to identify the cognitive load problem and propose an improvement.

### Teacher Application Task

Teachers choose one instructional artifact and redesign it:

- a slide
- a worksheet
- a board explanation
- a set of instructions
- a worked example
- a homework task

They submit:

- original version
- revised version
- explanation of what cognitive load was reduced

### Manual Reflection Prompts

- Where do students in my class get overloaded?
- Which topic needs smaller steps?
- Which explanation needs a worked example?
- Where am I accidentally adding extraneous load?

## 10. Module 4: Prior Knowledge, Misconceptions, and Diagnosis

### Manual Chapter Title

**Start From What Students Already Know**

### Module Purpose

Teachers should understand why prior knowledge drives learning and how to diagnose student thinking before, during, and after instruction.

### Big Idea

The most important thing for new learning is what the learner already knows. Teaching improves when teachers uncover that knowledge instead of assuming it.

### Key Concepts

- prior knowledge
- prerequisite knowledge
- misconceptions
- diagnostic questions
- hinge questions
- all-student response systems
- knowledge gaps
- targeted remediation

### Source Alignment

- Wiliam: establish where learners are; elicit evidence
- Math Academy: knowledge frontier, prerequisite mastery, targeted remediation
- Rosenshine: ask many questions; check responses of all students; reteach when necessary

### Core Content

Teachers should learn:

- Students interpret new information through existing knowledge.
- Missing prerequisites increase cognitive load.
- Misconceptions are often coherent from the student's perspective.
- Good questions reveal thinking, not just correctness.
- Evidence should be collected from all students, not only volunteers.
- Diagnosis should change the next instructional move.

### Classroom Translation

Teachers should use:

- pre-lesson diagnostic questions
- hinge questions during lessons
- mini-whiteboards
- ABCD cards
- exit tickets
- misconception multiple-choice questions
- concept cartoons
- short explanations of reasoning

### Designing A Diagnostic Question

A good diagnostic question:

- focuses on one important concept
- can be answered quickly
- includes plausible wrong answers
- links wrong answers to likely misconceptions
- gives the teacher useful information
- informs a clear next action

### Hinge Question Criteria

A hinge question should:

- appear at a critical decision point in the lesson
- be answerable by all students in about two minutes
- allow the teacher to collect responses quickly
- be interpretable quickly
- reveal whether the class is ready to move on

### Common Mistakes

- asking "Does everyone understand?"
- relying on the same few students
- using questions that only test recall when diagnosis is needed
- asking open questions that are rich but impossible to interpret quickly
- collecting evidence but not adapting instruction
- diagnosing too late, after the unit has moved on

### LMS Assessment Ideas

Teachers classify sample questions:

- recall question
- discussion question
- diagnostic question
- hinge question

Teachers improve weak questions.

### Teacher Application Task

Teachers create one diagnostic or hinge question for an upcoming lesson.

Submission should include:

- question
- correct answer
- likely wrong answers
- what each wrong answer may reveal
- what the teacher will do if many students choose each option

### Manual Reflection Prompts

- Which misconceptions appear repeatedly in my subject?
- How do I currently find out what students know?
- Do I collect evidence from all students or only some students?
- What do I do when the evidence surprises me?

## 11. Module 5: Explicit Instruction, Modelling, and Guided Practice

### Manual Chapter Title

**Teaching New Material Clearly**

### Module Purpose

Teachers should understand how explicit instruction, modelling, worked examples, questioning, and guided practice help students acquire new knowledge without overloading working memory.

### Big Idea

When students are novices, clear instruction and guided practice are not opposed to deep learning. They are often the route into it.

### Key Concepts

- explicit instruction
- direct instruction
- worked examples
- modelling
- think-alouds
- guided practice
- scaffolding
- fading
- high success rate
- independent practice

### Source Alignment

- Rosenshine: small steps, models, guided practice, high success rate, scaffolds
- Math Academy: direct instruction plus active practice; unguided discovery is inefficient for novices
- Wiliam: evidence from students should guide pacing and adaptation

### Core Content

Teachers should learn:

- Novices need examples, models, and guided practice.
- Worked examples reduce unnecessary search.
- Initial practice should be supported.
- Practice should become more independent as competence grows.
- High success rate matters because repeated failure creates overload and discouragement.
- The goal is not permanent teacher dependence; the goal is successful independence.

### Classroom Translation

A strong sequence for new material:

1. Activate prerequisite knowledge.
2. State the learning goal.
3. Present a small step.
4. Model with a worked example.
5. Ask questions during the model.
6. Guide students through a similar example.
7. Check all students' understanding.
8. Provide another example or non-example.
9. Fade support.
10. Move into independent practice only when students are ready.

### Common Mistakes

- explaining for too long before students do anything
- giving independent work before enough guided practice
- assuming modelling means simply showing the answer
- asking students to discover procedures too early
- failing to fade support
- using group work when individual practice is needed
- moving on despite low success rate

### LMS Assessment Ideas

Show lesson segments and ask:

- Where should the teacher model?
- Where should the teacher ask a question?
- Where is guided practice needed?
- What scaffold would help?
- When should the scaffold be removed?

### Teacher Application Task

Teachers redesign one upcoming explanation using:

- prerequisite check
- worked example
- guided practice item
- all-student check
- independent practice item

### Manual Reflection Prompts

- Where do I expect students to infer too much?
- Which topics need more modelling?
- Which scaffolds do I use well?
- Which scaffolds do I leave in place too long?

## 12. Module 6: Retrieval, Spacing, and Interleaving

### Manual Chapter Title

**Making Learning Stick**

### Module Purpose

Teachers should understand why retrieval, spacing, and interleaving improve durable learning and how to build them into normal teaching.

### Big Idea

Students remember more when they repeatedly retrieve learning over time, especially when practice is spaced and mixed.

### Key Concepts

- retrieval practice
- testing effect
- spacing effect
- spaced repetition
- distributed practice
- interleaving
- mixed practice
- blocking
- overlearning
- desirable difficulty
- forgetting curve

### Source Alignment

- Math Academy: spaced repetition, interleaving, testing effect
- Wiliam: evidence of learning should guide teaching; performance can mislead
- Rosenshine: daily review, weekly review, monthly review

### Core Content

Teachers should learn:

- Retrieval strengthens memory more than re-reading.
- Spacing improves long-term retention.
- Interleaving helps students choose the right method and avoid robotic performance.
- Blocking is useful at the beginning of learning but weak as a long-term review strategy.
- Desirable difficulties can reduce immediate fluency while improving later performance.
- Students may dislike or undervalue effective practice because it feels harder.

### Classroom Translation

Routines teachers can use:

- daily 5-minute retrieval starter
- low-stakes quizzes
- cumulative homework
- weekly mixed review
- monthly review tasks
- exit tickets that revisit earlier learning
- "last week, last month, last term" questions
- practice sets with a small number of new problems plus older review
- no-notes retrieval before checking notes

### Designing Retrieval Practice

Good retrieval practice:

- is low stakes
- requires recall, not recognition only
- includes feedback
- is repeated over time
- includes older material
- is short enough to be sustainable
- helps the teacher see what has been retained

### Designing Interleaving

Good interleaving:

- comes after students have initial understanding
- mixes problem types that students need to distinguish
- includes enough variety to require decision-making
- avoids overwhelming novices too early
- is used especially in review and homework

### Common Mistakes

- using retrieval only as assessment, not learning
- making every quiz high stakes
- reviewing only right before a test
- giving long blocked worksheets after students already show initial mastery
- assuming students will review effectively on their own
- allowing students to look at notes too early during retrieval

### LMS Assessment Ideas

Teachers compare study plans:

- Plan A: re-read notes for 30 minutes
- Plan B: retrieve key ideas, check, correct, repeat later

Teachers identify which practice schedules are spaced or massed, blocked or interleaved.

### Teacher Application Task

Teachers create a two-week retrieval and spacing plan for one topic.

Submission should include:

- target knowledge
- initial teaching date
- retrieval opportunities
- spacing intervals
- feedback method
- how results will affect teaching

### Manual Reflection Prompts

- What do students in my class forget most often?
- How much review is built into my normal teaching?
- Where can I replace re-reading or re-teaching with retrieval?
- Which topics need interleaved review?

## 13. Module 7: Automaticity, Fluency, and Higher-Order Thinking

### Manual Chapter Title

**Why Fluency Frees Thinking**

### Module Purpose

Teachers should understand that automaticity is not the enemy of creativity or deep thinking. It often enables them.

### Big Idea

Students need some knowledge and skills to become automatic so working memory is available for reasoning, problem-solving, creativity, and transfer.

### Key Concepts

- automaticity
- fluency
- foundational knowledge
- working memory
- cognitive load
- expertise
- prerequisite fluency
- practice to mastery

### Source Alignment

- Math Academy: automaticity frees working memory and enables higher-order thinking
- Rosenshine: daily review, overlearning where necessary, independent practice
- Wiliam: evidence should reveal whether students can use learning independently

### Core Content

Teachers should learn:

- If basic skills consume too much attention, students cannot handle complex tasks.
- Automaticity allows students to coordinate multiple ideas.
- Fluency is not mindless if it supports meaningful thinking.
- Repetition is useful when it builds reliable access, but wasteful if it becomes endless blocked overpractice.
- Automaticity develops over time through retrieval, spaced practice, feedback, and application.

### Classroom Translation

Subject examples:

- Reading: decoding must become fluent for comprehension.
- Writing: sentence control and grammar free attention for argument and style.
- Mathematics: number facts and procedures free attention for problem-solving.
- Science: core vocabulary and representations support explanation and reasoning.
- History: secure chronology and concepts support interpretation.
- Languages: vocabulary and grammar patterns support communication.

### Common Mistakes

- treating fluency as low status
- assuming all practice is rote
- assuming creativity appears without knowledge
- moving to complex tasks before basics are secure
- drilling without feedback or purpose
- confusing speed with understanding

### LMS Assessment Ideas

Teachers identify whether a student's struggle is caused by:

- missing concept
- missing prerequisite
- lack of automaticity
- cognitive overload
- weak motivation
- unclear instructions

### Teacher Application Task

Teachers identify one foundational skill or knowledge set in their subject that needs greater automaticity.

They design:

- short practice routine
- retrieval schedule
- success criteria
- feedback method
- plan to avoid boredom and overpractice

### Manual Reflection Prompts

- What must students know automatically in my subject?
- Where do weak foundations masquerade as low ability?
- How can I build fluency without reducing learning to drill?

## 14. Module 8: Formative Assessment and Responsive Teaching

### Manual Chapter Title

**Using Evidence to Adapt Teaching**

### Module Purpose

Teachers should understand formative assessment as a decision-making process, not as a type of test or a set of tricks.

### Big Idea

Assessment is formative when evidence of learning is used to adapt teaching and learning.

### Key Concepts

- formative assessment
- responsive teaching
- evidence of learning
- learning intentions
- success criteria
- Wiliam's five key strategies
- teacher, peer, learner roles
- short-cycle formative assessment

### Source Alignment

- Wiliam: core module
- Rosenshine: checking understanding, questioning, guided practice
- Math Academy: mastery evidence, targeted remediation, learning analytics as a technology analogue

### Core Content

Wiliam's five strategies:

1. Clarifying, sharing, and understanding learning intentions and success criteria.
2. Engineering effective discussions, tasks, and activities that elicit evidence of learning.
3. Providing feedback that moves learners forward.
4. Activating students as learning resources for one another.
5. Activating students as owners of their own learning.

The three formative questions:

- Where is the learner going?
- Where is the learner now?
- How does the learner get there?

### Classroom Translation

Teachers should plan:

- what evidence they need
- when they will collect it
- how they will collect it from all students
- how they will interpret it
- what they will do if students are ready
- what they will do if students are not ready

### Common Mistakes

- collecting assessment data but not using it
- using formative assessment only at the end of a lesson
- confusing grades with feedback
- asking only volunteers
- writing learning intentions that students do not understand
- sharing success criteria without examples
- using peer assessment without training students

### LMS Assessment Ideas

Teachers map classroom practices to Wiliam's five strategies.

Example:

- mini-whiteboards: eliciting evidence
- exemplar comparison: clarifying success
- redrafting after comments: feedback that moves learning forward
- peer critique using criteria: students as resources
- reflection checklist: students as owners

### Teacher Application Task

Teachers plan one lesson using a formative assessment decision point.

Submission:

- learning intention
- success criteria
- evidence collection method
- expected responses
- teacher decision if most students understand
- teacher decision if many students do not understand

### Manual Reflection Prompts

- What evidence do I usually collect during a lesson?
- How often does that evidence change what I do next?
- Which of the five strategies is strongest in my classroom?
- Which is weakest?

## 15. Module 9: Feedback That Moves Learning Forward

### Manual Chapter Title

**Feedback Students Actually Use**

### Module Purpose

Teachers should understand why feedback can help or harm learning and how to design feedback routines that cause student thinking and action.

### Big Idea

Feedback is only powerful when students understand it, think about it, and use it to improve.

### Key Concepts

- task-focused feedback
- process-focused feedback
- self/ego feedback
- grades vs comments
- feedback that causes thinking
- feedback action time
- success criteria
- redrafting
- improvement routines

### Source Alignment

- Wiliam: feedback should cause thinking; assessment formative only if used
- Rosenshine: feedback during guided practice; checking understanding
- Math Academy: immediate practice feedback, targeted remediation, keeping the bar high while adding support

### Core Content

Teachers should learn:

- More feedback is not automatically better.
- Feedback can reduce performance if it shifts attention to ego, comparison, or helplessness.
- Grades can distract from comments.
- Comments should be actionable.
- Feedback should not do all the thinking for the student.
- Feedback needs time built in for response.
- Feedback should connect to criteria and next steps.

### Classroom Translation

Effective feedback routines:

- comment-only marking for selected tasks
- whole-class feedback on common errors
- coded feedback requiring student correction
- feedback questions rather than corrections
- redraft time in class
- "find and fix" tasks
- exemplar comparison before revision
- peer feedback using criteria
- feedback logs

### Common Mistakes

- writing too much feedback
- giving feedback too late
- giving feedback when there is no opportunity to improve
- correcting everything
- praising ability instead of strategy, effort, or improvement
- giving grades and expecting students to read comments deeply
- giving vague comments such as "add more detail"

### LMS Assessment Ideas

Teachers evaluate sample feedback:

- Is it evaluative or actionable?
- Does it focus on task, process, or self?
- Does it cause thinking?
- Can the student act on it?
- Is there time to use it?

### Teacher Application Task

Teachers redesign one feedback routine.

Submission:

- original feedback practice
- revised feedback practice
- how students will act on feedback
- how the teacher will know whether feedback was used

### Manual Reflection Prompts

- How much of my feedback do students actually use?
- Where am I doing thinking students should do?
- How can I reduce marking load while increasing student action?

## 16. Module 10: Self-Regulated Learning and Metacognition

### Manual Chapter Title

**Helping Students Own Their Learning**

### Module Purpose

Teachers should understand self-regulated learning as the long-term goal of formative assessment and learn how to build it explicitly.

### Big Idea

Students become better learners when they can judge quality, monitor their understanding, choose strategies, and take action.

### Key Concepts

- metacognition
- self-regulation
- planning
- monitoring
- evaluating
- self-assessment
- criteria
- exemplars
- attribution
- growth mindset
- motivation

### Source Alignment

- Wiliam: students as owners of their own learning
- Math Academy: students misjudge learning; effortful practice; retrieval and mastery
- Rosenshine: independent practice, review, scaffolds gradually removed

### Core Content

Teachers should learn:

- Self-assessment is not simply asking students to rate themselves.
- Students need a concept of quality.
- Students need criteria and exemplars.
- Students need practice monitoring their own understanding.
- Students need strategies for what to do when stuck.
- Students need to attribute success and failure to controllable, changeable causes.
- Growth mindset must be connected to better strategies and better practice, not empty encouragement.

### Classroom Translation

Useful routines:

- compare work to exemplars
- identify strengths and next steps
- error analysis
- checklist before submission
- reflection after retrieval quiz
- learning logs
- traffic lights with required follow-up action
- "what will I do next?" prompts
- student-generated questions
- correction plans

### Common Mistakes

- asking students to self-assess without criteria
- using traffic lights but not acting on the results
- equating confidence with understanding
- praising effort without improving strategy
- leaving students to regulate learning before they know how
- assuming metacognition develops automatically

### LMS Assessment Ideas

Teachers identify strong and weak self-regulation routines.

Example:

Weak: "Circle red, yellow, or green."

Stronger: "Circle red, yellow, or green, then choose one action: rewatch model, attempt another example, ask peer to explain, write one question, or start extension."

### Teacher Application Task

Teachers create one self-regulation routine connected to a real task.

Submission:

- task
- criteria or exemplar
- student self-monitoring prompt
- action options
- teacher follow-up

### Manual Reflection Prompts

- Do my students know what quality looks like?
- Do they know what to do when they are stuck?
- Do they mistake familiarity for mastery?
- How can I teach them to monitor learning more accurately?

## 17. Optional Module 11: Mastery Learning and Targeted Remediation

### Manual Chapter Title

**Moving Students Forward From Secure Foundations**

### Module Purpose

Teachers should understand mastery learning and targeted remediation as a way to prevent students from accumulating gaps.

### Big Idea

Students should not be asked to build advanced knowledge on insecure prerequisites.

### Key Concepts

- mastery learning
- prerequisite knowledge
- knowledge frontier
- zone of proximal development
- targeted remediation
- corrective remediation
- preventative remediation
- foundational gaps
- high standards with increased support

### Source Alignment

- Math Academy: mastery learning, knowledge graph, targeted remediation
- Wiliam: establishing where learners are and adapting instruction
- Rosenshine: high success rate, guided practice, checking understanding

### Core Content

Teachers should learn:

- Traditional pacing often moves students forward before prerequisites are secure.
- Missing foundations increase cognitive load and reduce motivation.
- Mastery learning keeps the standard high but gives students enough practice and support to reach it.
- Targeted remediation should address the specific missing knowledge causing the struggle.
- Technology can automate some of this, but teachers can approximate it through better diagnosis and grouping.

### Classroom Translation

Teacher approximations:

- prerequisite checks before new units
- small targeted review groups
- correction tasks based on error patterns
- flexible practice sets
- "must know before this lesson" lists
- retake after corrective practice
- intervention on specific components rather than broad reteaching

### Common Mistakes

- lowering the standard instead of increasing support
- reteaching the whole lesson when one prerequisite is missing
- using generic remediation
- moving on because the calendar says so
- treating all low performance as lack of effort
- ignoring advanced students who are ready to move faster

### LMS Assessment Ideas

Teachers analyze student errors and identify likely missing prerequisites.

### Teacher Application Task

Teachers choose one upcoming topic and list:

- required prerequisites
- how they will check them
- what they will do for students missing each prerequisite
- how students who are ready can move deeper or faster

### Manual Chapter Title

**How Teachers Keep Improving**

### Module Purpose

Teachers and leaders should understand how to convert the course into sustained practice change.

### Big Idea

Teacher learning works best when teachers choose small changes, try them, gather evidence, discuss results, and repeat the cycle with peer support.

### Key Concepts

- teacher learning communities
- deliberate practice for teachers
- action planning
- peer accountability
- tight but loose implementation
- habit change
- evidence cycles
- professional trust

### Source Alignment

- Wiliam: Teacher Learning Communities, knowing-doing gap, tight but loose
- Math Academy: deliberate practice, accountability for learning
- Rosenshine: practices that can become shared instructional routines

### Core Content

Teachers should learn:

- One-off workshops rarely change practice.
- Teachers need choice and ownership.
- Changes should be small enough to try.
- Peer accountability increases follow-through.
- Evidence should be about student learning, not teacher compliance.
- Implementation should be tight on principles and loose on techniques.

### Suggested Teacher Learning Community Structure

Recommended meeting length: 75-90 minutes.

Agenda:

1. Opening and purpose: 5 minutes
2. Brief learning recap: 10 minutes
3. Teacher implementation reports: 25-35 minutes
4. Problem-solving discussion: 15-20 minutes
5. New technique or example: 10-15 minutes
6. Individual action planning: 10 minutes
7. Commitments and close: 5 minutes

### Action Plan Template

```text
My focus routine:

What I tried:

What happened:

Evidence from students:

What I learned:

What I will adjust:

My next small step:

When I will try it:

What evidence I will bring next time:
```

### Common Mistakes

- turning TLCs into administrative meetings
- discussing ideas without commitments
- requiring everyone to use the same technique
- evaluating teachers instead of supporting learning
- skipping evidence
- trying too many changes at once

### Manual Chapter Title

**How To Judge Educational Claims**

### Module Purpose

Teachers should become better consumers of educational research and avoid fads.

### Big Idea

Research does not remove judgment. It helps teachers make better trade-offs.

### Key Concepts

- evidence-informed practice
- effect sizes
- implementation quality
- context
- trade-offs
- opportunity cost
- what works vs what worked
- sensitivity to outcome measures
- publication bias

### Source Alignment

- Wiliam: educators as critical consumers of research
- Math Academy: evidence-based learning strategies
- Rosenshine: convergence across cognitive science, master teacher studies, and cognitive supports

### Five Questions For Research

Use Wiliam's practical questions:

1. Does this solve a problem we have?
2. If we do this, how much faster or better will students learn?

- What evidence would show it is working?
- What could get worse?
- What support will teachers need?

## 28. Tone Guidelines For The Manual

The manual should be:

- practical
- respectful
- evidence-informed
- direct
- classroom-centered
- non-faddish
- clear about trade-offs

- implying teachers are lazy or ignorant
- making research sound simpler than it is
- presenting one technique as universally effective
