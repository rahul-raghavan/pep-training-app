# Learning Science — Full Program Structure

## Audience
Elementary and middle school teachers across all PEP campuses (Montessori environment).

## Context
- Mixed-stage classrooms — students grouped by ability, not age. Never reference "Class 3" or "Class 6" in content.
- Small groups of 8–10 students per teacher.
- No formal grades or exams. Some marking exists, but the culture is non-graded.
- Collaborative, independent work is central to the Montessori approach.
- EI ASSET assessments are the annual external diagnostic tool teachers are familiar with.

## Tone
General learning science (not PEP-specific framing), but every concept must land with a real, practical classroom example that feels like a Montessori classroom — small groups, mixed stages, collaborative work, teacher as guide. No abstract theory without a "here's what this looks like on Monday morning" moment. Simple language. Application-oriented. Inspirational without being cloying.

## Voice Exercise Design Principles
- Default format: **rank or take a stand**. Present 2–3 teachers/approaches/situations and ask "which produces the most learning?" rather than open-ended "explain this concept."
- Scenarios must reflect Montessori reality: small groups, mixed-stage, no exams, collaborative environment.
- When an open-ended prompt is used, it should require the teacher to describe a specific change to their practice, not just recite theory.

## Standard Course Dimensions (based on existing courses)

| Element | Per section | Per course |
|---------|-----------|------------|
| Content blocks | 4–7 (text, callout, key_point, etc.) | 40–70 total |
| MCQ exercises | 3 | ~27 across 9 sections |
| Voice exercises | 1 | 9 (one per section) |
| End-of-course assessment | — | 12–15 scenario-based MCQs |
| Sections | — | 8–10 |

**Voice exercise format:** Each section ends with a voice exercise. It has:
- `question` — short title
- `scenario` — a realistic situation the teacher must respond to verbally (prefer rank/compare format)
- `guidance` — what a good answer should include (shown to the teacher as a rubric)
- `ai_prompt` — evaluation instructions for Claude (not shown to teacher)

### Flow Rules
- **LS 101** is the prerequisite for everything.
- **LS 201 and LS 202** can be taken in either order or in parallel after 101.
- **LS 301** requires both 201 and 202 (feedback only makes sense when you understand how memory works AND how to gather evidence of learning).
- **LS 401 and LS 402** can be taken in either order after 301. LS 402 is for team leads and coordinators only.

## Module Summaries

### LS 101: Learning Science — The Big Picture
**Goal:** Cover the full landscape in one course. Every teacher walks away knowing *what* learning science says, *why* it matters, and *where* they'll go deeper. Sets the stage for 201–402.

**Sections:**

| # | Section | What it covers | Key sources |
|---|---------|---------------|-------------|
| 1 | **Why This Matters** | Teacher quality is the #1 lever for student outcomes. But knowing something doesn't mean doing it — that's the knowing-doing gap. Why structured professional learning works better than tips and tricks. | Wiliam: Teacher Expertise Is the Improvement Lever; Research-Informed Teaching |
| 2 | **What Is Learning, Really?** | Learning = durable change in long-term memory. Performance in the moment ≠ learning for the long run. The fluency trap: when students look like they've got it but haven't. Example: a student works through fractions confidently but can't do them three weeks later. | Wiliam: Learning Memory and Retrieval; Designing for Durable Learning |
| 3 | **The Bottleneck: Working Memory** | Students can only hold 3–5 new things at once. That's not a flaw — it's how brains work. Why small steps, modelling, and worked examples aren't "spoon-feeding" — they're managing cognitive load so real learning can happen. | Wiliam: Cognitive Load Theory in Practice; Rosenshine's Principles (small steps, modelling) |
| 4 | **Making It Stick** | Retrieval practice (pulling from memory), spacing (spreading practice over time), and interleaving (mixing topics). Why re-reading and highlighting feel productive but don't work. What revisiting material across weeks actually does for the brain. | Wiliam: Learning Memory and Retrieval; Designing for Durable Learning; Math Academy Way (retrieval, spacing, interleaving) |
| 5 | **Student Ownership & Metacognition** | Students who monitor their own learning learn more. But self-regulation isn't magic — it has to be taught. Self-assessment, peer learning, and what "clarity about quality" means in practice. In a Montessori environment where independence is the goal, metacognition is what makes independence *effective*. | Wiliam: Metacognition and Self-Regulation; Collaborative Learning and Peer Thinking |
| 6 | **Collaboration: When It Works and When It Doesn't** | Sitting together ≠ learning together. Research on when collaboration produces learning and when it produces disguised inactivity. Individual accountability, structured protocols, and why pairs before groups. Directly relevant to our Montessori classrooms where collaborative work is central. | Wiliam: Collaborative Learning and Peer Thinking |
| 7 | **Formative Assessment: Teaching as Responsive** | The 5 key strategies. "Build plan B into plan A." Teaching isn't delivering content — it's making decisions based on what students actually understand right now. Diagnostic questions as daily tools (not just EI ASSET once a year). | Wiliam: Why Formative Assessment Matters; Formative Assessment Full Course |
| 8 | **Feedback That Actually Works** | Most feedback fails — or doesn't happen at all. In our no-grades environment, feedback matters *more*, not less. Good feedback is work *for the learner*, not commentary *from the teacher*. The three-approach comparison: tick and "good work" vs detailed comments vs detective task. | Wiliam: Feedback That Moves Learning Forward |
| 9 | **Rosenshine's Principles: The Montessori Bridge** | 10 research-backed teaching principles that map onto what we already do — and reveal where we can improve. Daily review = retrieval. Small steps = cognitive load. Questioning = formative assessment. Independent practice = building automaticity. Framed not as "traditional teaching" but as the science behind why Montessori's best practices work — and where we sometimes skip steps. | Rosenshine's Principles of Instruction |

**Section-level detail (exercises per section):**

#### Section 1: Why This Matters
- 3 MCQs on: the research case for teacher learning as the #1 lever, knowing-doing gap, why one-off PD fails
- Voice: *"Two teachers discuss professional development. Teacher A says: 'I've been teaching for 15 years — I learn more from experience than any workshop.' Teacher B says: 'I've been teaching for 15 years too, but I've realised experience alone doesn't make me better — structured learning with colleagues does.' Which teacher's view is better supported by research, and why?"* — should show empathy for Teacher A's position, then make the research case for Teacher B using the knowing-doing gap and the limits of unstructured experience.

#### Section 2: What Is Learning, Really?
- 3 MCQs on: learning vs performance, fluency illusion, long-term memory as the goal
- Voice: *"A parent says: 'My child was doing really well with the material in class, but when you revisited it a few weeks later, she couldn't do it anymore. What's going on?' Explain what's happening without blaming the student or alarming the parent."* — should explain the performance-learning gap in accessible language, normalise it as how all brains work, and describe what the school does to address it (revisiting, spacing, retrieval).

#### Section 3: The Bottleneck — Working Memory
- 3 MCQs on: working memory limits, cognitive load (intrinsic vs extraneous), why modelling and small steps help
- Voice: *"Three teachers are each introducing a new concept. Teacher A covers three related ideas in one lesson because they're connected. Teacher B covers one idea thoroughly and saves the other two for tomorrow. Teacher C covers all three but gives students a summary sheet to refer to. Rank the three approaches for a group of students who are new to this topic — which produces the most durable learning?"* — should identify Teacher B as strongest for novices, explain why Teacher A overloads working memory, and why Teacher C's summary sheet doesn't solve the problem (extraneous load from juggling three new ideas persists).

#### Section 4: Making It Stick
- 3 MCQs on: retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, why re-reading/highlighting fail
- Voice: *"You notice a student preparing for a presentation by reading through her notes over and over. She feels confident. Based on what you've learned, what would you suggest she do differently, and how would you explain to her why your suggestion works better — even though it feels harder?"* — should recommend retrieval practice (close notes, try to recall), explain the fluency trap, and acknowledge that the harder strategy is the effective one.

#### Section 5: Student Ownership & Metacognition
- 3 MCQs on: self-regulation, calibration, why metacognition must be taught within subject work
- Voice: *"A student says: 'I practised a lot but I still couldn't do it.' You ask how she practised and she says she re-read her notes and copied the key points. What questions would you ask her next, and what would you help her change?"* — should probe for strategy awareness, introduce the three metacognitive questions (planning, monitoring, evaluating), and explain that effort without the right strategy doesn't produce learning.

#### Section 6: Collaboration — When It Works
- 3 MCQs on: social loafing, individual accountability, when collaboration helps vs hurts, structured protocols
- Voice: *"You observe a group of four students working on a research task. One student is doing all the writing while the others watch. The students seem fine with this arrangement. What's the problem, and what would you change about the task design?"* — should identify the lack of individual accountability, explain that the non-writing students aren't doing cognitive work, and propose a structural change (e.g., individual accountability check, think-pair-share, everyone explains).

#### Section 7: Formative Assessment — Teaching as Responsive
- 3 MCQs on: the 5 strategies, formative vs summative, diagnostic questions, "build plan B into plan A"
- Voice: *"Two teachers plan a lesson on the same topic. Teacher A follows her plan smoothly and covers everything. Teacher B builds in two 'check points' where she asks a diagnostic question, discovers confusion, and has to skip her last activity to re-teach. Which teacher's students are more likely to remember the material a week later, and why?"* — should identify Teacher B and explain that responsive teaching beats smooth coverage, connecting to "students do not always learn what we teach."

#### Section 8: Feedback That Actually Works
- 3 MCQs on: why most feedback fails, feedback as work for the learner, the detective principle, why feedback matters more without grades
- Voice: *"Three teachers respond to the same piece of student work. Teacher A puts a tick and writes 'Good work.' Teacher B writes detailed comments on every paragraph. Teacher C writes: 'Your opening is strong. One of your paragraphs makes a claim without evidence — find it and fix it. Bring it to me by Thursday.' Rank the three approaches — which produces the most improvement in the student's next piece of work?"* — should rank Teacher C first (task-based, specific, actionable), Teacher B second (well-intentioned but overwhelming), Teacher A last (no information, no action), and explain that in a no-grades environment, feedback is the primary driver of improvement.

#### Section 9: Rosenshine's Principles — The Montessori Bridge
- 3 MCQs on: mapping principles to learning science, connecting principles to Montessori practice, identifying where Montessori naturally aligns and where gaps exist
- Voice: *"A colleague who teaches at a traditional school says: 'Rosenshine's Principles are about direct instruction — that's the opposite of Montessori.' How would you respond? Where do the principles align with what we do, and where might we have gaps?"* — should show that Rosenshine's principles are about how learning works, not about a teaching style. Should map at least 2–3 principles to Montessori practice (e.g., guided practice = the three-period lesson, independent practice = work cycles) and honestly identify where we sometimes skip steps (e.g., daily review/retrieval, explicit modelling before independence).

**End-of-course assessment:** 12–15 scenario-based MCQs spanning all sections. Scenarios should reflect Montessori classrooms: small groups, mixed stages, no grades, collaborative work. Example: "A student works through a set of problems confidently during the lesson but can't solve similar problems three weeks later. Which concept best explains this?" (Answer: performance ≠ learning / fluency illusion.)

### LS 201: How Learning Works
**Goal:** Deep dive into the cognitive science of learning. Teachers understand *how* students form, store, and retrieve knowledge — and what that means for lesson design.

**Key sources:** Wiliam (Cognitive Load, Learning Memory, Designing for Durable Learning), Math Academy Way, Rosenshine

**Sections (9):**

| # | Section | Key topics |
|---|---------|------------|
| 1 | How Experts Think Differently | Schemas, chunking, organised knowledge. Why a chess master sees patterns, not pieces — and what that means for building student expertise. In mixed-stage classrooms, students at different levels literally have different mental architectures. |
| 2 | Working Memory: The Bottleneck | Intrinsic vs extraneous load. Concrete design implications: split attention, redundancy, modality effects. How to tell whether struggle is productive or just overload. |
| 3 | The Expertise Reversal Effect | What helps novices hurts experts — and vice versa. Critical for mixed-stage classrooms: the same lesson structure can't serve everyone. Why scaffolding must be gradually removed. Fading worked examples. |
| 4 | Retrieval Practice | Pulling from memory is learning, not just checking. The testing effect — without exams. Low-stakes retrieval: brain dumps, retrieval grids, "close your notebook and write what you remember." |
| 5 | Spacing and Interleaving | Distributed practice vs massed practice. Why mixed work cycles have a built-in advantage. Scheduling retrieval across a term, not just within a unit. |
| 6 | Automaticity and Fluency | Why some things must become automatic (reading, number facts). Frees working memory for higher-order thinking. Deliberate practice vs mere repetition. |
| 7 | Learning vs Performance | The core distinction. Why smooth lessons can produce no learning. Desirable difficulties. When struggle helps and when it's just frustration. |
| 8 | Putting It Together: Lesson Design | The two-phase model: first help students understand (manage load), then make it stick (retrieval, spacing). How this maps to the Montessori lesson → practice → independent work arc. |
| 9 | Auditing Your Own Practice | Reflection: which of your current routines align with learning science? Which don't? Where does our Montessori practice naturally support durable learning, and where do we have blind spots? One concrete change to make this week. |

Each section: 3 MCQs + 1 voice exercise (rank/compare format). 12–15 end-of-course assessment MCQs.

### LS 202: Formative Assessment
**Goal:** Deep dive into Wiliam's 5-strategy framework. Teachers leave with a toolkit of classroom techniques, not just theory. Framed around small-group Montessori teaching, not traditional whole-class instruction.

**Key sources:** Wiliam (Learning Intentions, Responsive Teaching, FA Full Course, EFA Plus, Eliciting Evidence, Better Questioning)

**Anchor:** EI ASSET assessments as the familiar reference point — "you already see diagnostic questions once a year; this course is about using the same principle every lesson."

**Sections (9):**

| # | Section | Key topics |
|---|---------|------------|
| 1 | What Formative Assessment Actually Is | Not a type of test. Evidence used to improve teaching and learning in real time. The 5-strategy framework overview. Why it matters even — especially — in small groups where you think you can "read the room." |
| 2 | Clarity: Learning Intentions & Success Criteria | Task vs learning intention vs success criteria. Writing criteria students can actually use. Exemplars that clarify, not confuse. What "the student knows what they're working towards" looks like in practice. |
| 3 | Checking Every Student's Thinking | Moving beyond "Does everyone understand?" Even with 8 students, nodding isn't understanding. Quick written responses, show-me techniques, turn-and-explain. Seeing all thinking, not just the confident students'. |
| 4 | Diagnostic Questions | Designing questions where wrong answers reveal misconceptions — not random errors. Distractor-driven questions. Why open questions sometimes tell you less. Connection to EI ASSET: the same principle, used daily instead of annually. |
| 5 | Diagnostic Questions as Decision Points | Questions placed where the lesson could branch. Every student responds. Teacher interprets in 30 seconds. Changes what happens next. The "collect evidence to decide, not to admire" principle. |
| 6 | Responsive Teaching in Action | Planning lessons with built-in contingencies. "Plan B inside plan A." Real examples of branching lesson plans. Why the "messy" lesson often produces more learning than the smooth one. |
| 7 | Activating Students as Resources for Each Other | Peer explanation, peer assessment, structured collaboration. How to make it work (structure, accountability) and when it backfires (no structure, unequal loading). |
| 8 | Activating Students as Owners of Their Learning | Self-assessment, reflection routines. Building the habit of "Do I actually understand this?" Connecting to metacognition. |
| 9 | Making It Routine | Choosing 2–3 techniques to embed this term. Planning your first formative assessment routine. Avoiding the "technique of the week" trap. One change, practised until it's automatic, then add another. |

Each section: 3 MCQs + 1 voice exercise (rank/compare format). Use "diagnostic question" throughout, never "hinge question." 12–15 end-of-course assessment MCQs.

### LS 301: Feedback & Student Ownership
**Goal:** The hardest module. How to give feedback that actually changes learning, and how to build students who own their learning process. Directly addresses the "no grades, so no feedback" culture.

**Prerequisites:** LS 201 + LS 202 (because feedback depends on understanding memory AND on having evidence systems in place)

**Key sources:** Wiliam (Feedback That Moves Learning Forward, Feedback Revisited, Metacognition, EFA Feedback and Self-Regulation, Collaborative Learning)

**Sections (9):**

| # | Section | Key topics |
|---|---------|------------|
| 1 | **Why Feedback Matters More Without Grades** | In a graded system, grades (poorly) do some of the work of signalling quality. In a no-grades environment, feedback is the *only* mechanism that helps students understand where they are and what to do next. Without it, students have no mirror. The research case: no grades doesn't mean no feedback — it means feedback carries *all* the weight. |
| 2 | Why Most Feedback Fails | Even when feedback is given, it often fails. Timing, specificity, learner readiness. The Butler study: comments only > grades only > grades + comments. What this means for us: our advantage is that we don't have grades getting in the way — but only if we actually give actionable feedback. |
| 3 | Feedback as Work for the Learner | The "detective, not the told" principle. Designing feedback tasks where students locate, diagnose, and fix. Three teachers respond to the same work — comparison exercise. |
| 4 | Wise Feedback | High standards + belief the student can meet them. The research on trust, identity, and feedback. Why "Good work, keep it up" can be more damaging than honest criticism. The three-approach comparison: vague praise vs blunt criticism vs wise feedback. |
| 5 | When Not to Give Feedback | Feedback isn't always the answer. When practice, re-teaching, or restructuring the task is better. Matching the response to the diagnosis. Avoiding feedback overload. |
| 6 | Metacognition: What It Is and Why It Matters | Planning, monitoring, evaluating. Sadler's framework: know quality, monitor performance, choose action. Why metacognition isn't a separate "thinking skills" lesson. Must be taught within subject work. |
| 7 | Teaching Self-Regulation | Building the habit of "Do I actually understand this?" Self-assessment routines. Calibration exercises (predict → check → compare). The role of motivation and belief. Gradual transfer of responsibility from teacher to student. |
| 8 | Peer Assessment as a Bridge | Peer feedback teaches self-assessment. Structures that work: two stars and a wish, ladder of feedback. When peer work helps and when it harms. Start with pairs, not groups. |
| 9 | Redesigning Your Feedback Practice | Take one piece of student work from this week. Redesign how you'd respond using what you've learned. Compare: what would you have done before? What will you do now? Practise the wise feedback frame. |

Each section: 3 MCQs + 1 voice exercise (three-teacher comparison format where possible). 12–15 end-of-course assessment MCQs.

### LS 401: Designing for Durable Learning
**Goal:** Applied module. Teachers redesign actual units and lessons using everything from 101–301. This is where theory becomes practice in the specific context of Montessori classrooms.

**Key sources:** Math Academy Way (mastery, spacing, interleaving), Wiliam (Designing for Durable Learning), Rosenshine, Blueprint

**Sections (8):**

| # | Section | Key topics |
|---|---------|------------|
| 1 | The Two-Phase Design Model | First help students understand (manage cognitive load), then make it stick (retrieval + spacing). How this maps to the Montessori lesson → guided practice → independent work cycle. |
| 2 | Designing Retrieval Routines | Retrieval starters, brain dumps, retrieval grids. Making retrieval a daily habit, not an event. How to build retrieval into the start of a work cycle without turning it into a quiz. |
| 3 | Spacing Across a Term | Curriculum-level planning: when to revisit, how to schedule cumulative practice. Why "cover and move on" fails. Spacing calendars for mixed-stage groups. |
| 4 | Interleaving in Practice | Mixing problem types within independent work. When interleaving helps (discrimination between similar concepts) and when it doesn't (brand-new content). Why Montessori's mixed work cycles have a natural advantage here — if designed intentionally. |
| 5 | The Release Sequence | Modelling → guided practice → independent practice. Fading scaffolds. Knowing when students are ready for independence — and when they're not. In Montessori: the three-period lesson as a release sequence. |
| 6 | Mastery and Targeted Remediation | What mastery learning actually means. Diagnosing gaps in a mixed-stage group. Targeted remediation without holding everyone back. |
| 7 | Designing for Transfer | Near vs far transfer. Why transfer is hard and rare. What you can do: varied practice, comparing examples, explicit connection-making across subjects. |
| 8 | The Learning-Science Lesson Audit | Take a real unit you teach. Audit it against everything in this program. Identify 3 specific changes. Plan implementation. Share with a colleague for accountability. |

Each section: 3 MCQs + 1 voice exercise (rank/compare format). 12–15 end-of-course assessment MCQs.

### LS 402: Leading Teacher Learning (Team Leads Only)
**Goal:** For coordinators, heads of department, and school leaders. How to build a professional learning system that actually changes practice — not just "sit in a workshop and forget." Framed around what PEP is already building: the workshop series, online courses, and peer accountability structures.

**Key sources:** Wiliam (Leading Teacher Learning, Research-Informed Teaching, Turning Research Into Classroom Decisions), Blueprint

**Sections (8):**

| # | Section | Key topics |
|---|---------|------------|
| 1 | Why Most PD Doesn't Work | The knowing-doing gap. Research on what changes teacher practice (structures > inspiration). Why our workshop + online course model is designed differently — and what your role is in making it stick. |
| 2 | Teacher Learning Communities at PEP | The TLC model adapted for our context: small groups of teachers, regular check-ins, shared agenda, peer observation. Not a new initiative — a name for the structure we're building with these workshops, courses, and accountability partners. |
| 3 | Small Steps and Peer Observation | The "tight but loose" principle. Teachers choose one small technique, try it for two weeks, get peer feedback, refine. Why small beats ambitious — the same cognitive load principle that applies to students applies to us. |
| 4 | Using Research Wisely | The 5 adoption questions: Does it solve our problem? How much faster will students learn? What will it cost? Can we implement it? Do we know what to do? How to evaluate new ideas without either blindly adopting or cynically dismissing. |
| 5 | Effect Sizes and Meta-Analyses | What they tell you and what they don't. Why "effect size of 0.7" doesn't mean "definitely do this." Being a critical consumer of education research, not a skeptic and not a zealot. |
| 6 | De-implementation | What to stop doing to make room for what works. The initiative stocktake. HEAT scoring: harm, ease, acceptability, time. Sometimes the most important leadership decision is what to remove. |
| 7 | Tracking Without Surveillance | Logic models, evidence portfolios, teaching sprints. How to know if something is working without making teachers perform for observers. Observation for learning, not evaluation. |
| 8 | Building Your PD Architecture | Design a 1-year professional learning plan for your team using everything in this module. Structure, cadence, accountability, review points. Connect it to the LS course sequence and the TLC model. |

Each section: 3 MCQs + 1 voice exercise (rank/compare format). 12–15 end-of-course assessment MCQs.
