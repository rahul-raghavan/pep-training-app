## Section 1: What Formative Assessment Actually Is
**Slug:** what-formative-assessment-is
**Estimated Minutes:** 15

### Content

# It's Not a Type of Test

Let's start by clearing up a very common misunderstanding. Formative assessment is not a type of test. It's not a quiz you give on Fridays. It's not a worksheet with a score. It's not summative assessment done more frequently.

Many schools have taken the idea of formative assessment and turned it into a ritual — an exit ticket at the end of every lesson, a quiz every two weeks, a "formative test" before the "real test." If the results don't change what happens next in the classroom, none of that is formative assessment. It's just more testing.

The word "formative" doesn't describe the test. It describes what you DO with the information.

> [!info]
> **Key distinction:** An assessment isn't formative or summative by its design — it's formative or summative by its purpose. The same question can be formative or summative depending on whether the teacher uses the results to adjust instruction or just to record a score.

# Dylan Wiliam's Definition

Here's the definition we'll use throughout this course, from Dylan Wiliam — one of the world's leading researchers on this topic:

**Assessment is formative when evidence about student understanding is elicited, interpreted, and used by teachers, learners, or their peers to make decisions about what to do next.**

Read that again carefully. There are three parts:

1. **Elicited** — you actively gather evidence of student thinking. You don't wait for it to appear. You design moments that make thinking visible.
2. **Interpreted** — you make sense of what you see. What does this response tell me about what the student understands and doesn't understand?
3. **Used** — you act on it. You change what happens next in the lesson, the next lesson, or the next interaction with that student.

If any of these three parts is missing, it's not formative assessment. You can gather evidence and never look at it. You can look at it and never act on it. Both of those are common — and both are a waste of time.

# The Five Key Strategies

Wiliam organises formative assessment into five strategies. You met these briefly in LS 101. Now we'll go much deeper — each strategy gets its own section in this course.

| Strategy | What it means |
|----------|--------------|
| 1. Clarifying and sharing learning intentions and success criteria | Students and teacher are clear about what's being learned and what quality looks like |
| 2. Engineering effective discussions, activities, and tasks that elicit evidence | The teacher designs moments that make student thinking visible |
| 3. Providing feedback that moves learning forward | Feedback tells the student what to do next — not just what they got wrong |
| 4. Activating learners as instructional resources for one another | Students help each other learn through structured peer interaction |
| 5. Activating learners as owners of their own learning | Students monitor and regulate their own learning |

Notice that only Strategy 2 is about the teacher gathering information. Strategies 4 and 5 put students in the role of assessors — of each other's work and their own. This is important. Formative assessment isn't just a teacher activity. When it's working well, everyone in the room is involved.

# Why This Matters: The Gap Between Teaching and Learning

Here's the uncomfortable truth that sits underneath all of this: students don't always learn what we teach.

You can deliver a beautiful explanation. You can model the skill perfectly. You can design an engaging activity. And students can still walk away without learning what you intended. Not because you did something wrong — but because learning is not a direct transfer from teacher to student. It's something that happens inside the student's mind, and you can't see it happening.

Formative assessment is the system that closes the gap between teaching and learning. Without it, you're teaching blind. You find out whether students learned only when it's too late to do anything about it — on a test, at the end of a unit, at the end of the year.

With formative assessment, you find out during the lesson, while you can still respond. That's the difference.

> "The big idea of formative assessment is that evidence about student learning is used to adjust instruction to better meet student needs — in other words, teaching is adaptive to the learner's needs."
> — Dylan Wiliam

# Formative vs Summative: Different Questions

The simplest way to understand the difference:

- **Summative assessment** answers: "How much did they learn?"
- **Formative assessment** answers: "What do I do next?"

Summative is backward-looking — it measures what happened. Formative is forward-looking — it shapes what will happen. Both are valuable. But in a school like ours — with small groups, no formal exams, and a focus on actual learning — formative assessment is where the daily power lies.

You don't need test scores to tell you how your students are doing. You need real-time evidence of their thinking so you can respond to it in the moment.

> [!warning]
> **The ritual trap:** Exit tickets, diagnostic quizzes, and check-ins can all be powerful formative tools — but only if you actually use the results to change what happens next. The moment they become a routine you go through without acting on the data, they stop being formative and become paperwork.

# The Task Is Not the Learning Intention

This is one of the most common confusions in teaching, and it causes real problems. Let's clear it up.

The **task** is what students do. The **learning intention** is what students are supposed to learn from doing it. These are not the same thing.

Example: The task is "write a persuasive paragraph." The learning intention is "use evidence to support a claim." A student can complete the task — they can write a persuasive paragraph — without achieving the learning intention. They might write a paragraph full of opinions with no evidence at all. They completed the task. They missed the learning.

Another example: In a Montessori classroom, the task might be "work with the bead frame to do four-digit multiplication." The learning intention is "understand place value in multi-digit multiplication." A student can mechanically move beads and get the right answer without understanding the place value concept at all. Task completed. Learning intention missed.

When we're unclear about this distinction, we assess the wrong thing. We check whether students finished the task instead of whether they learned what the task was designed to teach.

> [!info]
> **The test:** If every student in your group completes the task perfectly but doesn't learn the underlying concept, was the lesson successful? If your answer is "no," then you already understand why learning intentions and tasks are different things.

# What Good Learning Intentions Look Like

A learning intention answers the question: "What will students understand or be able to do as a result of this lesson that they couldn't before?"

Here's what doesn't work:

- "Students will understand fractions" — too vague. Which aspect of fractions? Understand in what way?
- "Students will complete the worksheet" — that's a task, not a learning intention
- "Students will enjoy the science experiment" — that's a hope, not a learning outcome

Here's what works better:

- "Students will be able to compare two fractions with different denominators and explain which is larger"
- "Students will identify the claim in a persuasive text and evaluate whether the evidence supports it"
- "Students will explain why plants need light using the concept of photosynthesis"

Notice the pattern: good learning intentions are specific enough that you could check whether a student achieved them. "Understand fractions" — how would you check that? "Compare fractions with different denominators and explain which is larger" — you can check that in under a minute.

# Success Criteria: What Quality Looks Like

Learning intentions tell you what students should learn. Success criteria tell students what quality looks like — how they'll know if they've done it well.

The key principle: success criteria must be concrete enough that students can actually use them. Abstract language doesn't help.

| Vague (unhelpful) | Concrete (actionable) |
|---|---|
| "Your paragraph is well-written" | "Your paragraph states a position and gives at least one specific piece of evidence from the text" |
| "Show your working" | "Write down each step so someone who wasn't here could follow your thinking" |
| "Good presentation" | "You spoke clearly, made eye contact with at least two people, and explained your main point without reading from notes" |

The left column sounds professional but gives students nothing to work with. The right column tells them exactly what to aim for.

# The Power of Exemplars

Here's something that research consistently shows: showing students examples of strong and weak work is more powerful than describing quality in words.

Students need to SEE what "good" looks like. When you show them two pieces of work — one that meets the success criteria and one that doesn't — and ask them to identify the differences, they develop a much clearer understanding of quality than they get from any verbal description.

This is especially powerful in mixed-stage classrooms. When students are at different levels, exemplars give everyone a concrete reference point. Instead of trying to describe quality in words that some students won't fully understand, you show them. The work speaks for itself.

> [!tip]
> **Practical tip:** Start collecting examples of student work at different quality levels (with names removed). Over time, you'll build a library of exemplars you can use year after year. Even two examples — one strong, one that needs improvement — is enough to anchor a powerful discussion about quality.

# When NOT to Share Learning Intentions

Here's a nuance that surprises many teachers: you don't always need to share the learning intention with students.

Sometimes, in discovery activities, sharing the learning intention gives away the punchline. If you're designing an activity where students are supposed to discover a pattern or arrive at a conclusion through investigation, telling them the conclusion in advance defeats the purpose.

The learning intention always exists in the teacher's mind — you always know what you want students to learn. But it doesn't always need to be posted on the board at the start of the lesson. Sometimes you reveal it at the end: "So what did we discover today? That's what this lesson was really about."

The key question is: will sharing the learning intention help students focus their effort, or will it spoil the learning experience? For most lessons, sharing helps. For discovery and inquiry activities, it sometimes doesn't.

> [!warning]
> **A common mistake:** Some schools require every lesson to begin with the learning intention written on the board. This becomes a ritual — teachers write it, students copy it, nobody really engages with it. A learning intention is only useful if students genuinely understand what they're working toward. Posting it on the board is not the same as making it clear.

# The "Does Everyone Understand?" Problem

You've asked it. Every teacher has. "Does everyone understand?" And every student either nods, stays silent, or mutters "yes."

This question gives you zero useful information. Even in a small group of 8 students, "Does everyone understand?" tells you nothing about what individual students actually think. The students who understand say yes. The students who don't understand also say yes — because they don't want to be the one who holds up the group, or because they think they understand when they don't (remember the fluency trap from LS 101?).

The fundamental problem is this: if you want to know what every student thinks, every student needs to produce a response. Asking the group a question and waiting for volunteers tells you what the confident ones think. It tells you nothing about the quiet ones.

# Why Observation Is Misleading

In small groups, teachers often feel like they can just "see" who understands. "I only have 8 students — I can tell who's getting it."

But can you? Watching students work tells you about activity, not understanding. A student who is writing diligently might be copying from the student next to them. A student who is staring at the ceiling might be thinking deeply. A student who looks confused might have just had a bad morning. A student who is nodding along might be completely lost but doesn't want to show it.

Even in a group of 8, 2-3 students are usually invisible. They've developed sophisticated survival strategies: they nod at the right moments, they look at others' work before committing to their own answer, they time their responses so they're never the first to answer (or the last). These students aren't being deliberately deceptive — they're doing what years of classroom experience have taught them to do: stay safe by staying invisible.

> [!warning]
> **The small-group illusion:** Small groups make teachers feel like they have full visibility of every student. This is almost never true. Even with 8 students, 2-3 are usually operating below your radar — nodding, copying, and staying quiet. You need systems that make ALL thinking visible, not just the thinking of the most vocal students.

# All-Student Response Techniques

The principle is simple: every student produces a response. You see all thinking, not just the confident volunteers. Here are techniques that work well in small groups of 8-10:

**Quick written responses.** Give students a slip of paper or a mini-whiteboard. Ask a question. Everyone writes their answer. Everyone holds it up (or places it face-down for you to collect). You see every response. This takes 60 seconds and gives you more information than 10 minutes of "Does everyone understand?"

**Show-me fingers.** For multiple-choice diagnostic questions, students hold up 1, 2, 3, or 4 fingers to show their answer choice. You see every answer instantly. The key: everyone shows at the same time (count "3-2-1-show") so no one can copy.

**Turn-and-explain.** Students turn to a partner and explain the concept in their own words. You listen in on the conversations. This is powerful because explaining requires real understanding — you can't explain something you don't understand. And you hear the specific language students use, which tells you a lot about their thinking.

**Written exit questions.** One question before students leave. Not a full exit ticket with five questions — one carefully chosen question that checks the key learning intention. You read the responses before the next session with that group.

> [!tip]
> **Start with one technique.** You don't need to use all of these. Pick one that feels natural — many teachers start with mini-whiteboards or show-me fingers because they're quick and visual. Use it consistently until it becomes automatic, then consider adding another.

# The Simultaneous Response Principle

There's one critical rule that makes all of these techniques work: students must respond simultaneously.

If students answer one at a time — or if some students can see other students' answers before committing to their own — the shy, uncertain, or struggling students will copy. The whole point is lost.

This is why "3-2-1-show" matters for show-me fingers. This is why mini-whiteboards are held up at the same time, not one by one. This is why you might collect written responses face-down rather than having students read them aloud in turn.

The simultaneous response principle ensures you get genuine, independent thinking from every student — not just the first confident answer repeated by everyone else.

# Every Student, Every Lesson

The goal isn't to use these techniques occasionally. It's to make checking every student's thinking a normal, unremarkable part of every lesson. When it becomes routine, something important happens: students stop seeing it as a test and start seeing it as how learning works.

In groups of 8-10, you have an enormous advantage over larger classes. You can genuinely see every response, follow up with individual students, and adapt in real time. But that advantage only exists if you have systems that make every student's thinking visible. Without those systems, small groups just mean that fewer students are invisible — not that none are.

> "The purpose of classroom questions is not to determine who knows the answer and who does not — it's to generate information that helps the teacher decide what to do next."
> — Dylan Wiliam

# From Concept to Craft

In LS 101, you learned the key idea: a diagnostic question is one where each wrong answer reveals a specific misconception. You learned why this matters — it doesn't just tell you who's wrong, it tells you what they're thinking.

Now we go deeper. In this section, you'll learn how to actually design diagnostic questions. This is a craft — it takes practice — but the basic process is straightforward once you understand it.

# Step 1: Identify the Key Concept and Common Misconceptions

Before you write a single word of the question, you need to answer two things:

1. What is the key concept I want to check?
2. What are the 2-3 most common misconceptions students have about this concept?

The second question is where your experience as a teacher is invaluable. You've seen students get confused about specific things over and over. You know which mistakes come up every year. Those recurring mistakes are the raw material for diagnostic questions.

For example, if you're teaching subtraction with regrouping, you know that some students will subtract the smaller digit from the larger digit regardless of position (72 - 48 = 36, because they do 8-2 and 7-4). That's a specific, predictable misconception. You can design a question around it.

If you're teaching persuasive writing, you know that some students confuse an opinion with evidence ("I think recycling is good because it's really important"). That's a specific misconception. You can check for it.

> [!info]
> **Where to find misconceptions:** Your own teaching experience is the best source. What mistakes do you see again and again? What do students confuse with what? If you teach the same topic yearly, you probably already know the top 2-3 misconceptions — even if you've never written them down. Writing them down is the first step.

# Step 2: Write the Correct Answer

Once you know the concept and the misconceptions, write the correct answer first. Make it clearly correct — not a trick, not ambiguous. The correct answer should be one that a student who genuinely understands the concept would choose confidently.

# Step 3: Design the Distractors

This is the most important step — and the part that makes diagnostic questions different from regular multiple-choice questions.

Each wrong answer (called a "distractor") should be the answer that a student with a specific misconception would choose. The distractors aren't random wrong answers. They're carefully designed windows into student thinking.

Here's an example:

**What is 0.7 + 0.03?**
- A) 0.10 — student added digits without understanding place value (7 + 3 = 10)
- B) 0.73 ✓ — correct
- C) 1.0 — student thinks 0.7 + 0.03 must round up to a whole number
- D) 0.037 — student concatenated the digits (7 and 3) after the decimal point

Each wrong answer tells a different story. If a student chooses A, you know they're adding without place value understanding. If they choose D, they're treating decimals like whole numbers after the decimal point. You don't just know they're wrong — you know exactly how they're thinking and what needs to be addressed.

> [!tip]
> **The distractor test:** For each wrong answer, ask yourself: "What would a student have to believe to choose this answer?" If you can't answer that question, the distractor isn't doing its job. Replace it with one that maps to a known misconception.

# Why Open Questions Sometimes Tell You LESS

This surprises many teachers: a well-designed diagnostic question can tell you more about student thinking than an open question.

If you ask "What is the perimeter of this rectangle?" and a student writes "24," you know they're wrong (the answer is 20). But you don't know WHY. Did they multiply instead of add? Did they forget one side? Did they add all four sides but make an arithmetic error? The wrong number alone doesn't tell you what to teach next.

A diagnostic question with designed distractors gives you the "why" immediately:

**What is the perimeter of a rectangle with length 7 and width 3?**
- A) 10 — only added length + width once
- B) 20 ✓ — correct (7+3+7+3 or 2×7+2×3)
- C) 21 — multiplied length × width (found area instead of perimeter)
- D) 14 — only added the two lengths

Each wrong answer reveals a different misunderstanding. In 30 seconds, you know exactly what each student is thinking — and you know what to do about it.

Open questions have their place — they're essential for deeper thinking and creativity. But for quick checks of conceptual understanding, a well-designed diagnostic question is often more powerful because it's more interpretable.

# Connection to EI ASSET

You're already familiar with EI ASSET — the annual diagnostic assessment your students take. Here's something you may not have realised: ASSET is built on exactly this principle.

Each wrong answer on an EI ASSET question maps to a known misconception. When you get ASSET results, you're not just getting scores — you're getting a map of what your students misunderstand and why. That's what makes ASSET diagnostic rather than just a test.

The insight is: you don't need EI to write diagnostic questions. You can use the same principle in every lesson. EI does it once a year at scale. You can do it every day in your small group. The principle is the same — design questions where wrong answers reveal specific misconceptions, then use that information to teach.

# Designing as a Team

When teachers collaborate on diagnostic questions, the quality improves dramatically. Here's why: you might know two common misconceptions about a topic, but your colleague knows a third one you haven't encountered. Between you, you cover more ground.

At PEP, where teachers work closely together, this is a natural fit. Spend 15 minutes with a colleague: pick a concept, list the misconceptions you each see, and write 2-3 diagnostic questions together. You'll end up with better questions than either of you would write alone — and you'll learn from each other's experience.

> "Every teacher can learn to write better questions. And when teachers design questions together, sharing the misconceptions they see in their classrooms, the questions — and the teaching — get dramatically better."
> — Dylan Wiliam

# From Checking to Deciding

In the last section, you learned how to design diagnostic questions. But a good diagnostic question isn't just a clever quiz item — it's a decision point in your lesson.

A decision point is a moment where you ask a diagnostic question, look at the responses, and decide what happens next. Not tomorrow. Not next week. Right now.

This is the shift from checking ("let me see who gets it") to deciding ("based on what I see, here's what I'm going to do"). Checking is passive. Deciding is active. Formative assessment lives in the deciding.

# The Three Possible Outcomes

When you ask a diagnostic question to your group of 8-10 students, one of three things will happen:

**Most students get it right.** They understand the concept. Move on. Don't spend 15 more minutes practising something they already know — you'll bore them and waste time you could use on something they actually need.

**Most students get it wrong.** They don't understand yet. Stop. Don't push forward into harder material built on a foundation that doesn't exist. Reteach the concept — but differently than the first time (because the first approach didn't work). Use a different example, a different analogy, a different angle.

**The group is split.** Some students understand, some don't — and their wrong answers reveal different misconceptions. This is the most interesting outcome, and it's where your small group is an enormous advantage. You can pair students who understand with those who don't. You can have students with different answers explain their reasoning to each other. The disagreement itself becomes the learning activity.

> [!info]
> **The split is a gift.** When students are evenly divided — some choosing the correct answer, some choosing a specific distractor — you have a perfect setup for productive discussion. Ask one student from each "side" to explain their reasoning. The debate that follows often produces deeper understanding than any teacher explanation could.

# Design Principles for Decision-Point Questions

Not every question works as a decision point. A good decision-point question follows three rules:

1. **Answerable in under 2 minutes.** If it takes longer, it disrupts the flow of the lesson and you lose the ability to respond in real time.
2. **Interpretable in under 30 seconds.** When you see the answers (fingers up, whiteboards showing), you need to know immediately what they tell you. This means well-designed distractors — you should be able to scan the room and know instantly what students are thinking.
3. **Clear instructional response for each outcome.** Before you ask the question, you should know: if most get it right, I'll do X. If most get it wrong, I'll do Y. If it's split, I'll do Z. This is "Plan B built into Plan A" — a concept from LS 101.

If you can't plan a response for each outcome, the question isn't useful as a decision point. You're collecting evidence to admire, not evidence to act on.

> "Don't collect evidence to admire it. Collect evidence to decide."
> — Dylan Wiliam

# Planning Decision Points in Your Lesson

Here's the practical skill: look at your lesson plan and identify the 1-2 moments where understanding is most likely to break down.

These are the moments where:
- A new concept is introduced that builds on a previous one
- Students might confuse two similar ideas
- There's a common misconception you know from experience
- The lesson shifts from concrete to abstract (or vice versa)

Those are your decision points. Place a diagnostic question there. Have your Plan B ready.

For example, in a lesson on multiplication as repeated addition, the decision point might come when you ask students to represent "4 × 3" — some students will think it means "4 + 3" (confusing multiplication with addition). That's predictable. Place your diagnostic question there. If the confusion shows up, you have a specific response planned. If it doesn't, you move on with confidence.

# The Connection to "Plan B"

In LS 101, you learned the principle: "Build Plan B into Plan A." Diagnostic questions at decision points are exactly how you do this.

Plan A is your lesson as designed — the sequence you've planned, the activities you've prepared, the path you expect to follow. Plan B is what you'll do when the diagnostic question reveals that students aren't where you thought they'd be.

The power is that you're not surprised. You're not scrambling. You anticipated the misconception, designed a question to detect it, and prepared a response. Your Plan B isn't a backup plan you hope you won't need — it's a prepared pathway you're ready to take if the evidence tells you to.

| Without decision points | With decision points |
|---|---|
| Teach the lesson as planned | Teach until the decision point |
| Hope students understand | Check whether students understand |
| Find out they didn't on the next assessment | Find out right now |
| Reteach next week (if you notice) | Reteach right now, with a prepared Plan B |

> [!tip]
> **Start with one.** You don't need decision points at every step of every lesson. Start by identifying the single most critical moment in your lesson — the one place where misunderstanding is most likely to occur — and place one diagnostic question there. One is enough to transform a lesson from "I hope they got it" to "I know whether they got it."

# What Responsive Teaching Looks Like

You now have the tools: clear learning intentions, all-student response techniques, diagnostic questions placed at decision points. But what does it actually look like when a teacher uses all of these in real time? How does it feel?

This section is about responsive teaching in action — real examples of teachers adapting their lessons based on what they discover about student thinking. These aren't theoretical scenarios. They're the kinds of moments that happen in every small group, every day.

# The Planned vs Responsive Tension

Here's a tension many teachers feel: "If I change my lesson in the middle, doesn't that mean I planned badly?"

No. It means you planned well.

Good planning gives you a structure to deviate from. A teacher who has thought carefully about the learning intention, anticipated misconceptions, prepared diagnostic questions, and planned responses for each outcome is MORE flexible, not less. She can pivot because she has somewhere to pivot to.

A teacher who hasn't planned can't respond effectively to what students reveal. She's stuck following her original plan because she has no alternative prepared. Paradoxically, the better your plan, the less likely you are to follow it exactly — because you're equipped to respond to what actually happens.

> [!info]
> **Planning enables flexibility.** This seems contradictory but it's not. A detailed plan with decision points and prepared alternatives gives you the confidence to deviate. A loose plan with no alternatives locks you into whatever you originally intended.

# Example 1: The Misconception Discovery

A teacher is working with a group of 9 students on subtracting decimals. She asks a diagnostic question: 3.5 − 1.8 = ?

She shows four options: A) 1.7, B) 2.3, C) 2.7, D) 1.3

Students show their answers on whiteboards. She sees: 3 students chose A (correct), 1 chose B, and 5 chose C.

Answer C (2.7) reveals a specific misconception — students subtracted 1 from 3 and 5 from 8 (treating each column independently without regrouping). Five of nine students share this misconception.

She stops. She doesn't continue to the next problem. She puts a new example on the board using base-ten blocks, making the regrouping physically visible. She walks through 3.5 − 1.8 using the blocks, showing why you can't just subtract 5 from 8 in the tenths column. Then she asks a similar question: 4.2 − 2.6 = ? She checks again. This time, 7 of 9 get it right. She moves on.

Total time spent: 6 minutes. Six minutes that saved her from building a lesson sequence on a foundation of misunderstanding.

# Example 2: The Productive Split

A teacher asks a diagnostic question about whether a paragraph in a text is fact or opinion. The group splits almost evenly — 4 say fact, 5 say opinion.

Instead of revealing the answer, she does something more powerful. She pairs students with different answers: "You said fact. You said opinion. Each of you has 90 seconds to convince your partner. Go."

The room erupts in quiet, focused argument. Students are citing specific words from the paragraph, making claims about what counts as evidence, wrestling with the boundary between fact and opinion. This is exactly the kind of thinking the lesson was designed to develop — and it emerged naturally from the disagreement.

After the pairs report back, she helps the group resolve the question. But the real learning happened in the argument, not in the answer.

# Example 3: The Confident Skip

A teacher has planned 15 minutes of guided practice on a concept she introduced last session. But first, she asks a quick diagnostic question to see what students remember.

All 8 students get it right. Not one incorrect answer.

She faces a choice: stick with her plan (15 minutes of practice on something they already know) or skip ahead to the harder application she'd planned for later.

She skips. She moves directly to the application task, which is more challenging and more interesting. She gained 15 minutes by not practising something students already knew. Her students spend that time on harder, more productive work instead of being bored by easy repetition.

> [!tip]
> **Skipping forward is just as important as stopping to reteach.** Responsive teaching isn't just about catching problems — it's also about not wasting time on things students already know. When the evidence tells you they're ready, trust it and move on.

# The Messy Lesson Is the Learning

Responsive teaching doesn't look polished. A lesson where the teacher stops midway, pulls out an unplanned analogy, re-checks understanding, pairs students for debate, and skips the practice she'd prepared looks messy from the outside. It doesn't follow the neat arc of a scripted lesson.

But it produces more learning — because it meets students where they actually are, not where the plan assumes they'll be.

The scripted lesson looks impressive. The responsive lesson works.

This doesn't mean planning doesn't matter — it matters enormously (as we discussed). But the plan is a starting point, not a contract. The best lessons are the ones where the teacher was prepared enough to abandon the plan when the evidence told her to.

> "The best teachers do not follow lesson plans slavishly. They use them as a framework within which they can respond to the evidence of learning they collect minute by minute."
> — Dylan Wiliam

# Strategy 4: Students Helping Students

Wiliam's fourth strategy is "activating learners as instructional resources for one another." In plain language: students helping each other learn.

This isn't group work for the sake of group work. It's not "stronger students teach weaker students." It's a structured approach where both students in an interaction learn — the one explaining and the one listening.

Why does this work? Because explaining something to someone else is one of the most powerful learning activities there is. When you explain, you have to organise your knowledge, find the right words, identify the key points, and respond to questions. All of that forces you to think more deeply than you would on your own. The explainer doesn't just help the listener — the explainer learns more too.

> [!info]
> **The explanation effect:** Research consistently shows that students who explain concepts to peers achieve deeper understanding than students who only study the concept alone. The act of explaining forces you to organise, clarify, and make connections — all of which strengthen your own learning.

# Peer Explanation

The simplest version: after introducing a concept, have students explain it to a partner in their own words. You listen in.

This serves two purposes. First, you hear students' language — how they describe the concept, what they emphasise, what they leave out, what they get wrong. This tells you more about their understanding than any quiz could. Second, students who can't explain the concept discover that they don't understand it as well as they thought (remember the fluency trap?). The act of trying to explain exposes gaps that silent nodding hides.

In your small groups of 8-10, you can listen to every pair. You can hear specific language that reveals misconceptions. You can identify which students are articulate but confused (they talk fluently but say wrong things) and which are quiet but solid (they struggle to articulate but understand deeply).

# Peer Assessment: Giving Feedback, Not Grades

Peer assessment doesn't mean students grading each other. It means students giving feedback to each other — specific, constructive feedback using clear criteria.

One useful structure is "two stars and a wish":

- **Two stars:** Two specific things the work does well. Not "it's good" — specific. "Your opening sentence makes me want to keep reading" or "You gave a clear example that helped me understand your point."
- **One wish:** One specific area to improve. Not "make it better" — specific. "I think your argument would be stronger if you added evidence from the text" or "I wasn't sure what you meant in the third sentence."

The key word is SPECIFIC. Generic praise ("good job") or generic criticism ("try harder") is useless. Students need to be taught how to give specific feedback — it's a skill, not an instinct.

# Why Peer Assessment Builds Self-Assessment

Here's the deeper reason peer assessment matters: by evaluating someone else's work, students develop a sense of quality that they then apply to their own work.

When a student looks at a peer's work and says, "This argument needs evidence," she's developing the ability to look at her OWN work and think, "Does MY argument have evidence?" Peer assessment is a bridge to self-assessment — which is Wiliam's Strategy 5 and the subject of the next section.

This is why peer assessment should come before self-assessment in your teaching. Students need practice recognising quality in OTHER people's work before they can reliably recognise it in their own.

> [!tip]
> **Use anonymous work first.** When students are new to peer assessment, use anonymous examples — work samples with names removed (or work from a previous year). This removes the social risk of criticising a friend's work and lets students practise the skill of giving feedback without the emotional weight.

# The Risks — and How to Manage Them

Peer assessment can go wrong. Here are the risks and how to handle them:

**Unequal power dynamics.** In every group, some students have more social power than others. If a popular student dismisses a quieter student's feedback, it can shut down participation. Mitigation: establish clear norms ("we listen to all feedback respectfully") and intervene immediately when power dynamics distort the process.

**Incorrect peer feedback.** Students sometimes give wrong advice. A student might tell a peer that their correct answer is wrong, or suggest a change that makes the work worse. Mitigation: always review peer feedback before students act on it. In the early stages, position peer feedback as "suggestions to consider" rather than "corrections to make."

**Social pressure.** Students may give only positive feedback to be nice, or give harsh feedback to assert status. Mitigation: use the "two stars and a wish" structure to require both positive and constructive feedback. Model what good feedback sounds like before students try it.

# Start with Pairs, Not Groups

When introducing peer learning, start with pairs. Always.

Pairs are simpler — there's one person to talk to, one person to listen to, and nowhere to hide. In a group of four, two students can stay silent while the other two do the work. In a pair, both students must participate.

Once pairs work well — once students know how to explain, listen, and give feedback in a pair — you can expand to trios or small groups. But pairs first.

# The Ladder of Feedback

For more structured peer feedback, use the Ladder of Feedback — a four-step protocol:

1. **Clarify.** "I want to make sure I understand. Are you saying that...?" Ask questions to understand the work before evaluating it.
2. **Value.** "I think what works well is..." Identify specific strengths.
3. **Suggest.** "Have you considered...?" Offer specific suggestions for improvement.
4. **Raise questions.** "I'm wondering about..." Identify areas that need more thought.

The order matters. Starting with clarification prevents misunderstandings. Starting with value builds trust. Suggestions come only after the student knows their work has been understood and appreciated. Questions open further thinking without being directive.

> [!warning]
> **Don't skip the teaching.** Students don't automatically know how to give good peer feedback. You need to model it, practise it, and refine it — just like any other skill. The first few attempts will be rough. That's normal. Keep at it.

# Strategy 5: The Ultimate Goal

Wiliam's fifth and final strategy is the most ambitious: activating learners as owners of their own learning. In practical terms, this means students who can monitor and regulate their own learning — who can honestly assess what they understand and what they don't, and take action to close the gap.

This is the ultimate goal of formative assessment. When it's working at its best, students don't need the teacher to tell them what they need to work on — they can figure it out themselves. They become their own formative assessors.

But this is also the hardest strategy to implement, because it goes against a powerful force: the fluency trap.

# Self-Assessment Is Not Self-Grading

Let's be clear about what self-assessment means here. It does NOT mean:
- "Give yourself a score out of 10"
- "How well do you think you did?"
- "Rate your effort today"

Those are self-evaluation — and they're almost useless, because students don't have reliable access to the quality of their own understanding. A student who feels confident might be wrong. A student who feels uncertain might be right.

Real self-assessment means: "Do I actually understand this? How do I know?" It's not about feelings. It's about evidence.

This connects directly to metacognition from LS 101 — the three questions:
1. **Plan:** What am I trying to learn? What approach will I take?
2. **Monitor:** Is this approach working? Do I actually understand, or does it just feel familiar?
3. **Evaluate:** Did I achieve the learning intention? What evidence do I have?

Self-assessment is the "monitor" and "evaluate" steps in action.

# Traffic Lights: Simple but Powerful

One of the simplest self-assessment tools is the traffic light system. Students signal their confidence:

- 🟢 **Green:** I understand this. I could explain it to someone else.
- 🟡 **Amber:** I partly understand. I have some questions or confusion.
- 🔴 **Red:** I'm lost. I need help.

This takes 5 seconds. Students hold up a coloured card, or you use a quick hand signal (thumbs up, sideways, down). You get an instant read on the room.

But here's the critical point: traffic lights only work if the teacher acts on the signals. If students show red and the teacher says "Okay, let's keep going," students learn that the signals don't matter. They stop being honest. Within a week, everyone shows green all the time because it's the socially safe choice.

When a student shows red, something must happen. It doesn't have to be immediate — but the student needs to see that their signal led to a response. That might be the teacher coming to check in during independent work, pairing them with a peer, or revisiting the concept with the whole group.

> [!warning]
> **The ignored traffic light.** If you ask students to signal their confidence but don't respond to the signals, you're training them to lie. Students quickly learn whether their honest self-assessment leads to help or is ignored. If it's ignored, they'll default to "green" because it's easier and safer.

# Reflection Routines

Build brief reflection into the end of every session. Not a long journal entry — a quick, focused moment where students answer:

1. **What did I learn today?** (Not "what did we do" — what did I LEARN?)
2. **What am I still confused about?** (Specific — not "nothing" or "everything")
3. **What will I do about it?** (An action — "I'll ask about this tomorrow" or "I'll practice this tonight")

This takes 2-3 minutes. It trains students to distinguish between "what we did" (the task) and "what I learned" (the learning intention) — a distinction we explored in Section 2.

Over time, students get better at this. Early attempts will be vague ("I learned about maths" or "I'm not confused about anything"). That's normal. Prompt them: "What specific thing about maths? Can you explain one thing you learned?" Gradually, their reflections become more precise and more honest.

# The Fluency Trap Revisited

Here's the core challenge with self-assessment: students confuse familiarity with understanding. This is the fluency trap from LS 101, and it's the biggest obstacle to honest self-assessment.

A student reads through her notes and everything makes sense. "I understand this," she thinks. But she's experiencing recognition (familiarity), not genuine understanding (recall). She'd discover the difference if she tried to explain the concept without looking at her notes — but she hasn't tried that, so she genuinely believes she understands.

This is why self-assessment must be paired with verification. "You think you understand? Show me." Ask the student to explain the concept, solve a problem, or apply the idea to a new situation. If they can, great — their self-assessment was accurate. If they can't, you've helped them discover the gap between how they feel and what they actually know.

This isn't about catching students being wrong. It's about teaching them to test their own understanding — to develop the habit of asking themselves, "Can I actually DO this, or does it just feel familiar?"

> [!tip]
> **The explain-it test:** Teach students a simple self-check: "If I can't explain this to someone else without looking at my notes, I don't really understand it yet." This turns self-assessment from a feeling ("I think I get it") into an action ("Let me try to explain it and see").

# Gradual Transfer of Responsibility

You can't hand students full responsibility for their own learning overnight. It's a gradual transfer:

1. **Teacher monitors.** You check understanding through diagnostic questions and all-student response techniques. Students are passive recipients of the checking.
2. **Teacher prompts students to monitor.** You ask "How confident are you?" "What specifically do you understand?" "What questions do you have?" Students begin to practise self-assessment, but with your prompting.
3. **Students monitor independently.** Students ask themselves these questions without being prompted. They self-check, seek help when needed, and take ownership of their own learning gaps.

Most students are at stage 1 or 2. Getting to stage 3 takes time, practice, and trust. You can't skip steps — a student who has never been asked to reflect on their own understanding won't suddenly start doing it independently.

The goal isn't to get every student to stage 3 by the end of a term. The goal is to be deliberately moving every student along this continuum, at their own pace, with appropriate support.

# The Knowing-Doing Gap Returns

You've now learned a lot about formative assessment. You understand what it is (and isn't). You know how to write learning intentions, design diagnostic questions, place them at decision points, respond to evidence, activate students as peer resources, and build self-assessment skills.

The question is: will you actually do any of this on Monday?

This is the knowing-doing gap — the same challenge we addressed in LS 101. Knowing that diagnostic questions are powerful doesn't mean you'll use them. Understanding responsive teaching doesn't mean you'll deviate from your plan when the evidence tells you to. Learning about peer feedback doesn't mean you'll set it up.

The gap isn't about motivation. You're probably motivated right now. The gap is about habit. When you're in the middle of a lesson with 8 students and the clock is ticking and the materials need organising and someone is off task — in that moment, you default to your habits, not your knowledge. And if formative assessment isn't a habit yet, it won't happen.

# The Technique-of-the-Week Trap

Here's a pattern that kills implementation: a teacher tries a different technique every week.

Week 1: mini-whiteboards. Week 2: traffic lights. Week 3: peer feedback. Week 4: exit tickets. Week 5: diagnostic questions.

Each technique is used once or twice, feels awkward, doesn't work perfectly, and is abandoned for the next one. After five weeks, the teacher has tried five techniques and embedded none of them.

The problem isn't the techniques — they all work. The problem is that nothing becomes routine. Techniques only become powerful when they're automatic — when you don't have to think about HOW to use them, so you can focus on WHAT they reveal.

> [!warning]
> **The enemy of routine is variety.** Trying too many techniques too fast means none of them stick. Pick 2-3 techniques. Use them until they're automatic — until you don't have to think about the mechanics anymore. Then add more. Depth before breadth.

# What "Routine" Looks Like

A sustainable formative assessment routine has three components, matching the three natural moments in any lesson:

**Start: A retrieval starter.** Every session opens with a brief question or activity that asks students to recall something from a previous lesson. This isn't a warm-up for fun — it's retrieval practice, which you learned about in LS 101 as one of the most powerful learning strategies. It takes 3-5 minutes and serves a dual purpose: it strengthens memory AND it tells you what students remember (or don't).

**Middle: A diagnostic question at the key decision point.** At the moment in the lesson where understanding is most likely to break down, you ask one carefully designed diagnostic question. You check every student's response simultaneously. Based on what you see, you decide: move on, reteach, or use the productive split. This takes 2-3 minutes but determines whether the rest of the lesson is built on solid ground.

**End: A brief reflection.** Students answer: What did I learn? What am I still confused about? What will I do about it? This takes 2-3 minutes and trains self-assessment over time.

Three moments. Three techniques. Seven to ten minutes total. That's a sustainable routine.

| Lesson moment | What you do | Time | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Start | Retrieval starter | 3-5 min | Strengthen memory + check what students remember |
| Middle | Diagnostic question | 2-3 min | Check understanding at the critical point + decide next steps |
| End | Reflection | 2-3 min | Build self-assessment habits |

> [!info]
> **Seven minutes that transform your teaching.** A retrieval starter, one diagnostic question, and a brief reflection. That's 7-10 minutes out of a session. It won't eat your teaching time — it will make the rest of your teaching time dramatically more effective, because you'll know whether students are actually learning.

# Planning Your First Routine

Here's your practical next step:

1. **Choose your retrieval starter format.** Will you use a question on the board? A "tell your partner" prompt? A quick written recall? Pick ONE format and use it for at least two weeks.

2. **Choose your checking technique.** Mini-whiteboards? Show-me fingers? Written slips? Pick ONE and use it consistently. Get comfortable with the mechanics so you can focus on reading the responses.

3. **Choose your reflection format.** Verbal round-robin? Written slip? Traffic light plus one sentence? Pick ONE and use it every session.

Start on Monday. Use these three techniques — and ONLY these three — for two weeks. Don't add anything new during those two weeks. Focus on making these three things smooth and automatic. After two weeks, evaluate: What's working? What needs adjusting? Then refine and continue.

# Peer Accountability: The TLC Model

In LS 101, you learned about Teacher Learning Communities (TLCs) and the knowing-doing gap. Here's where they connect to formative assessment implementation.

Tell a colleague what you're trying. Be specific: "I'm going to use a retrieval starter, one diagnostic question at the key decision point, and a reflection at the end of each session for the next two weeks."

Check in after two weeks. Share what happened — what worked, what was hard, what surprised you. This isn't about accountability in a punitive sense. It's about making your intention social — which research shows dramatically increases follow-through.

When you tell someone else what you're going to do, you're more likely to do it. When you know someone will ask you about it, you're more likely to persist through the awkward early stage. This is the TLC model: small groups of teachers supporting each other's implementation over time.
