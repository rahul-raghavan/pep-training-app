# Learning Science Workshop — Facilitator Guide

**For:** Rahul (facilitator)
**Audience:** ~20 teachers, mixed elementary and middle school
**Format:** Scenario → group discussion (rank / take a stand) → research reveal → "Monday morning" takeaway
**Setup:** 4–5 tables of 4–5 teachers each, mixed across programs
**Context:** Small groups (8–10 students), no formal grades/exams. Elementary uses Montessori materials-led approach; middle school uses teacher-led small-group instruction. Both value independence and collaborative work.

---

## Before You Start

### Room Setup
- 4–5 round tables (or desks pushed together), 4–5 chairs each
- Mix elementary and middle school teachers at every table — assign seats in advance
- Each table gets: a few blank sheets of paper, pens, and a table number card
- You need: a whiteboard or flip chart, markers, and a way to keep time visible

### Materials to Prepare
- Print the scenario cards (one per table per case) — or project them
- Have a timer visible to the room (phone timer on a screen works)
- Optional: sticky notes for the reflection rounds

### How Each Case Works (explain this to teachers at the start)

> "I'm going to give you a situation. Every situation is something that could happen in any of our classrooms. Your table has 7 minutes to discuss it. In most cases, I'll ask you to rank options or take a stand — there's no sitting on the fence. Then we'll hear from a couple of tables, and I'll share what researchers have found. There are no trick questions — but some of the answers might surprise you."

### Timing Per Case
| Step | Time |
|------|------|
| Read the scenario aloud | 1 min |
| Table discussion | 7 min |
| 2–3 tables share their thinking | 4 min |
| Research reveal + discussion | 8 min |
| **Total per case** | **~20 min** |

### Session Plan Overview

| Session | Title | Cases | Time |
|---------|-------|-------|------|
| **Session 1** | What Is Learning? | Cases 1–4 | ~95 min |
| **Session 2** | Teaching That Responds | Cases 5–8 | ~90 min |
| **Session 3** | Feedback, Ownership & What's Next | Cases 9–12 | ~90 min |

Run Sessions 1–2 on Day 1 (with a break between), Session 3 on Day 2. Or spread across 3 half-sessions over 2–3 days. Adapt as needed.

# SESSION 1: What Is Learning?

## Opening (10 min)

**Say something like:**

> "Over the next few days, we're going to do something different. No lecture. No slides full of bullet points. Instead, I'm going to give you real classroom situations, and you'll figure them out together.
>
> Here's the thing: researchers have been studying how learning works for decades. They've run thousands of studies. And a lot of what they've found is surprising — it goes against what feels intuitive. Some of it might challenge things we currently do in our classrooms.
>
> That's not a criticism. It means we have an opportunity. If we understand how learning actually works, we can make small changes that have big effects.
>
> After these sessions, we'll have an online course that goes deeper into each of these ideas. Think of these workshops as the appetiser — enough to change how you think, and to make you curious to learn more."

**Then:** Quick round of introductions at each table (name, which program, one thing they're curious about). 2 minutes.

## CASE 1: The Revision Surprise

### Scenario (project or read aloud)

> **The situation:** You've been working with a group of students on equivalent fractions. Over several lessons, you used manipulatives, worked through problems together, and gave them practice. By the end of the week, the students were solving equivalent fraction problems confidently — you could see it in their work.
>
> Three weeks later, you're introducing a new topic that builds on fractions. You ask the group to simplify a few fractions as a warm-up. More than half of them stare at the paper. They can't do what they were doing fluently just three weeks ago.
>
> **Discuss at your table:**
> 1. What happened? Why did they "forget"?
> 2. Does this happen in your classroom? When?
> 3. Whose "fault" is it — the students', the teaching, or something else entirely?

### What tables will probably say
- "Students didn't revise." "They're lazy." "They didn't really understand — they just followed the steps." These are normal responses. Don't correct them yet.

### Research Reveal

> "Here's what research tells us — and it's one of the most important things we'll talk about today:"

**The big idea: Performance is not the same as learning.**

- **Performance** is what students can do right now, in this moment, with everything fresh in their minds.
- **Learning** is a durable change in long-term memory — what they can still do weeks or months later, in a new context.

These are not the same thing. A student can perform beautifully on Friday and have learned almost nothing for the long term.

**Why this happens:**

- When you teach something and students practise it immediately, they're using **short-term memory**. It's still "in the air." Of course they can do it.
- For something to stick in long-term memory, the brain needs to **retrieve** it multiple times, over **spaced intervals**, ideally in **varied contexts**.
- One round of teaching + immediate practice = good performance, weak learning.

**The uncomfortable truth:**

> "A smooth, successful-looking lesson can produce no durable learning. And a lesson that feels harder and more effortful can produce much stronger learning. Researchers call this the **fluency trap** — when ease is mistaken for understanding."

### Monday Morning Takeaway

> "So what does this change? If you want to know whether students have actually learned something, you need to check again later — next week, next month. Not with a test — just revisit it. The way you structure practice between now and then is what determines whether learning sticks. We'll get into exactly how in the next few cases."

## CASE 2: The Practice Puzzle

### Scenario

> **The situation:** You notice four students in your classroom preparing for an upcoming presentation on the solar system. Each has a different approach:
>
> - **Student A** is reading through their notes for the third time, highlighting key sentences in different colours.
> - **Student B** has put their notes away and is trying to write down everything they can remember about each planet — then checking what they missed and trying again.
> - **Student C** made a beautiful, colour-coded mind map of the solar system. It's detailed and neat. They keep looking at it admiringly.
> - **Student D** is mixing things up — first recalling facts about Mars, then switching to a maths problem from yesterday, then back to the solar system, then to a vocabulary exercise from last week.
>
> **Discuss at your table: Rank these students from most effective to least effective preparation. Be ready to defend your ranking.**

### Research Reveal

**The ranking, according to decades of research:**

1. **Student B** (retrieval practice) — the most effective learning strategy known. Putting the notes away and trying to recall forces the brain to reconstruct knowledge. Every time you retrieve, the memory gets stronger. This is called the **testing effect** — but it has nothing to do with exams. Testing yourself is not checking what you know — it's actually *building* your knowledge.

2. **Student D** (interleaved practice) — mixing topics forces the brain to figure out *which* knowledge to use and *when*, not just *how*. It feels harder and messier, but produces much stronger long-term retention. When your students mix different types of work during independent time — whether in an elementary work cycle or during middle school independent practice — they get this benefit naturally, as long as they're genuinely engaging with varied material, not just switching between easy tasks.

3. **Student A** (re-reading and highlighting) — feels productive but barely works. After the first read, each re-read produces almost no new learning. Highlighting gives a false sense of engagement without any memory benefit.

4. **Student C** (beautiful notes) — the effort went into the presentation, not the thinking. If the mind map required genuine synthesis, it would help. But making it pretty is decoration, not learning.

**The cruel twist:**

> "Students consistently rate re-reading as their best strategy and feel most confident after using it. But it's one of the weakest. Meanwhile, retrieval practice feels difficult and uncertain — so students avoid it. **Students are reliably wrong about what works for their own learning.** This matters for us as teachers: if we let students choose how to practise, they'll often choose what feels good over what works."

### Monday Morning Takeaway

> "This changes how we design practice time. When students are working independently — which they do a lot in our classrooms — the *type* of practice matters enormously. Re-reading notes or staring at a page isn't working. Closing the book and trying to recall from memory is.
>
> By the way — this is exactly what Mindspark does for your students in maths and English. Every time they use it, they're doing retrieval practice. The software spaces out concepts over time so they revisit material just as it's starting to fade. And it adapts difficulty so students are challenged but not overwhelmed. Now you know *why* Mindspark works — it's not just extra practice. It's implementing the three most powerful learning strategies we know of: retrieval, spacing, and adaptive challenge."

## CASE 3: The Packed Lesson

### Scenario

> **The situation:** A teacher is introducing a group of students to the concept of ecosystems. In a single lesson, she:
> - Explains the terms ecosystem, habitat, food chain, producer, consumer, and decomposer — six new vocabulary words
> - Reads aloud a passage about a rainforest ecosystem
> - Asks students to draw a food chain with labels
> - Then asks them to write a paragraph comparing the rainforest ecosystem to a desert ecosystem
> - Finishes by asking each student to name one thing they learned
>
> The students are busy the whole time. The teacher feels good about how much she covered. But when she revisits the topic two days later, most students can only vaguely say "it's about animals and plants."
>
> **Discuss at your table: Rank the five activities from most valuable to least valuable. If you could only keep TWO, which would you keep and why?**

### Research Reveal

**The big idea: Working memory is a bottleneck — and it's tiny.**

- Human working memory can hold about **3 to 5 new items** at once. That's it. Not six new vocabulary words. Not new vocabulary + reading + drawing + comparing + reflecting — all in one sitting.
- When students get overloaded, they don't learn less effectively — they learn **almost nothing**. The brain can't form lasting memories when working memory is flooded.
- This isn't a flaw in students. It's how every human brain works — yours and mine included.

**Two kinds of difficulty:**

- **Intrinsic load** — the genuine complexity of the material. Ecosystems have real complexity. You can manage it by breaking it into steps: food chains today, producers and consumers tomorrow, comparing ecosystems later in the week.
- **Extraneous load** — difficulty that comes from *how* you present it. Asking students to simultaneously remember new words, apply them to a reading, and draw a diagram = extraneous load. It's wasted effort that takes up working memory without building understanding.

**Why small steps aren't "spoon-feeding":**

> "There's a common belief that making things easier means we're not challenging students enough. But managing cognitive load isn't about making things easy — it's about making sure the difficulty is in the *right place*. You want students' brains working hard on understanding ecosystems — not juggling six tasks at once. Whether you're preparing a Montessori lesson in elementary or planning a small-group session in middle school, the principle is the same: introduce less at once, and students actually learn more."

### Monday Morning Takeaway

> "Before your next lesson, count: how many genuinely new things am I introducing? If it's more than 3 or 4, something needs to move to the next session. Cover less in one sitting, and they'll actually learn more over time. This feels counterintuitive, but it's one of the most robust findings in cognitive science."

## CASE 4: Collaboration — When It Works and When It Doesn't

### Scenario

> **The situation:** Four small groups in a classroom are working on a research project about different civilisations. The teacher has asked them to "work together and prepare a presentation." Here's what actually happens:
>
> - **Group 1:** One strong student does all the research and writing. The others chat and occasionally say "Yeah, that's good." They present confidently — but only one student actually knows the material.
> - **Group 2:** They divide the civilisation into four parts — art, government, daily life, religion. Each student prepares their part alone. They combine the parts at the end. No one reads anyone else's section.
> - **Group 3:** The students discuss the civilisation together. Each person explains what they found. They challenge each other's understanding — "But wait, how does that connect to what you said about their government?" They build a shared explanation that's richer than any one person could have produced.
> - **Group 4:** Two students argue about formatting and who goes first in the presentation. Most of the time is spent on logistics. The content is thin.
>
> **Discuss at your table: Rank the groups from most learning to least learning. Then answer: what made the difference?**

### Research Reveal

**The big idea: Collaboration is not automatically beneficial. It has to be designed.**

This matters especially for us, because collaborative work is central to how we teach — in elementary's Montessori work and in middle school's group projects and discussions. But research shows group work can create:
- **Social loafing** (Group 1) — some students coast because others carry the load
- **Divide-and-ignore** (Group 2) — looks collaborative but no one encounters anyone else's thinking
- **Coordination waste** (Group 4) — time spent organising exceeds time spent learning
- **Genuine shared thinking** (Group 3) — the only group where collaboration actually produced learning

> "Sitting together does not mean learning together. Students can be in a group and learn less than if they'd worked alone. We value collaboration across both our programs. But valuing it means being honest about when it's working and when it's not."

**When does collaboration actually help?**

- When the task is **conceptually rich** enough to benefit from multiple perspectives — not just dividing labour
- When there's **individual accountability** — every person must demonstrate their own understanding
- When there's **structure** — clear expectations for what "working together" actually looks like
- When students have been **taught how to collaborate** — explain reasoning, ask probing questions, build on each other's ideas

**Practical structures:**

- **Think-pair-share:** Think alone first (so everyone generates their own thinking), then discuss with a partner, then share. The solo thinking step is critical — without it, the loudest student dominates.
- **Best composite answer:** Groups discuss, but the final answer must be "better than any one person would have produced alone" — this sets the bar for real collaboration.
- **Individual accountability check:** Call on any random student to explain the group's answer. If they can't, the group hasn't succeeded.

> "Start with pairs before groups. Pairs are the simplest form of collaboration — nowhere to hide, lower coordination costs. Once pair work is strong, move to groups of three or four with clear roles."

### Monday Morning Takeaway

> "Look at your classroom this week. When students are working together, ask yourself: is every student doing cognitive work, or is one student doing the thinking while others watch? If you're not sure, try this: at the end of a group task, ask a random student — not the natural spokesperson — to explain the group's thinking. What you hear will tell you whether collaboration is producing learning or just producing noise."

> "Let's take stock. In four cases, we've uncovered four principles:
> 1. **Performance is not learning.** Looking good today doesn't mean it stuck.

> 3. **Working memory is tiny.** Cover less in one sitting, and they learn more over time.

## CASE 5: Three Ways to Teach the Same Thing

### Scenario

> **The situation:** Three teachers are each introducing the same concept to a group of students who are new to it — let's say long division, or constructing an argument in writing, or balancing a chemical equation. Pick whichever fits your experience.
>
> **Teacher A** believes students learn best by figuring things out. She gives a brief explanation, then hands out a set of problems: "Work through these. Struggle is good — try different approaches, and I'll come around to help if you're stuck." Students are busy and engaged. Some figure it out; many spin their wheels.
>
> **Teacher B** solves three problems on the board, thinking aloud at each step — narrating her decision-making. "First I ask myself... then I check... now I notice that..." Then students try three similar problems in pairs. Then they try five problems independently, with the difficulty gradually increasing.
>
> **Teacher C** gives a thorough explanation with detailed notes, then asks students to copy the notes into their notebooks and review them. Students are quiet and compliant. The notebooks look neat.
>
> A month later, all three groups are given a new problem that requires applying the same concept in a slightly different context.
>
> **Discuss at your table: Rank the three teachers — whose students will perform best on the new problem a month later? Be ready to defend your ranking.**

### What tables will probably say
- Many will favour Teacher A ("discovery learning" and "productive struggle" are popular ideas). Some will favour Teacher B. Few will pick Teacher C, but some might defend the value of notes. This is a genuinely debatable scenario — let the discussion run.

### Research Reveal

**The ranking, according to research:**

1. **Teacher B** (modelling + guided practice + gradual release) — For students who are new to a concept, seeing an expert think through the problem step by step dramatically reduces cognitive load. The brain can focus on understanding the *structure* of the solution instead of burning all working memory on trial and error. This is called the **worked example effect** — one of the strongest findings in cognitive science.

2. **Teacher A** (discovery / productive struggle) — This approach has real value, but **not for novices**. When students don't yet have mental "blueprints" (researchers call them **schemas**) for a type of problem, open-ended exploration often leads to frustration and inefficient floundering — not productive struggle. However, for students who already have the basics down, Teacher A's approach can deepen understanding.

3. **Teacher C** (explain + copy) — Copying notes is one of the weakest learning activities. Students are transcribing, not thinking. The neatness of the notebook tells you nothing about what's in the student's head.

**The expertise reversal effect — this is the key nuance:**

> "Here's what makes this interesting: once students have built those mental blueprints through enough practice with Teacher B's approach, the scaffolding actually becomes *unhelpful*. Worked examples start to slow experts down. At that point, Teacher A's approach — more open problems, less hand-holding — becomes the right move.
>
> So the answer isn't 'Teacher A is wrong' or 'Teacher B is always right.' It's that **the right approach depends on where the student is**. Novices need modelling and support. Students who've built expertise need challenge and independence. The art is knowing when to shift."

> "Across both our programs, we value student independence. This research doesn't contradict that — it refines it. It says: give students the tools first, *then* set them free to explore. Model the thinking, guide the practice, and *then* release them to work independently. In elementary, this is the arc from demonstration to the work cycle. In middle school, it's explain → practise together → work independently. The independence is the goal; the modelling is how you get there."

### Monday Morning Takeaway

> "Think about the next new concept you're introducing. Before you set students loose to work independently, try this: solve two or three problems in front of them while *thinking aloud*. Narrate your decision-making — the hesitations, the checks, the choices. Then do a few together with them. Then let them try alone. The sequence matters more than any single technique."

## CASE 6: "Does Everyone Understand?"

### Scenario

> **The situation:** You're working with a group of 8 students on adjectives. You've explained the concept, given examples, and done a few practice sentences together. You look around the table and ask: "Does everyone understand?"
>
> A few students nod. One says "Yes." Two stay quiet. You move on.
>
> The next day, you discover that three of the eight students can't identify adjectives in new sentences — and one of them is a student who nodded.
>
> **Discuss at your table:**
> 1. What went wrong with "Does everyone understand?"
> 2. Even in a small group of 8, why didn't you catch the confusion?
> 3. What could you do instead to find out what *every* student in your group actually knows?

### What tables will probably say
- Some might argue: "With only 8 students, I can tell who's confused by looking at their faces." Challenge this gently — the research shows otherwise, even in small groups.

### Research Reveal

**The big idea: Teaching is decision-making — but you can't decide without evidence.**

- "Does everyone understand?" gives you no useful information — even in a small group. The students who nod are the confident ones. The confused ones either don't know they're confused (they think they get it), or they don't want to be the one who slows things down.
- Even experienced teachers in small settings misjudge student understanding. We overestimate how much students are following because we confuse attention with comprehension.

**Formative assessment is not a test. It's a decision system.**

Dylan Wiliam defines it this way: assessment is formative when teachers **collect evidence of student understanding** and **use it to make decisions about what to do next**.

> "The key phrase is: *from every student*. Even with eight students, if only three are giving you signals, you're guessing about the other five."

**Simple techniques that work — even in small groups:**

- **Quick written response:** Everyone writes their answer on a slip of paper or whiteboard. You see all eight answers in seconds — no one can hide behind a nod.
- **Show me:** Ask students to hold up fingers (1, 2, 3, or 4) to indicate their answer to a question. Everyone responds simultaneously.
- **Turn and explain:** Ask students to turn to a partner and explain the concept. Walk around and listen — you'll hear the confusion you couldn't see.
- **Exit question:** Before students move on, each one answers one question on paper. Takes 2 minutes. Tells you exactly who needs more help.

> "The point isn't the technique — it's the principle. You need a way to see what *every* student is thinking, not just the ones who volunteer signals. In small groups, it's tempting to think you already know. But nodding and understanding are not the same thing."

### Monday Morning Takeaway

> "Tomorrow, at the point where you'd normally ask 'Does everyone understand?', try this instead: ask a specific question and have everyone write their answer. Look at all the answers before you move on. You'll be surprised at what you find — even in a group of eight."

## CASE 7: The Diagnostic Question

### Scenario

> **The situation:** You're working with a group on area and perimeter. You've covered both concepts and done several examples. Now you want to know: do they actually understand the *difference*, or are they mixing them up?
>
> You write this question on the board:
>
> *"A farmer has a rectangular field that is 8m long and 5m wide. He wants to build a fence around it. How much fencing does he need?"*
>
> - A) 40 metres
> - B) 26 metres
> - C) 13 metres
> - D) 80 metres
>
> Every student writes their answer down. You look at them all.
>
> **Discuss at your table:**
> 1. What does each answer tell you about what the student is thinking?
> 2. Why is this better than just asking "What's the perimeter of this rectangle?"
> 3. What would you do differently depending on which answers you see?

### Research Reveal

**The big idea: Good questions don't just check answers — they diagnose thinking.**

Let's decode the answers:
- **A (40m):** The student calculated 8 x 5 = 40. They found the *area*, not the perimeter. They're confusing the two concepts — this is the most common misconception.
- **B (26m):** Correct. 2(8+5) = 26. They understand perimeter.
- **C (13m):** They added 8+5 = 13. They only counted two sides — a partial understanding.
- **D (80m):** They multiplied and doubled, or made a computational error.

> "This is what's called a **diagnostic question**. It's designed so that every wrong answer reveals a *specific* misunderstanding — not a random error. If a student picks A, you know exactly what's wrong: they're confusing area and perimeter. That's actionable information."

**What makes a diagnostic question powerful:**

- Every wrong answer maps to a known misconception
- Every student answers — you see the whole group
- You can interpret the results in about 30 seconds
- It tells you exactly what to do next: if most say B, move on; if most say A, stop and address the confusion; if answers are split, have students with different answers explain their reasoning to each other

> "Wiliam says: *Don't collect evidence to admire it. Collect evidence to decide.* A diagnostic question gives you the evidence AND tells you the decision."

**You've already seen this in action — EI ASSET assessments:**

> "If this idea feels familiar, it should. The EI ASSET assessments that our students take each year are essentially compilations of diagnostic questions — designed by EI at each grade level to reveal specific misconceptions, not just whether a student got the right answer. The principle is the same: design questions where wrong answers tell you *what* the student is thinking, not just *that* they're wrong. The difference is that EI does this once a year. We can do it every lesson."

### Monday Morning Takeaway

> "Think about a concept you're working on this week where students commonly get confused. Can you design one question where each wrong answer tells you something specific? You don't need four options — even two options where one reveals the key misconception is powerful. Try it tomorrow."

## CASE 8: Two Lesson Plans

### Scenario

> **The situation:** Two teachers plan a lesson on photosynthesis for the same group of students.
>
> **Teacher A** writes a detailed lesson plan: introduction, read-aloud, guided exploration with materials, independent recording in notebooks. She follows it exactly as planned. The lesson flows smoothly. Students are engaged throughout. She covers everything she intended.
>
> **Teacher B** plans the same content but builds in three "check points" — moments where she'll pause and ask a diagnostic question to see if students are following. At each check point, she has two paths prepared:
> - If they get it → move to the next part
> - If they don't → she has a backup: a simpler example, a pair discussion, or a re-explanation using a different approach
>
> Her lesson looks messier. She doesn't get through everything she planned. She has to skip the independent recording because she spent extra time on the read-aloud when she discovered most students had misunderstood a key term.
>
> **Discuss at your table: Rank the two teachers — whose students will remember more a week later? And a harder question: which lesson would look "better" to an observer walking in?**

### Research Reveal

**The big idea: Students do not always learn what we teach.**

> "This is one of Wiliam's most important lines: *Students do not learn what we teach. If they did, we wouldn't need to check at all.* Teaching is not delivery — it's responsive decision-making."

**Why Teacher B's messy lesson wins:**

- Teacher A delivered content. Teacher B taught *students*. The difference is that Teacher B was responding to evidence in real time.
- Teacher A's lesson looked polished to an observer. But she had no idea whether students were following — she never checked. She was performing a lesson, not navigating one.
- Teacher B's "messiness" *is* the learning. When she stopped to re-explain because students misunderstood a key term, that wasn't a failure — that was the most important teaching moment of the lesson.

**"Build Plan B into Plan A":**

- An effective lesson is planned but not rigid. It has **decision points** built in — moments where the teacher finds out what students actually understand and adjusts accordingly.
- Those check points connect everything from this session: you use a diagnostic question (Case 7) to get evidence from every student (Case 6), and then you adapt in real time.

> "The best teachers aren't the ones whose lessons go perfectly according to plan. They're the ones who know when to deviate — because they've designed their lessons to tell them when something isn't working."

> "And here's the awkward truth: the lesson that looks smoother to an observer might be the one that's producing less learning. Coverage is not the same as teaching."

### Monday Morning Takeaway

> "In your next lesson, identify one key moment where understanding could break down. Plan a 2-minute check — a diagnostic question, a quick written response, anything that gives you evidence from every student. And plan what you'll do if the answer isn't what you hoped. That's it. One check point. One backup plan. You don't need to redesign the whole lesson."

## CASE 9: The Three Feedback Approaches

### Scenario

> **The situation:** Three teachers review the same piece of student writing — a short research report by a student named Priya. The report has a strong opening but weak evidence in the middle section, and the conclusion doesn't connect back to the main argument. Each teacher responds differently.
>
> **Teacher A** puts a tick at the top of the page, writes "Good work, Priya! Keep it up!" and hands it back.
>
> **Teacher B** writes detailed comments: "Your introduction is engaging but paragraph 3 needs more evidence. Your conclusion should reference your thesis. Also check spelling in paragraph 2. The structure could be stronger overall." She spends 10 minutes on this one paper.
>
> **Teacher C** writes a short note: "Your introduction drew me in — the opening question works well. Now here's your task: one of your middle paragraphs makes a claim without any supporting evidence. Find it, identify what's missing, and bring me a revised version by Thursday." She spends 3 minutes.
>
> **Discuss at your table: Rank the three approaches — which will produce the most improvement in Priya's next piece of writing? And which will produce the least? Be specific about why.**

### What tables will probably say
- Many will pick Teacher B (detailed comments) as the best. Some will pick Teacher C. Few will defend Teacher A, but some might say it's "enough" — especially if the general culture is that feedback isn't necessary without grades. This is where the interesting discussion happens.

### Research Reveal

**The ranking, according to research:**

1. **Teacher C** — Three minutes of feedback that produces more learning than Teacher B's ten minutes. Why? Because the feedback is a **task**, not a verdict. Priya has to *think*: which paragraph? What's missing? How do I fix it? The cognitive work is on the student, not the teacher. And there's a deadline — it's not optional.

2. **Teacher B** — Detailed, well-intentioned, and mostly wasted. Here's what research shows actually happens: students skim long comments, feel overwhelmed by multiple corrections, and don't know where to start. Worse — when there's no grade, many students interpret detailed corrections as "this was bad" and disengage. And when there *is* some form of marking attached, students focus entirely on that and ignore the comments.

3. **Teacher A** — The tick and "Good work" is the weakest response. Priya learns nothing about what she did well or what to improve. But here's what makes this worth discussing:

> "In our schools, because we don't have traditional grades, there's sometimes a feeling that written feedback isn't necessary either — that the relationship and conversation are enough. And conversation is powerful. But the research is clear: students improve most when they receive specific, actionable feedback that requires them to *do something* with it. No grades doesn't mean no feedback. In fact, it means feedback matters *more* — because there's no grade to hide behind. It's the feedback that drives the learning."

**The detective principle:**

Teacher C's approach embodies what researchers call the "detective, not the told" principle:
- Instead of telling students exactly what's wrong, give them enough information to **locate, diagnose, and fix** the problem themselves
- "Find the paragraph where your claim has no evidence" > "Paragraph 3 needs more evidence"
- "One of your calculations uses the wrong operation — which one?" > "Question 4 is wrong"
- "Read your conclusion and your introduction side by side — what's missing?" > "Your conclusion doesn't connect to your thesis"

> "The goal of feedback is not to correct the work. It's to improve the *student*. If you fix everything for them, the work gets better but the student doesn't."

### Monday Morning Takeaway

> "Next time you're reviewing student work, try Teacher C's approach. Identify the one most important thing to improve. Turn it into a task for the student — not a correction, a task. Make it specific enough that they know where to look, but open enough that they have to think. Set a deadline. Three minutes of this kind of feedback is worth more than thirty minutes of detailed corrections that no one reads."

## CASE 10: The Metacognition Puzzle

### Scenario

> **The situation:** A student comes to you and says: "I practised so much but I still couldn't do it when you asked me. I don't know what happened."
>
> You ask her: "How did you practise?"
>
> She says: "I read through all my notes. I even copied out the important parts again. I spent a whole hour on it."
>
> She's not lying. She really did spend an hour. She really did feel prepared.
>
> **Discuss at your table:**
> 1. What went wrong? She clearly put in effort.
> 2. How would you help this student prepare differently next time?
> 3. Do the students in your classroom know *how* to practise effectively?

### Research Reveal

**The big idea: Students are reliably wrong about what works for their own learning.**

- This student worked hard, but her strategies (re-reading, copying) are among the *least* effective approaches. She felt prepared because re-reading creates a feeling of **fluency** — "I recognise this, so I must know it."
- Research shows students consistently choose strategies that *feel* productive over strategies that *are* productive. Re-reading feels smooth. Retrieval practice feels hard and uncertain. So students avoid the thing that actually works.

**This is a metacognition problem — not a motivation problem.**

- **Metacognition** means thinking about your own thinking — being able to monitor whether you actually understand something, and choosing a better strategy when you don't.
- This student couldn't tell the difference between "I've seen this" and "I can do this from memory." That's a **calibration failure** — and it's extremely common at every age.

**Three questions that build metacognition:**

Teach students to ask themselves:
1. **Planning:** "What's my goal, and what strategy will I use?"
2. **Monitoring:** "Is this working? Can I actually do this without looking at my notes?"
3. **Evaluating:** "What worked? What didn't? What will I do differently next time?"

> "But here's the critical point: you can't teach metacognition as a separate 'thinking skills' lesson. It only works when it's embedded in real subject work. 'Monitor your understanding' is meaningless unless the student has enough knowledge to monitor. Metacognition is built on top of knowledge, not instead of it."

### Monday Morning Takeaway

> "Try this tomorrow: before students start their work, ask 'What's your plan? What are you going to do first?' It takes 30 seconds. Halfway through, walk past and ask 'How's it going? Do you need to change anything?' At the end, ask 'What went well? What would you do differently?' Three questions — plan, monitor, evaluate — built into the work they're already doing. That's metacognition. Not a lesson about thinking. Just three questions, every day, until students start asking themselves."

## CASE 11: The Feedback That Builds Trust

### Scenario

> **The situation:** Three teachers respond to a student named Aarav who has submitted a weak project — the effort is minimal, the thinking is shallow, and it's clear he rushed through it.
>
> **Teacher A** writes: "Aarav, I can see you put in some effort. Nice attempt. Let me know if you need help."
>
> **Teacher B** writes: "Aarav, this isn't your best work. I've seen what you're capable of, and this falls short. I need you to redo it and take it seriously this time."
>
> **Teacher C** writes: "Aarav, I'm giving you this feedback because I hold you to a high standard and I believe you can meet it. Your opening section shows you understand the question — that tells me you have the foundation. But your second section makes a claim without backing it up. Here's what I want you to do: go back to your sources, find one piece of evidence that supports your point, and rewrite that section. Bring it to me by Thursday."
>
> **Discuss at your table: Rank the three approaches — which one is most likely to get Aarav to actually improve his work? Which is least? And critically — which one builds the strongest relationship over time?**

### Research Reveal

**The ranking:**

1. **Teacher C** — This is what researchers call **wise feedback**. It works because it sends two signals simultaneously:
   - "I have high standards" — this isn't personal, I expect real work
   - "I believe you can meet them" — I'm pushing you because I see capability, not because I'm disappointed in you

2. **Teacher B** — Well-intentioned, but risky. "I need you to redo it" without specific guidance leaves Aarav knowing he failed but not knowing what to do differently. And "take it seriously" can feel like an accusation. This approach works only if the relationship is already very strong.

3. **Teacher A** — Feels kind. Is actually the weakest response. "Nice attempt" when the work was clearly weak can signal one of two things to a student: either the teacher doesn't care enough to be honest, or the teacher doesn't believe the student is capable of more. Both are damaging over time.

**Why wise feedback works:**

> "The research on wise feedback found that students — especially students who've experienced inconsistent feedback or who aren't sure if their teacher truly believes in them — respond dramatically better when critical feedback comes with an explicit frame: 'I have high standards AND I believe you can reach them.' Without that frame, the same critical feedback can be experienced as dismissal or punishment."

**Teacher C's approach also follows the principles from Case 9:**

- Names something specific that works (the opening section) — so the student knows they're not starting from zero
- Identifies the specific problem (second section, claim without evidence)
- Gives a concrete task (find evidence, rewrite)
- Sets a deadline (Thursday)

> "Notice that Teacher C spent the *least* emotional energy but produced the most actionable feedback. She didn't need to express disappointment or praise — she just described reality, affirmed belief, and gave a task."

### Monday Morning Takeaway

> "Next time a student's work falls short, resist two temptations: the temptation to soften it into meaninglessness ('Nice try!'), and the temptation to express frustration ('This isn't good enough'). Instead, try the wise feedback frame: 'I'm giving you this feedback because I expect a lot from you and I believe you can deliver. Here's specifically what needs to change, and here's what I want you to do about it.' It takes practice, but students respond to it powerfully."

## CASE 12: The Knowing-Doing Gap

### Scenario

> **The situation:** You attend a fantastic professional development session. The facilitator is engaging. You learn about retrieval practice, spacing, formative assessment, feedback design. You take detailed notes. You feel energised and ready to try new things.
>
> You go back to your classroom. Week 1, you try a few new things — a retrieval starter, a diagnostic question. Week 2, things get busy — assessments, reports, parent meetings. You fall back to your usual routines. By week 4, you're teaching exactly the way you were before. Your notes are in a bag somewhere.
>
> This isn't a hypothetical. Research shows this is what happens with most professional development.
>
> **Discuss at your table:**
> 1. Why does this happen? You understood the ideas and were motivated to use them.
> 2. What would need to be different for the change to actually stick?
> 3. What support would *you* need to change one thing about your teaching practice — honestly?

### Research Reveal

**The big idea: Knowing is not doing. Changing practice requires structures, not just inspiration.**

- Research shows that **one-shot professional development** — even brilliant sessions — changes almost nothing about teaching practice long-term. Teachers walk away inspired, but without ongoing support, they revert within weeks.
- This isn't a criticism of teachers. It's how habit change works for everyone. Knowledge alone doesn't change behaviour — not for your students, and not for you.

**What actually changes practice:**

1. **Small steps, not big transformations.** Pick one technique. Try it for two weeks. Refine it. Then add another. Ambitious overhauls fail because they overload working memory — yes, the same principle that applies to your students applies to you. The brain can only change one habit at a time.

2. **Peer accountability.** When you commit to trying something and know a colleague will ask you about it next week, follow-through increases dramatically. This is the idea behind **Teacher Learning Communities** — small groups of teachers who meet regularly over months, each working on specific small changes and reporting back.

3. **Observation for learning, not evaluation.** When a colleague watches you try a new technique and gives you honest feedback, that's professional learning. When someone watches to rate your performance, that's evaluation. Mixing them up kills the learning.

> "Here's the uncomfortable truth: the most important factor in whether you'll use what you've learned in these sessions is not how good the ideas are. It's whether there's a structure that supports you in practising them over the coming weeks and months. Ideas without structures fade. Structures without ideas are empty. You need both."

### Monday Morning Takeaway

> "That's why we're not stopping here. These sessions are the start, not the finish."

**Activity (5 min):**

> "On a piece of paper, write down:
> 1. **One idea** from these sessions that you want to try in your classroom
> 2. **One specific thing** you'll do differently in your next lesson — be concrete
> 3. **One person at your table** who will check in with you next week about whether you tried it
>
> Turn to that person now and exchange your plans. You have 2 minutes. This is your accountability structure — and it starts now."
