## Section 1: Why Most PD Doesn't Work
**Slug:** why-most-pd-doesnt-work
**Estimated Minutes:** 15

### Content

# The Knowing-Doing Gap

Here's a pattern every school leader has seen. You send teachers to a brilliant workshop. They come back buzzing with ideas. They talk about it in the staffroom. They mean to try it. And then... nothing changes.

This isn't a problem with teachers. It's not laziness or resistance. It's a structural problem — the same structural problem that applies to all human learning. Knowledge alone doesn't change behaviour. If it did, everyone who read a diet book would be thin. Everyone who attended a leadership conference would be a great leader.

The knowing-doing gap is real and it's well-documented. Schools invest enormous time and money in professional development — workshops, conferences, consultants, online courses — and most of it produces almost no lasting change in what actually happens in classrooms. The research is clear: one-shot workshops, even excellent ones, produce little measurable impact on teaching practice.

> [!warning]
> **The uncomfortable truth:** Most professional development is a waste of time and money. Not because the content is bad — but because the delivery structure ignores everything we know about how learning works. The same cognitive principles that apply to students apply to teachers.

# Why Inspiration Doesn't Stick

Think about it through the lens of what you learned in LS 201. Knowledge needs to be retrieved, spaced, and practised to transfer to long-term memory. A two-day workshop violates all three principles:

- **No retrieval practice:** Teachers receive information but aren't asked to recall it later.
- **No spacing:** Everything is massed into two intense days. The forgetting curve does its work.
- **No practice with feedback:** Teachers don't try the techniques in their own classrooms with someone watching and coaching.

A teacher leaves the workshop with good intentions and a folder full of handouts. But intentions don't survive the reality of Monday morning — 25 students, a packed schedule, urgent problems demanding attention. The workshop ideas sit in the folder. The folder sits in a drawer.

This is the same pattern you'd see if you taught students everything about fractions in one massive session and then never mentioned fractions again. You know that wouldn't work for children. It doesn't work for adults either.

# What the Research Says Actually Works

Decades of research on effective professional development converge on a clear set of conditions. PD that actually changes practice has these features:

| Feature | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Sustained over months, not days | Allows spacing and retrieval — the same principles from LS 201 |
| Focused on specific techniques | One technique practised well beats ten techniques skimmed |
| Includes peer accountability | People follow through when someone is watching and asking |
| Practice-feedback-refinement cycles | The same loop that drives student learning (LS 301) |
| Observation for learning, not evaluation | Teachers try things in real classrooms with trusted colleagues watching |

Notice the pattern? Every feature that makes PD effective mirrors what makes student learning effective. That's not a coincidence. Learning is learning — the brain doesn't work differently because the learner is 35 instead of 8.

# The Leadership Insight

Here's the shift this course asks you to make. As a classroom teacher, you asked: "How do I help this student learn?" As a leader, the question becomes: "How do I build a system where teachers keep learning — not for a workshop day, but permanently?"

The answer isn't better workshops. It's better structures. Structures that build in retrieval, spacing, practice, feedback, and accountability. Structures that don't depend on teacher motivation (which fluctuates) but on routine and habit (which persist).

> [!info]
> **The leader's job in one sentence:** Build structures that make sustained teacher learning inevitable, not optional. The same way you'd build classroom routines that make student learning inevitable — you design the system so that good practice is the default, not the exception.

# What We're Already Building at PEP

This isn't theoretical. The workshop series, the online LS courses, the peer accountability pairs from the training — these are already designed based on this research. The workshops aren't one-shot events. The courses are spaced over months. The accountability pairs build in peer observation and feedback.

Your role as a leader isn't to invent something new. It's to make sure the structure holds. To protect the time. To maintain the rhythm. To be the person who says, "We're doing this, and we're doing it properly." That's what this course will teach you to do.

# The Cost of Getting This Wrong

When PD doesn't work, the cost isn't just the money spent. It's the opportunity cost — the things teachers COULD have learned if the time had been used well. And it's the cynicism cost. Every failed initiative makes teachers more sceptical of the next one. "Oh, another programme. We'll try it for a term and then move on to the next thing."

That cynicism is rational. It's based on experience. The way to overcome it isn't enthusiasm or persuasion — it's by actually building something that works and sticking with it long enough for teachers to see results. That's what this course is about.

# What Is a Teacher Learning Community?

A Teacher Learning Community — a TLC — is a small group of teachers who meet regularly over an extended period, each working on improving one specific aspect of their teaching. It's not a committee. It's not a meeting. It's a professional learning structure with a clear purpose: sustained improvement through practice, feedback, and accountability.

The research on TLCs (sometimes called Professional Learning Communities or teacher inquiry groups) is strong. When structured well — and that "structured well" is doing a lot of work — they are one of the most effective forms of professional development available. When structured poorly, they're just another meeting that wastes teachers' time.

The key features of an effective TLC:

- **Small group:** 10-12 teachers. Large enough for diverse perspectives, small enough for accountability. Everyone speaks, everyone reports.
- **Regular meetings:** Fortnightly, 75 minutes. Not monthly (too much spacing). Not weekly (too much burden). Fortnightly is the sweet spot.
- **Extended duration:** At least one year, ideally two. Real change in teaching habits takes time — the same way building automaticity in students takes months of practice (LS 201).
- **Specific focus:** Each teacher chooses ONE technique to work on. Not "improve my teaching" — a specific, observable technique from the LS courses.

# How the Cycle Works

Here's the rhythm of a TLC. Each teacher follows this cycle, repeating it every two weeks:

**Step 1: Choose a technique.** Pick one specific thing from the LS courses — retrieval starters, diagnostic questions, detective feedback, whatever matches your current growth area. Just one.

**Step 2: Try it for two weeks.** Use the technique in your actual teaching. Not once — repeatedly. Get some practice under your belt. Notice what happens.

**Step 3: Report at the next TLC meeting.** Tell the group: What did you try? What happened? What worked? What didn't? What will you adjust next cycle?

**Step 4: Listen and learn from others.** Hear what your colleagues tried and what they learned. Steal ideas. Offer suggestions. See patterns across different classrooms and age groups.

**Step 5: Plan your next two weeks.** Either refine the same technique (if it's not yet working smoothly) or — only when it's become routine — add a new one.

> [!info]
> **The spacing connection:** Notice the two-week cycle? This is spacing by design. Teachers encounter the idea (in the LS course), try it (in their classroom), reflect on it (at the TLC meeting), and try again (next cycle). Each cycle is a retrieval-practice-feedback loop. The same principles from LS 201, applied to adult learning.

# The Structured Agenda

A TLC meeting is NOT a free-flowing discussion. It's not a gripe session about workload. It's not a lecture from the leader. It follows a structured agenda:

| Time | Activity | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 0-5 min | Check-in | Brief personal connection — builds trust |
| 5-20 min | Sharing round 1 | 3-4 teachers report on their technique: what they tried, what happened |
| 20-35 min | Discussion | Group responds: questions, suggestions, connections to other techniques |
| 35-50 min | Sharing round 2 | Remaining teachers report |
| 50-65 min | Discussion | Group responds |
| 65-75 min | Planning | Everyone states what they'll try in the next two weeks |

The agenda is tight. Every teacher reports. Every teacher plans. Nobody gets to sit back and observe. That's the accountability structure — knowing you'll be asked "What did you try?" is a powerful motivator.

# This Isn't New — It's What We Started

Here's the important frame for PEP. The TLC isn't a new initiative. It's a name for what we already started in the workshop series. The peer accountability pairs. The commitment to try one thing. The online LS courses providing the content. The follow-up sessions providing the rhythm.

Your job as a leader is to formalise what's already happening. Give it structure, protect the time, and keep it going when the initial enthusiasm fades. Because enthusiasm always fades. Structures persist.

> [!tip]
> **The leader's mindset shift:** You're not launching something new. You're building scaffolding around something that's already started. The workshop lit the fire. The LS courses provide the fuel. The TLC is the fireplace that keeps it contained and burning.

# What Makes TLCs Fail

TLCs fail when any of these things happen — and they're all preventable:

- **Meetings get cancelled.** "We're too busy this week." Once you skip one, it becomes easy to skip the next. The rhythm breaks.
- **The agenda drifts.** Instead of sharing techniques, the meeting becomes a complaint session about schedules, parents, or policies. Important topics — but not the TLC's job.
- **Free riders.** A teacher comes to every meeting but never tries anything between sessions. They listen, nod, and report "I didn't get to it this time." If this is tolerated, it becomes the norm.
- **Too ambitious.** A teacher tries to overhaul their entire practice at once instead of focusing on one technique. They burn out and give up.

Every one of these failure modes is the leader's responsibility to prevent. Not by being harsh — by being consistent. Protect the time. Hold the agenda. Expect participation. Keep the focus tight.

# Tight but Loose

The most effective teacher learning structures share a principle: they are tight on process but loose on content. Here's what that means:

**Tight:** Every teacher follows the same cycle. Choose a technique. Try it. Report back. Get feedback. Refine. Nobody opts out of the process. The rhythm is non-negotiable.

**Loose:** Each teacher chooses their OWN technique. A Montessori guide working with 4-year-olds might focus on wait time after questions. A middle school English teacher might focus on retrieval starters. A maths teacher might focus on diagnostic questions from LS 202. The technique matches their context, their students, their growth area.

This matters because teachers — like all learners — learn best when they have ownership of WHAT they're learning, even when they don't control HOW the learning is structured. Top-down mandates ("Everyone will implement retrieval starters by March") produce compliance, not learning. The tight-but-loose approach produces genuine professional growth.

> [!info]
> **The autonomy principle:** Give teachers choice over the content of their learning (which technique to focus on), but not over the structure (everyone participates in the cycle). This mirrors what good classroom teaching looks like — you give students choice within a clear framework, not unlimited freedom.

# Why Small Beats Ambitious

A common leadership mistake: asking teachers to overhaul their practice all at once. "We're implementing formative assessment across the school. Everyone should be using retrieval starters, diagnostic questions, exit tickets, AND peer assessment by end of term."

This is the educational equivalent of cramming. It fails for exactly the same reason cramming fails — cognitive overload. Teachers have limited bandwidth, just like students have limited working memory. Trying to change five things at once means doing all five things badly instead of one thing well.

The research is clear: teachers who focus on ONE small technique and practise it until it becomes routine make more progress than teachers who attempt broad changes. This is the same principle from LS 201 about automaticity. A technique that requires conscious effort competes with everything else the teacher is doing. A technique that's become automatic frees up mental resources.

The progression:
1. Choose one technique (e.g., retrieval starters from LS 201)
2. Try it. It will feel awkward and take effort.
3. Practise it for several cycles. It gets smoother.
4. It becomes routine — you do it without thinking about it.
5. NOW add another technique. You have the bandwidth because the first one is automatic.

This is spacing + automaticity + desirable difficulty, all from LS 201. The same science, applied to adult learning.

# Peer Observation for Learning

Here's one of the most powerful tools for teacher growth — and one of the most misused. Peer observation means a colleague watches you teach and gives you feedback. Not your manager. Not an evaluator. A peer.

The purpose is learning, not judgment. A colleague watches you try retrieval starters and notices things you can't see while you're teaching. "Your questions were good, but you only waited two seconds before calling on someone — the quieter students didn't have time to think." That's the kind of feedback you can't give yourself.

The structure is simple:
- **Pairs of teachers.** Each observes the other once per TLC cycle.
- **15-minute focus.** The observer watches just the part of the lesson where the technique is used. Not the whole lesson.
- **Immediate debrief.** Right after (or as close as possible). Short, focused: "Here's what I noticed about the specific technique."
- **One technique only.** The observer isn't evaluating the whole lesson. They're watching for one thing: how did the retrieval starter go?

# The Trust Problem

Here's the challenge. In most schools, observation = evaluation. Someone watching you teach means someone judging you. And judgment makes people defensive, cautious, and performative — the opposite of what you need for learning.

As a leader, you must explicitly separate peer observation from evaluation. Say it clearly: "When your colleague watches you teach, it's for YOUR learning. I will never ask them what they saw. It will never appear in a performance conversation. This is a completely separate system."

And then — this is the hard part — you have to actually keep that promise. If a peer observer mentions something to you in passing ("I noticed Sarah struggles with wait time"), you must NOT use that information in any evaluative context. If teachers learn that peer observation feeds into evaluation, the trust collapses instantly and permanently.

> [!warning]
> **The trust rule:** Peer observation for learning and formal evaluation must be completely separate systems. Different people, different purposes, different information streams. If they get mixed even once, teachers will never trust the learning observation again. Once lost, this trust is almost impossible to rebuild.

# Making It Work Practically

In a small school with around 20 teachers, peer observation pairs should be:
- **Cross-level where possible:** A Montessori guide paired with a middle school teacher can offer fresh eyes. The technique is the focus, not the age group.
- **Voluntary initially:** Let the enthusiastic pairs start first. Their positive experience creates social proof.
- **Reciprocal:** Both teachers observe each other. Nobody is "the one being watched" — both are learning.
- **Time-protected:** Build the 15 minutes of observation plus 15 minutes of debrief into the timetable. If it depends on teachers "finding time," it won't happen.

The debrief conversation follows a simple structure: "I watched you try [technique]. Here's what I noticed worked well. Here's one thing I'd suggest adjusting." That's it. No rubric. No form. No grade. Just a colleague helping a colleague get better.

# The Five Adoption Questions

As a leader, you'll be constantly bombarded with new ideas, programmes, and research. A colleague returns from a conference excited about brain-based learning. A consultant recommends a new reading programme. A parent sends you an article about growth mindset. A government initiative pushes coding across the curriculum.

How do you decide what to adopt and what to ignore? Without a framework, you'll either adopt everything (initiative overload) or adopt nothing (cynical inertia). Neither is good leadership.

Dylan Wiliam, one of the leading researchers on formative assessment, suggests five questions to ask before adopting any new practice:

**1. Does this solve a problem we actually have?**
Not a problem that exists in theory, or in another school, or in the news. A problem YOUR teachers face with YOUR students. If the practice solves a problem you don't have, it's a distraction.

**2. How much will students' learning improve?**
This is the bottom line. Not "how much will teachers enjoy the training?" or "how innovative does it look?" Will students learn more? If you can't answer this clearly, be cautious.

**3. What will it cost — in time, money, and opportunity cost?**
Every new initiative takes teacher time. That time comes from somewhere. If teachers are spending two hours a week on a new programme, what are they NOT doing with those two hours? The opportunity cost is often more important than the financial cost.

**4. Can we implement it properly?**
A practice that works brilliantly in a large urban school with 200 teachers and a full-time instructional coach may not be implementable in a school of 20 teachers where leaders also teach full timetables. Context matters enormously.

**5. Do we know what to do?**
Is the practice specific enough that teachers know exactly what to change in their classrooms? Vague ideas ("be more inquiry-based") aren't implementable. Specific techniques ("ask diagnostic questions with common misconceptions as distractors") are.

> [!info]
> **The five questions as a filter:** These questions aren't designed to make you reject everything. They're designed to make you adopt WELL. When a practice passes all five questions, you can invest in it with confidence. When it fails even one, that's a signal to pause and think harder.

# The Problem with Effect Sizes

If you read educational research — and as a leader, you should — you'll encounter effect sizes. An effect size is a number that describes how much a treatment improved learning compared to a control group. Bigger numbers mean bigger improvements.

That sounds simple and useful. And it can be — but it's also deeply misleading if you don't understand the limitations.

Here's what an effect size means in plain English. An effect size of 0.4 roughly means: "Students who received the treatment learned about the equivalent of an extra four months of schooling compared to students who didn't." An effect size of 0.8 means about eight extra months.

But here's the problem: you can't simply compare effect sizes across different studies and conclude that the practice with the bigger number is better. Why not?

- **Different contexts:** An effect size measured in a controlled lab setting with university students doesn't predict what will happen in your classroom with 8-year-olds.
- **Different measures:** Some studies measure narrow skills (did students remember specific facts?). Others measure broad outcomes (did students improve in overall maths?). A narrow test often produces larger effect sizes.
- **Different quality:** A poorly designed study can produce huge effect sizes that mean nothing. A well-designed study might produce modest effect sizes that are far more trustworthy.

# The Hattie Trap

You may have encountered John Hattie's work — ranking hundreds of educational interventions by effect size and suggesting that anything above 0.4 is "worth doing." This ranking is widely used in schools and has been enormously influential.

It's also misleading. Here's why:

- Averaging effect sizes across wildly different studies, populations, and contexts produces a number that doesn't describe any real situation.
- The ranking implies you can compare interventions directly: "Feedback (0.7) is better than homework (0.3)." But "feedback" in these studies might mean anything from detailed written comments to automated quiz results. "Homework" might mean anything from rote worksheets to creative projects.
- Some interventions with high average effect sizes (like "teacher credibility") are measured in ways that make them almost meaningless as guides for action.

This doesn't mean Hattie's work is useless. It means you should use it as a starting point for investigation, not as a definitive ranking. "Feedback has a high effect size" should prompt you to ask: "What kind of feedback? In what context? With what students? Is that relevant to us?"

> [!warning]
> **Don't rank by numbers alone:** An effect size of 0.4 from a rigorous, well-controlled study in a context similar to yours is worth more than an effect size of 1.2 from a poorly controlled study in a completely different context. Quality of evidence matters more than size of effect.

# Being a Critical Consumer

The goal isn't to become a research sceptic. It's to become a critical consumer. Here's the difference:

| Sceptic | Zealot | Critical consumer |
|---|---|---|
| "Research never applies to real classrooms" | "This study proves we should change everything" | "What does this evidence tell us, and what are its limitations?" |
| Rejects all evidence | Accepts all evidence uncritically | Evaluates evidence carefully |
| Makes decisions based on gut feeling | Makes decisions based on headlines | Makes decisions based on careful reading |

As a leader, you set the tone for how your team engages with research. If you wave around effect sizes without understanding them, your teachers will learn to be zealots or sceptics. If you model careful, thoughtful engagement — "Here's an interesting study. Let's look at what it actually measured and whether that applies to us" — your teachers will learn to think critically about evidence.

# Practical Application

When someone recommends a new programme or practice, run it through the five adoption questions. When someone cites research, ask:
- What exactly was studied? (Not just "reading intervention" — which specific practice, with which students, for how long?)
- How was improvement measured? (A standardised test? A researcher-designed assessment? Teacher judgment?)
- How similar is the study context to ours?
- What were the limitations the researchers themselves acknowledged?

This isn't about being difficult. It's about being responsible. Your teachers' time is limited and precious. Every minute spent on a new initiative is a minute not spent on something else. Your job is to make sure those minutes are invested well.

## Section 5: De-implementation
**Slug:** de-implementation
**Estimated Minutes:** 15

### Content

> [!tip]

# The Leadership Decision Nobody Talks About

Every leadership course teaches you how to introduce new initiatives. Almost none of them teach you the harder skill: how to STOP doing things.

Schools accumulate. New programmes are added, new meetings created, new practices mandated — year after year. But old programmes are rarely removed. Old meetings persist long after their purpose has faded. Old practices continue because "we've always done it that way."

The result is initiative overload. Teachers aren't overwhelmed by any single programme. They're overwhelmed by the TOTAL. The maths intervention programme plus the reading assessment framework plus the social-emotional learning curriculum plus the digital citizenship module plus the parent communication protocol plus the new report writing format plus, plus, plus.

Each one was added with good intentions. Each one probably has some value. But collectively, they create a situation where teachers can't do anything well because they're trying to do everything.

> [!info]
> **The accumulation trap:** Schools are much better at starting things than stopping them. Over time, this creates a crushing total load on teachers. De-implementation — deliberately stopping practices that aren't producing enough value — is one of the most important and least practised leadership skills.

# The Initiative Stocktake

Before you can decide what to stop, you need to know what you're doing. This sounds obvious, but most school leaders can't list every programme, practice, routine, and meeting that consumes teacher time. Things accumulate below the level of awareness.

The stocktake is a simple exercise. List everything that takes teacher time beyond direct teaching:

- Every programme and curriculum add-on
- Every regular meeting and its duration
- Every reporting and documentation requirement
- Every assessment that isn't directly used to improve teaching
- Every event, performance, or exhibition that requires preparation
- Every communication expectation (newsletters, parent emails, portfolios)

Be thorough. Include things that "only take a few minutes" — because twenty things that "only take a few minutes" each take hours every week.

Once you have the list, ask one question about each item: **Is this producing a meaningful improvement in student learning?** Not "is this nice to have?" or "would parents miss it?" but "is this actually making students learn more?"

# The HEAT Framework

For items that don't clearly produce learning improvements, use the HEAT framework to decide whether to keep, modify, or remove:

**H — Harm:** Is this practice actively harmful? Does it take so much time that it displaces better practices? Does it create stress without corresponding benefit? If yes, it's a strong candidate for removal.

**E — Ease:** How easy or hard is this to implement? A low-value practice that's easy to do might be worth keeping — the cost is low. A low-value practice that's hard to implement is definitely worth removing — the cost is high for little return.

**A — Acceptability:** Do teachers support this practice? Will removing it cause significant resistance? This matters not because popularity should drive decisions, but because removing something teachers value damages trust if not handled carefully.

**T — Time:** How much teacher time does this consume? A practice that takes 30 minutes a year isn't worth fighting about. A practice that takes two hours a week is a major time investment that needs to justify itself.

| HEAT score | What it means | Action |
|---|---|---|
| High harm + High time + Low acceptability | Actively damaging and time-consuming | Remove immediately |
| Low value + High time + High acceptability | Doesn't produce results but teachers like it | Remove with careful communication |
| Low value + Low time + High acceptability | Doesn't hurt much | Consider keeping — not worth the fight |
| High value + High time | Worth the investment | Keep and protect |

> [!tip]
> **Start with the obvious wins.** Every school has at least one practice that everyone knows is a waste of time but nobody has stopped. A meeting that could be an email. A report that nobody reads. A form that duplicates information from another form. Remove these first — they build momentum and credibility for harder de-implementation decisions later.

# The Political Challenge

Here's why de-implementation is a leadership challenge, not just an administrative one. Some practices exist not because they work, but because someone important started them.

The previous principal introduced the reading log programme. A respected senior teacher designed the student portfolio system. A parent committee advocated for the weekly newsletter format. Stopping any of these means implicitly saying "that wasn't as valuable as we thought" — and the person who started it may still be in the building.

This requires courage and diplomacy. Courage to make the decision. Diplomacy to communicate it respectfully. Some approaches:

- Frame it as evolution, not criticism: "This served us well, and now we're evolving our practice."
- Use evidence: "We tracked whether this was producing learning improvements, and the data shows it isn't."
- Involve teachers in the decision: "Here's the full list of what we're doing. Together, let's decide what to keep and what to let go."
- Be honest about the trade-off: "To do this new thing well, we need to stop doing something. What should go?"

# Making Room

Here's the direct connection to everything else in this course. Every new practice you want teachers to adopt requires TIME. Retrieval starters take 5 minutes of lesson time. Diagnostic questions require preparation time. TLC meetings take 75 minutes fortnightly. Peer observation takes 30 minutes per cycle.

Where does that time come from? If you add all of this without removing anything, you're asking teachers to do more with the same hours. That's a recipe for two things: burnout and shallow implementation. Teachers will technically do everything and genuinely do nothing.

De-implementation is the precondition for implementation. Before asking "what should we start doing?", ask "what should we stop doing to make room?"

> [!warning]
> **The room principle:** You cannot add without subtracting. Every hour of teacher time is already allocated. If you want teachers to invest time in a new practice, you must free up time by removing something else. Leaders who only add and never remove are the primary cause of initiative overload and teacher burnout.

## Section 6: Tracking Without Surveillance
**Slug:** tracking-without-surveillance
**Estimated Minutes:** 15

### Content

> [!tip]

# The Leader's Dilemma

You need to know if all this work — the TLCs, the peer observations, the LS courses — is actually producing results. Are teachers changing their practice? Are students learning more? Without tracking, you're investing time on faith alone.

But the moment tracking feels like surveillance, everything breaks. Teachers stop being honest about what's not working. Peer observation becomes performative. TLC meetings become about looking good instead of actually learning. The whole system collapses.

This is a genuine tension, and there's no easy resolution. But there are approaches that navigate it well.

# Logic Models: Seeing the Chain

A logic model is a simple framework that maps the chain from what you invest to what you hope to achieve:

**Inputs** → **Activities** → **Outputs** → **Outcomes**

For teacher learning at PEP, this might look like:

| Stage | What it includes | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Inputs | Time, courses, structures | LS courses, TLC time, peer observation time |
| Activities | What teachers do differently | Try retrieval starters, use diagnostic questions, give detective feedback |
| Outputs | What students experience | More retrieval practice, clearer feedback, better self-assessment |
| Outcomes | Learning improvements | Students retain more, identify their own gaps, improve on specific skills |

The logic model helps you think about WHAT to track. Most schools jump straight to outcomes (test scores, assessment results) and ignore the earlier stages. But if outcomes aren't improving, you need to know WHERE the chain is breaking. Is the problem that teachers aren't doing the activities? Or that they're doing them but students aren't responding? Or that students are responding but it isn't showing up on assessments?

> [!info]
> **Track the chain, not just the endpoint.** If you only track outcomes, you can't diagnose problems. If retrieval starters aren't improving retention, is it because teachers aren't using them (activities), or using them poorly (outputs), or using them well but your assessment doesn't measure retention (outcomes)? The logic model helps you find the weak link.

# Evidence Portfolios

An evidence portfolio is a collection of artefacts that demonstrates a teacher's professional growth over time. Not a performance portfolio for evaluation — a LEARNING portfolio that belongs to the teacher.

What goes in it:
- **Student work samples** showing changes over time (e.g., student responses to diagnostic questions at the start of the term vs. the end)
- **Self-reflections** from TLC meetings ("I tried retrieval starters this cycle. Here's what I noticed...")
- **Peer observation notes** (the brief feedback from their observation partner)
- **Diagnostic question results** showing what they learned about student thinking
- **Planning artefacts** showing how they designed retrieval activities, feedback, or assessments

What does NOT go in it:
- Formal lesson plans for inspection
- Performance review documentation
- Anything the teacher wouldn't choose to include

The portfolio is the teacher's own record of growth. They share what they want to share, when they want to share it. This makes it safe — and safety is the precondition for honesty about what's not working.

# Teaching Sprints

A teaching sprint is a short, focused improvement cycle — typically 4-6 weeks. It's more structured than the general TLC cycle and works well for teams working on the same area.

The sprint structure:
1. **Define:** The team identifies a specific improvement goal (e.g., "Students will be able to identify their own misconceptions in maths using diagnostic questions")
2. **Plan:** Teachers design their approach — what will they try, when, with which students?
3. **Act:** Teachers implement for 4-6 weeks, collecting evidence as they go
4. **Review:** The team meets to share evidence, discuss what worked, and identify what to adjust

Then start a new sprint with a new (or refined) goal.

Teaching sprints work because they're bounded. They have a clear start and end, a specific goal, and a defined evidence collection plan. They don't feel like permanent surveillance — they feel like a professional experiment. "We're trying this for six weeks and then reviewing the evidence."

> [!tip]
> **Sprints complement TLCs.** The TLC provides the ongoing rhythm (fortnightly check-ins on individual techniques). Teaching sprints provide focused bursts where a team works on a shared goal. They're not competing structures — they layer on top of each other.

# What NOT to Track

This is important: don't count implementation. Don't track how many times a teacher used a retrieval starter this week, or whether they did an exit ticket every lesson. That's surveillance, and it produces performative compliance — teachers doing the thing because they know it's being counted, not because it's helping students.

Instead, track IMPACT. Are students retaining more? Can they identify their own gaps? Are diagnostic question results showing better understanding over time? The technique is the means — the student learning is the end. Track the end.

If student learning isn't improving despite teachers implementing techniques faithfully, that's useful information too. Maybe the technique isn't right for this context. Maybe it needs to be adapted. But you can only have that productive conversation if teachers trust that they won't be penalised for honest reporting.

# Observation for Learning vs. Evaluation

We said this in Section 3, but it bears repeating because it's the most common point of failure. In many schools, the same person who conducts formal evaluations also does "learning walks" or informal observations. From the leader's perspective, these are different things. From the teacher's perspective, they're identical — someone with power watching them and making judgments.

The separation must be structural, not just verbal:
- **Different people:** If possible, the person who does peer observation should not be the person who does formal evaluation. In a small school, this is hard — but even having peer observation be genuinely between peers (not between a teacher and their direct supervisor) helps.
- **Different documentation:** Peer observation notes stay with the pair. They are never shared with leadership, never appear in a file, never inform a performance conversation.
- **Different purpose:** Stated clearly and repeatedly. "This is for your growth. I will never ask what your observer saw."

## Section 7: Building a Feedback Culture for Adults
**Slug:** feedback-culture-for-adults
**Estimated Minutes:** 15

### Content

> [!tip]
> **Quick retrieval:** In Section 6, we distinguished between tracking implementation and tracking impact. Why is counting how many times a teacher uses a technique a poor form of tracking? Recall before reading on.

# Everything You Learned in LS 301 Applies Here

In LS 301, you studied feedback in depth — what makes it effective, what makes it fail, how to give feedback that moves learning forward. Here's the key insight for this section: everything that works for student feedback works for adult feedback too. And everything that fails for student feedback fails for adult feedback too.

Vague praise ("Great lesson!") is as useless for a teacher as "Great job!" is for a student. Overwhelming criticism is as counterproductive for an experienced colleague as it is for a struggling learner. Feedback without trust is dismissed by adults just as readily as by children.

The principles transfer directly. But the application requires adaptation, because the power dynamics and emotional stakes are different with adults.

# Wise Feedback for Teachers

In LS 301, you learned about wise feedback — a frame developed by researchers studying how feedback can be heard across differences in status, background, and trust:

> "I'm giving you this feedback because I have high standards for our team and I believe you can meet them."

This frame has two components: **high standards** and **high belief**. It communicates: "I'm not picking on you. I'm pushing you because you're capable of more." Research shows that this frame dramatically increases the likelihood that critical feedback is heard, accepted, and acted upon.

For teachers, this sounds like:

- "I watched your retrieval starter, and I want to push you on it because I know you can make it stronger. The questions were good, but the wait time was too short — try counting to five silently before taking answers."
- "Your diagnostic question set was thoughtful. The distractors were well-designed. One thing that would make it even better: track which distractors each student chose, because that tells you which misconceptions to address next."

Notice the pattern: specific strength, specific push, high belief. Not "good job" and not "here's everything wrong."

> [!info]
> **The wise feedback formula for adults:** Specific acknowledgment of what works + specific identification of what to improve + the frame "I'm pushing you because I believe you can do this and our standards demand it." This works for 8-year-olds writing stories AND for experienced teachers refining their practice.

# The Detective Principle for Coaching

In LS 301, detective feedback means giving students enough information to find and fix the problem themselves, rather than correcting it for them. The same principle applies to coaching teachers.

**Telling:** "Your wait time is too short. Count to five before taking answers."

**Detective:** "I noticed that the same three students answered every retrieval question. What do you think was happening for the quieter students? What might help them participate?"

The detective approach is harder and slower. But it produces deeper learning because the teacher does the thinking. When you tell a teacher what to change, they change it — but they haven't built the skill of diagnosing their own practice. When you ask questions that help them diagnose it themselves, they build that capacity.

This doesn't mean you never tell. Sometimes — especially when a teacher is completely stuck — a direct suggestion is the right move. But the default should be questions, not instructions.

Good detective coaching questions:
- "What did you notice about student responses when you tried that?"
- "Which students were and weren't engaged? What was different about the two groups?"
- "If you were going to do that again tomorrow, what one thing would you change?"
- "What surprised you about how that went?"

# Normalising Professional Vulnerability

The best learning cultures — for adults and children — are ones where people openly share what ISN'T working. Not just successes. Not just polished results. The messy, uncomfortable, "I tried this and it flopped" stories.

As a leader, you create this culture or you destroy it. And the primary tool is modelling. If you want teachers to share their failures, you go first.

"I tried using a new meeting structure last week. It was supposed to give everyone more voice, but it actually made the discussion more scattered and we ran out of time. Here's what I learned and what I'll do differently."

That's vulnerability. It's uncomfortable. And it's enormously powerful. When the leader — the person with the most status and the most to lose — admits failure openly, it signals that failure is safe. That it's expected. That it's how learning works.

If, on the other hand, the leader only shares successes and presents a polished, confident exterior, teachers learn that failure is to be hidden. And hidden failure means nobody learns from it.

> [!warning]
> **The vulnerability asymmetry:** The leader must go first. Teachers will not share their failures with someone who never shares their own. And teachers will NEVER share their failures with someone who uses those failures against them. Modelling vulnerability and protecting vulnerability are both leadership responsibilities.

# Separating Development from Evaluation

This is the hardest challenge in building a feedback culture for teachers. Development and evaluation serve different purposes and require fundamentally different conditions:

| | Development | Evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Growth and improvement | Accountability and standards |
| **Tone** | Exploratory, honest, curious | Summative, definitive |
| **What the teacher says** | "Here's what's not working" | "Here's what's going well" |
| **Trust required** | Very high | Moderate |
| **Power dynamic** | Collegial | Hierarchical |

When these two are mixed — when the same conversation, the same person, or the same system tries to do both — teachers will always default to the evaluation mode. They'll present their best work, hide their struggles, and tell you what you want to hear. The development conversation dies.

In a small school, complete separation is hard. The same people often play multiple roles. But you can at least separate the CONVERSATIONS: "Right now, this is a development conversation. Nothing you say here will appear in any evaluation. I'm asking so I can help, not so I can judge."

And then — as with peer observation — you must keep that promise consistently.

# The Components

Your PD architecture should include each of these components:

**1. The LS course sequence**
Which courses will your team complete, in what order, by when? The logical sequence:
- LS 101 (the foundation — this comes first for everyone)
- LS 201 + 202 (these can run in parallel or in sequence — 201 provides theory, 202 provides assessment tools)
- LS 301 (feedback — builds on everything in 201 and 202)
- LS 401 (advanced practice — for teachers who've completed the foundations)

Not everyone needs to be on the same course at the same time. Stagger the sequence so you can provide support and discussion partners at each level.

**2. TLC structure**
- How often? (Fortnightly, 75 minutes — as established in Section 2)
- Who's in which group? (If you have more than 12 teachers, you'll need two groups)
- What's the agenda template? (The structured format from Section 2)
- Who facilitates? (This can rotate, but someone must own the structure)

**3. Peer observation pairs**
- Who observes whom? (Cross-level where possible — Montessori paired with middle school)
- How often? (Once per TLC cycle — so fortnightly)
- What's the debrief structure? (15 minutes, focused on the one technique)
- How will you protect trust? (Complete separation from evaluation)

**4. De-implementation plan**
- What will you stop doing to make room? (You can't add all of this without removing something)
- How will you run the initiative stocktake?
- Which items are candidates for HEAT evaluation?

**5. Tracking and evidence**
- What evidence will you collect? (Teaching sprints? Portfolios? Both?)
- What will you track at each level of the logic model?
- What will you explicitly NOT track?

**6. Communication plan**
- How will you share progress with teachers? (Celebrate growth, share what's working)
- How will you communicate to parents? (What is the school investing in and why?)
- How will you report to leadership? (Evidence of impact, not just implementation)

> [!info]
> **The architecture metaphor:** You're not building a single wall — you're designing a building. Each component (courses, TLCs, peer observation, de-implementation, tracking, communication) is a structural element. If one is missing, the building is unstable. If they don't connect to each other, the building doesn't hold together. Your job is to make sure every piece is in place and every piece connects.

# The One-Year Arc

Here's a realistic timeline for a school like PEP, where the workshop series and online courses are already underway:

**Term 1: Foundation**
- All teachers complete LS 101 (if they haven't already)
- TLCs established: fortnightly meetings begin with the structured agenda
- Peer observation pairs formed (voluntary first — let enthusiastic pairs model it)
- De-implementation stocktake: identify what to stop doing
- Each teacher chooses ONE technique from LS 101 to focus on

**Term 2: Depth**
- Teachers move to LS 201 and/or 202
- Peer observation becomes routine for all pairs
- First teaching sprint: a focused 4-6 week cycle on a shared goal
- Evidence portfolios begin: teachers start collecting artefacts
- De-implementation actions: at least one low-value practice removed

**Term 3: Application**
- Teachers ready for LS 301 (or 401 for those further ahead)
- Teaching sprint reviews: what did the evidence show?
- TLCs now well-established — the rhythm is automatic
- Evidence review: what's improving? Where are the gaps?
- Plan for Year 2: what needs to continue, what needs to adjust?

> [!tip]
> **Don't front-load.** It's tempting to launch everything in Term 1. Resist. Start with the essentials (LS 101, TLCs, one technique) and layer on complexity gradually. This follows the same small-steps principle from Section 3 — if you launch five components at once, all five will be done poorly.

# Connect to What PEP Is Already Doing

This architecture isn't being built from nothing. The workshop series introduced the ideas. The online LS courses provide the content. The peer accountability pairs from the workshop started the observation habit. The commitment to try one thing started the technique focus.

Your PD architecture formalises and extends what's already started. It gives it structure, rhythm, and permanence. It takes the energy from the workshop and channels it into something sustainable.

The difference between a school that changed temporarily and a school that changed permanently is this: the first had a great workshop. The second built a system.

# The Leader's Commitment

Everything in this course — every section, every structure, every principle — depends on one thing: a leader who follows through. Who protects the TLC time when the schedule gets tight. Who maintains the agenda when conversations drift. Who models vulnerability when it feels risky. Who holds the standard when it's easier to let things slide.

The system doesn't run itself. It runs because someone — you — makes it run. That's the commitment:

- I will protect this time.
- I will maintain the structure.
- I will model the learning — taking an LS course myself, trying techniques, sharing what works and what doesn't.
- I will celebrate the growth — naming specific improvements, not just offering generic praise.
- I will keep going when the initial enthusiasm fades — because structures persist long after motivation fluctuates.

> "The role of the leader is not to come up with all the great ideas. The role of the leader is to create an environment in which great ideas can happen."
> — Simon Sinek

This is your environment to build. The ideas are already here — in the research, in the courses, in your teachers' daily practice. Your job is to build the structure that lets those ideas take root and grow.
