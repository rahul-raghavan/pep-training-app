What Is Learning?

Case 1 · The Revision Surprise

You've been working with a group of students on equivalent fractions. Over several lessons, you used manipulatives, worked through problems together, and gave them practice. By the end of the week, students were solving equivalent fraction problems confidently.

Three weeks later, you revisit fractions. More than half the group stares blankly. They can't do what they were doing fluently just three weeks ago.

At your table:
  1. What happened? Why did they "forget"?
  2. Does this happen in your classroom?
  3. Whose fault is it?
4
Performance is not the same as learning.
  • Performance = what students can do right now, with everything fresh
  • Learning = what they can still do weeks later, independently
  • A smooth lesson can produce no durable learning
5
Key Actionable Takeaway

If you want to know whether students have actually learned something, check again later — next week, next month. The way you structure practice between now and then determines whether learning sticks.

6
Case 2 · The Practice Puzzle

Four students are preparing for a presentation on the solar system:

Student A — reading notes for the third time, highlighting key sentences

Student B — notes put away, writing down everything she can remember, then checking what she missed

Student C — made a beautiful colour-coded mind map, keeps admiring it

Student D — mixing topics: recalling Mars facts, then a maths problem from yesterday, back to the solar system, then vocabulary from last week

At your table:

Rank these students from most effective to least effective preparation.

Be ready to defend your ranking.

7
The ranking:
  1. Student B — retrieval practice (pulling from memory IS learning)
  2. Student D — interleaved practice (mixing forces discrimination)
  3. Student A — re-reading (feels productive, barely works)
  4. Student C — beautiful notes (effort went into formatting, not thinking)
8
Students are reliably wrong about what works for their own learning.
  • Re-reading feels best → weakest results
  • Retrieval practice feels hardest → strongest results
9
Key Actionable Takeaway

This is exactly what Mindspark does in maths and English — retrieval practice, spaced over time, at the right difficulty level. Now you know why we use it. In all subjects: closing the book and recalling from memory beats re-reading every time.

Case 3 · The Packed Lesson

A teacher introduces ecosystems in a single lesson:

  • Explains 6 new vocabulary words
  • Reads aloud a passage about rainforests
  • Students draw and label a food chain
  • Students write a paragraph comparing rainforest and desert ecosystems
  • Finishes with "name one thing you learned"

Students are busy the whole time. Two days later, most can only say "it's about animals and plants."

At your table:
  1. Rank the five activities from most to least valuable.
  2. If you could only keep TWO, which would you keep and why?
11
Working memory holds 3–5 new items. That's it.
  • When overloaded, students don't learn less — they learn almost nothing
  • This isn't a flaw in students. It's how every brain works.
  • Intrinsic load (real complexity) vs extraneous load (poor presentation)
12
Key Actionable Takeaway

Before your next lesson, count: how many genuinely new things am I introducing? If it's more than 3–4, something needs to move to the next day. Cover less, they learn more.

13
Case 4 · Collaboration — When It Works

Four groups are working on a research project about civilisations:

Group 1 — One strong student does everything. Others watch.

Group 2 — Each person does a separate part alone. They staple them together. No one reads anyone else's section.

Group 3 — Students discuss together, challenge each other's understanding, build a shared explanation richer than any one person could produce.

Group 4 — Two students argue about formatting. Most time spent on logistics. Content is thin.

At your table:
  1. Rank the groups from most learning to least learning.
  2. What made the difference?
14
Sitting together does not mean learning together.
  • Social loafing — some coast, others carry
  • Divide-and-ignore — looks collaborative, no shared thinking
  • Structure matters: individual accountability, conceptually rich tasks, taught collaboration skills
15
Key Actionable Takeaway

Add one rule: every individual must be able to explain the group's answer. Call on a random student — not the spokesperson. If one person can't explain it, the group hasn't succeeded.

16

Theme 1: What we've uncovered

  1. Performance ≠ learning — looking good today doesn't mean it stuck
  2. Students choose strategies that feel best, not strategies that work best
  3. Working memory is tiny — cover less, they learn more
  4. Collaboration needs design — sitting together ≠ learning together

Case 5 · Three Ways to Teach the Same Thing

Three teachers introduce a new concept to a group of students who are new to it.

Teacher A — brief explanation, then "work through these problems, I'll come around to help." Students are busy. Some figure it out; many spin their wheels.

Teacher B — solves 3 problems thinking aloud, then students try in pairs, then independently with increasing difficulty.

Teacher C — thorough explanation with detailed notes, students copy into notebooks. Notebooks look neat.

A month later, all three groups get a new problem applying the same concept.

At your table:
  1. Rank the three teachers — whose students perform best on the new problem a month later?

Be ready to defend your ranking.

20
Novices need to see before they do.
  1. Teacher B — model → guide → release (the worked example effect)
  2. Teacher A — valuable for students who already have the basics (not novices)
  3. Teacher C — copying notes is transcribing, not thinking
21
The expertise reversal effect:
  • What helps a novice hurts an expert.
  • Scaffolding that beginners need becomes counterproductive once expertise develops.
  • The art is knowing when to shift.
22
Key Actionable Takeaway

Before independent practice, solve 2–3 problems in front of students while thinking aloud. Narrate your decisions. Then do a few together. Then let them try alone. The sequence matters.

23
Case 6 · "Does Everyone Understand?"

You're working with a group of 8 students on adjectives. You've explained the concept, given examples, done practice sentences together. You ask: "Does everyone understand?"

A few nod. One says "Yes." Two stay quiet. You move on.

Next day: three of the eight can't identify adjectives in new sentences — including one who nodded.

At your table:
  1. What went wrong with "Does everyone understand?"
  2. Even with only 8 students, why didn't you catch the confusion?
  3. What could you do instead?
24
"Does everyone understand?" gives you no useful information — even in a small group.
  • Nodding and understanding are not the same thing
  • Formative assessment = evidence used to decide what to do next
  • You need every student to produce a response
25
Key Actionable Takeaway

Tomorrow, where you'd normally ask "Does everyone understand?", have everyone write their answer instead. Look at all responses before you move on.

26
Case 7 · The Diagnostic Question

You're working with students on area and perimeter. You write this question:

"A farmer has a rectangular field, 8m long and 5m wide. He wants to build a fence around it. How much fencing does he need?"

A) 40 metres    B) 26 metres    C) 13 metres    D) 80 metres

Every student writes their answer.

At your table:
  1. What does each answer tell you about the student's thinking?
  2. Why is this better than just asking "What's the perimeter?"
  3. What would you do if most students chose A?
27
A diagnostic question: every wrong answer reveals a specific misunderstanding.
  • A (40m) = found area, not perimeter — confusing the two concepts
  • B (26m) = correct
  • C (13m) = only added two sides — partial understanding
  • D (80m) = computation error
28
Your EI ASSET assessments work on this exact principle.
  • Diagnostic questions designed to reveal misconceptions, not just right/wrong
  • The difference: EI does it once a year. You can do it every lesson.
  • "Don't collect evidence to admire it. Collect evidence to decide."
29
Key Actionable Takeaway

Think about a concept you're teaching this week where students get confused. Design one question where each wrong answer tells you something specific. Try it tomorrow.

30
Case 8 · Two Lesson Plans

Teacher A — detailed plan: intro, explanation, exploration, recording. Follows it exactly. Lesson flows smoothly. Covers everything.

Teacher B — same plan, but builds in three checkpoints. At each, she asks a diagnostic question. If they get it → move on. If not → backup activity. Her lesson looks messier. She skips her last activity to re-teach a key term.

At your table:
  1. Rank the two teachers — whose students remember more a week later?
  2. And: which lesson would look "better" to an observer?
31
Students do not always learn what we teach.
  • Teacher A delivered content. Teacher B taught students.
  • The "messy" lesson is the learning.
  • The smooth lesson might look better to an observer — but coverage is not teaching.
32
Key Actionable Takeaway

In your next lesson, identify one moment where understanding could break down. Plan a 2-minute check. Plan what you'll do if the answer isn't what you hoped. One checkpoint. One backup plan.

Theme 2: What we've added

  1. Novices need modelling before independence
  2. "Does everyone understand?" tells you nothing — even with 8 students
  3. Diagnostic questions reveal thinking, not just answers
  4. Build Plan B into Plan A — the best lessons have decision points

Case 9 · The Three Feedback Approaches

Three teachers review the same student writing — a report with a strong opening but weak evidence in the middle section.

Teacher A — puts a tick, writes "Good work, keep it up!"

Teacher B — writes detailed comments on every paragraph

Teacher C — writes: "Your opening drew me in. One paragraph makes a claim without evidence. Find it, add the evidence, bring it to me by Thursday."

At your table:
  1. Rank the three approaches — which produces the most improvement in the student's next piece of work?
  2. Which produces the least?
37
Feedback is a task, not a verdict.
  1. Teacher C — specific praise + detective task + deadline (3 minutes, most learning)
  2. Teacher B — detailed but teacher did all the thinking (10 minutes, less learning)
  3. Teacher A — no information, no action (least learning)
38
No grades doesn't mean no feedback.
  • It means feedback matters more — it's the only mechanism for signalling quality and next steps.
  • Without it, students have no mirror.
39
Key Actionable Takeaway

Next time you review student work: identify the one most important thing to improve. Turn it into a task — not a correction. Make it specific enough to locate, open enough to think. Set a deadline.

Case 10 · The Metacognition Puzzle

A student says: "I practised a lot but I still couldn't do it."

You ask how she practised. She says: "I read through all my notes. I even copied the key points. I spent a whole hour."

She's not lying. She really did spend an hour. She really did feel prepared.

At your table:
  1. What went wrong? She clearly put in effort.
  2. How would you help her prepare differently?
  3. Do the students in your classroom know how to practise effectively?
41
This is a metacognition problem, not a motivation problem.
  • Metacognition = thinking about your own thinking
  • She can't tell the difference between "I've seen this" and "I can do this"
  • Students are reliably poor at judging their own learning
42
Key Actionable Takeaway

Three questions, every day, built into the work:
Before: "What's your plan?"
During: "Is it working? Do you need to change anything?"
After: "What went well? What would you do differently?"

43
Case 11 · The Feedback That Builds Trust

Three teachers respond to a student named Aarav who submitted weak work:

Teacher A — "I can see you put in some effort. Nice attempt. Let me know if you need help."

Teacher B — "This isn't your best work. I've seen what you're capable of. Redo it and take it seriously."

Teacher C — "I'm giving you this feedback because I hold you to a high standard and I believe you can meet it. Your opening shows you understand the question. But your second section makes a claim without backing it up. Find one piece of evidence and rewrite that section. Bring it to me by Thursday."

At your table:
  1. Rank the three approaches — which is most likely to get Aarav to improve?
  2. Which builds the strongest relationship over time?
44
Wise feedback: high standards + belief the student can meet them.
  1. Teacher C — specific, actionable, trust-building
  2. Teacher B — well-intentioned but no guidance on what to fix
  3. Teacher A — feels kind, but signals low expectations
45
Key Actionable Takeaway

When work falls short: "I'm giving you this feedback because I expect a lot from you and I believe you can deliver. Here's specifically what needs to change, and here's what I want you to do about it."

46
Case 12 · The Knowing-Doing Gap

You attend a fantastic professional development session. You learn about retrieval practice, spacing, formative assessment. You take notes. You feel energised.

Week 1: you try a few new things.
Week 2: things get busy. You fall back to usual routines.
Week 4: you're teaching exactly as before. Notes are in a bag somewhere.

Research shows this is what happens with most PD.

At your table:
  1. Why does this happen?
  2. What would need to be different for change to stick?
  3. What support would you need — honestly?
47
Knowing is not doing.
  • One-shot PD changes almost nothing long-term
  • What works: small steps (one technique at a time), peer accountability, observation for learning not evaluation
  • The same principles that apply to your students apply to you
