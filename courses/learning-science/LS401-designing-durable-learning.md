## Section 1: The Two-Phase Design Model
**Slug:** two-phase-design-model
**Estimated Minutes:** 15

### Content

# The Problem With "Teach It and Move On"

Here's the pattern that happens in almost every school: a teacher plans a good lesson, explains the concept clearly, checks that students seem to understand, and moves on to the next topic. The lesson went well. The students got it. Everyone's satisfied.

Two weeks later, the students have forgotten most of it.

This isn't a failure of the lesson. The lesson was fine. The failure is in what happened — or didn't happen — after the lesson. Understanding something in the moment is not the same as remembering it long-term. And yet most of our planning energy goes into that first moment of understanding, with very little thought given to what makes it last.

This is the central problem this course will solve.

# Two Phases of Learning Design

Every piece of learning you design needs two phases, and both are equally important.

**Phase 1: Help students UNDERSTAND.** This is where you manage cognitive load — presenting in small steps, modelling your thinking, guiding practice, checking understanding, providing scaffolds. This is Rosenshine's principles and cognitive load theory in action. You did this well in LS 201 and 202.

**Phase 2: Make it STICK.** This is where you build durable memory — retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, independent practice until the skill is automatic. You learned about these tools in LS 101 and 201. Now you need to plan them deliberately into every unit you teach.

Most teachers focus heavily on Phase 1 and neglect Phase 2. We teach it, students seem to get it, and we move on. But without Phase 2, the learning fades. It's like planting seeds and never watering them.

| Phase | Goal | Tools | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phase 1: Understand | Build initial understanding | Small steps, modelling, guided practice, checking understanding, scaffolds | During the lesson |
| Phase 2: Stick | Build durable long-term memory | Retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, independent practice | After the lesson — days, weeks, months |

> [!warning]
> **The most common design mistake:** Spending all your planning time on Phase 1 (the lesson itself) and assuming that if students understood it today, they'll remember it next month. They won't — not without Phase 2. Understanding is necessary but not sufficient for durable learning.

# What This Looks Like in Our Schools

**In elementary (Montessori):** Phase 1 is the three-period lesson and guided practice with materials. The teacher presents the concept, the student works with materials under guidance, and understanding is established. Phase 2 is the work cycle and structured retrieval across days and weeks — returning to the concept with retrieval practice, spaced review, and eventually working without the materials.

**In middle school:** Phase 1 is the lesson — explain, model, guide. The teacher presents in small steps, thinks aloud while solving problems, and guides students through initial practice. Phase 2 is independent practice plus spaced review in subsequent lessons — retrieval starters, cumulative quizzes, interleaved problem sets that bring back old material alongside new.

In both cases, Phase 2 is where most of the forgetting prevention happens. And in both cases, it's the phase that gets the least planning attention.

# The Lesson Design Template

Here's a simple template you can use for every lesson or unit. Three questions:

1. **What am I teaching?** Be specific. Not "fractions" but "comparing fractions with different denominators." Not "ecosystems" but "how energy flows through a food chain."

2. **How will I present it? (Phase 1)** What are the small steps? Where will I model my thinking? What guided practice will I do before releasing students to work independently? How will I check understanding?

3. **How will I make it stick? (Phase 2)** When will students retrieve this — tomorrow? Next week? In a month? What format — retrieval starter, quiz, interleaved practice? How will I space the review?

Most teachers plan questions 1 and 2 carefully and skip question 3 entirely. This course will help you make question 3 automatic.

> [!info]
> **The template in one line:** What am I teaching? → How will I present it? → How will I make it stick? If you can't answer the third question for every topic you teach, you have a Phase 2 gap.

# The Two Teachers

Consider two teachers who both teach the same topic to a small group of 8 students.

**Teacher A** plans a brilliant Phase 1. Beautiful materials, clear modelling, step-by-step guided practice, checks understanding at every stage. Students leave the session with solid understanding. But Teacher A doesn't plan any Phase 2 — no retrieval practice, no spaced review, no interleaving. The topic is taught and considered "done."

**Teacher B** plans an adequate Phase 1. Clear enough, but not remarkable. Students leave with reasonable understanding — not as polished as Teacher A's students. But Teacher B plans deliberate Phase 2: a retrieval starter two days later, a brief review the following week, and an interleaved problem set three weeks later.

A month later, Teacher B's students remember significantly more than Teacher A's students. The adequate lesson with strong follow-through beats the brilliant lesson with no follow-through. Every time.

This is what the research consistently shows. Phase 2 matters more than Phase 1 for long-term retention. Both matter, but if you have to choose where to invest more planning time, invest in Phase 2.

# Retrieval as a Daily Habit

You learned in LS 101 that retrieval practice is one of the most powerful learning strategies we know. Every time students pull information from memory, they strengthen the memory trace. But knowing that retrieval works isn't enough — you need to build it into the fabric of your teaching so it happens every single session, without requiring extra planning each time.

The key insight: retrieval practice should be a ROUTINE, not an event. Not something you do occasionally when you remember. Something that happens at the start of every session, automatically, like taking attendance. When it's a routine, you don't have to plan it fresh each time — the format stays the same, only the content changes.

# Four Retrieval Formats That Work

**1. Free recall (brain dump).** Students write down everything they remember about a topic for 3 minutes. No notes, no hints. Then they check against their materials and mark what they missed. The checking step is essential — it shows students what they've forgotten and makes that forgotten material easier to retrieve next time.

**2. Cued recall (specific questions).** Three to five specific questions at the start of the session. "What are the three types of rock?" "What does the word 'denominator' mean?" "Name two causes of the water cycle." Students write answers, then self-check. Quick, focused, takes 3-5 minutes.

**3. Retrieval grids.** A table with topics across the top and rows for different sessions. Each session, students fill in what they remember about each topic — from memory. They check and correct. The same grid gets revisited over weeks, so students can see their own growth. This is especially powerful because students see the topics they keep forgetting.

| | Fractions | Water Cycle | Story Structure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Week 1 | What I remember... | What I remember... | What I remember... |
| Week 3 | What I remember... | What I remember... | What I remember... |
| Week 6 | What I remember... | What I remember... | What I remember... |

**4. Low-stakes quizzing.** Three to five questions, written or verbal. Not graded. Students answer, then immediately self-check. The point isn't the score — it's the act of retrieval. In our no-grades environment, this works naturally because there's no anxiety about "failing" a quiz. It's just a thinking exercise.

> [!info]
> **The format doesn't matter as much as the consistency.** Any of these four formats will work. What matters is that you pick one (or two) and do it EVERY session. The routine is the power — not the specific format.

# Making It Routine

The biggest mistake teachers make with retrieval practice is changing the format every week. One week it's a brain dump. Next week it's a quiz. The week after it's a game. This wastes cognitive load on understanding the new format instead of spending it on the actual retrieval.

Pick a format. Stick with it. Do it at the start of every session, in the same way, for at least a half-term. Students should know exactly what to expect: "We start with our retrieval questions. Always." The routine becomes automatic, and all the mental effort goes into the content — which is exactly where you want it.

Here's what a consistent retrieval routine looks like in practice:

- **Elementary:** "Take out your retrieval notebook. Write three things you remember from our last session. You have 2 minutes. Then we'll check." Same format, every session.
- **Middle school:** "Here are today's three retrieval questions on the board. Write your answers. You have 3 minutes. Then we'll go through them together." Same format, every lesson.

# The Checking Step

Retrieval practice without checking is incomplete. The checking step is where students discover what they've forgotten — and that discovery is itself a learning moment. When a student writes "I can't remember the third type of rock" and then checks and finds it's "metamorphic," that moment of correction strengthens the memory more than if the student had simply re-read it.

Make the checking step explicit. Don't just say "check your answers." Say: "Mark anything you got wrong or missed. Those are the things you need to focus on. The things you forgot are the most important things — because now you've retrieved them, you're much more likely to remember them next time."

# The Mindspark Connection

Mindspark already does retrieval practice automatically for maths and English. It serves questions on previously learned topics, spaces them over time, and adapts difficulty based on performance. For those subjects, the retrieval routine is built in.

But for every other subject — science, social studies, languages, art — you need to build retrieval routines manually. The principles are the same: retrieve from memory, check, correct, repeat at increasing intervals. You're just doing by hand what Mindspark does algorithmically.

> [!tip]
> **Practical tip:** For subjects covered by Mindspark (maths and English), let Mindspark handle the retrieval. For everything else, build a manual retrieval routine using one of the four formats above. Don't duplicate effort — focus your manual retrieval work where there's no automated system to do it.

# From Theory to Planning

In LS 101 and 201, you learned WHY spacing works — distributing practice over time produces dramatically better long-term retention than massing practice together. That's the theory. Now we need to turn it into something practical: actually planning spaced review into your term's curriculum.

Most teachers plan a term as a sequence: Topic A for two weeks, then Topic B for two weeks, then Topic C. Each topic is taught, practised, and then left behind as the next topic begins. This is the "cover and move on" model, and it's a recipe for forgetting.

The alternative is to plan not just WHEN you'll teach each topic, but WHEN you'll retrieve it afterward. Every topic gets a teaching window AND a retrieval schedule.

# The Spacing Calendar

A spacing calendar is a simple planning tool. It's a grid where you map out when each topic will be revisited after it's first taught. The intervals should expand: a brief retrieval soon after teaching (1-2 days), then again after a week, then after 2-3 weeks, then after a month or more.

Here's what a spacing calendar looks like for one subject over a term:

| Topic | Taught | Retrieve +2 days | Retrieve +1 week | Retrieve +3 weeks | Retrieve +6 weeks |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Food chains | Week 1 | Week 1 | Week 2 | Week 4 | Week 7 |
| Water cycle | Week 2 | Week 2 | Week 3 | Week 5 | Week 8 |
| Habitats | Week 4 | Week 4 | Week 5 | Week 7 | Week 10 |

Notice something important: by Week 7, students are retrieving food chains (from Week 1), water cycle (from Week 2), AND working on new material. This is cumulative review, and it's the engine of durable learning.

> [!info]
> **You don't need to re-TEACH old material.** Spaced retrieval isn't re-teaching. It's a 3-minute retrieval starter. "Write down the steps of the water cycle from memory." That's it. Three minutes, from memory, then check. The retrieval does the memory strengthening — not another full lesson.

# How to Build a Spacing Calendar

Step 1: List all the topics you'll teach this term. Be specific — not "fractions" but "comparing fractions" and "adding fractions with like denominators."

Step 2: For each topic, schedule 3-4 retrieval touchpoints at expanding intervals. Start with a short gap (1-2 days), then widen (1 week, 3 weeks, 6 weeks).

Step 3: Look at each week and see which old topics are due for retrieval. This becomes the content for your retrieval starters.

Step 4: Don't try to retrieve everything every week. Two to three old topics per week in your retrieval routine is plenty. Rotate through them.

# Cumulative Review

The most powerful form of spacing is cumulative review — instead of only reviewing what you just taught, include material from several weeks ago. Even 5 minutes of cumulative retrieval per session makes a large difference over a term.

In practice, this means your retrieval starters should include a mix:
- One question from the most recent lesson (1-2 days ago)
- One question from a few weeks ago
- One question from early in the term

This mix is easy to plan if you have a spacing calendar. You just look at what's "due" for each week.

> [!warning]
> **The "cover and move on" trap:** The most common objection to spacing is: "I don't have time to go back — I have too much curriculum to cover." Here's the research response: you can't afford NOT to go back. If students forget 80% of what you taught last month, then you didn't really "cover" it — you just talked about it. Better to teach slightly less content and ensure students actually retain it, than to rush through everything and have them remember almost nothing.

# The Practical Constraint

You don't need a 30-minute review session. You need 3-5 minutes at the start of each session devoted to retrieving old material. That's it. The retrieval routine you designed in Section 2 is the vehicle for your spacing calendar. The spacing calendar tells you WHAT to retrieve each day. The retrieval routine tells you HOW.

For elementary teachers: your retrieval starters during circle time or the start of a work cycle can pull from the spacing calendar. "Before we begin, let's remember: what were the three types of rocks we learned about two weeks ago?"

For middle school teachers: your daily retrieval questions at the start of each lesson can be drawn from the spacing calendar. Three questions — one from yesterday, one from two weeks ago, one from last month.

# A Worked Example

Imagine you teach science to a middle school group of 8 students. Your term topics are: food chains (Week 1-2), water cycle (Week 3-4), habitats (Week 5-6), human body systems (Week 7-8).

Without spacing: by Week 7, your students haven't thought about food chains for 5 weeks. Most of it is gone.

With spacing: your retrieval starters in Week 7 include questions on food chains, water cycle, and habitats alongside the current topic. Each takes 1-2 minutes. The act of retrieval keeps all four topics alive in memory while you teach the new material.

The total extra time investment: about 3-5 minutes per session. The payoff: dramatically better retention across the entire term.

# When to Interleave and When Not To

In LS 201, you learned that interleaving — mixing different types of problems or topics during practice — produces better learning than blocked practice (doing one type at a time). But there's an important nuance that determines when interleaving helps and when it actually hurts.

**Do NOT interleave brand-new content.** When students are first learning a concept, they need blocked practice to build a basic schema. If you mix addition, subtraction, and multiplication in a student's very first encounter with multiplication, you'll create confusion. The student doesn't yet have a stable understanding of multiplication to compare and contrast with other operations. At this stage, the cognitive load of switching between types overwhelms the learning benefit.

**DO interleave during consolidation.** Once students have basic competence in each individual topic — they can do addition, subtraction, AND multiplication with reasonable accuracy on their own — then mixing them together is powerful. The discrimination work (figuring out which strategy to use for each problem) is where the deep learning happens.

The rule of thumb: blocked practice first to build the skill, interleaved practice second to deepen and consolidate it.

> [!info]
> **The interleaving sweet spot:** Students need enough competence in each individual topic to attempt them independently, but not so much practice that the skill is fully automatic. That middle zone — "I can do each one, but I have to think about which approach to use" — is where interleaving does its best work.

# Subject-Specific Examples

**Maths.** Instead of 20 addition problems followed by 20 subtraction problems, mix them: addition, subtraction, addition, multiplication, subtraction. Students have to identify the operation each time, not just repeat the same procedure. This is where the Mindspark opportunity lies — you can set up interleaved assignments that mix topic types rather than blocking them.

**Writing.** Instead of three weeks on persuasive writing followed by three weeks on narrative writing, mix the prompts once students have basic competence in each type. "Today's prompt could be persuasive, narrative, or descriptive — read it carefully and decide which approach to use." The decision-making is the learning.

**Science.** After teaching different body systems separately, give students problems that require identifying which system is relevant. "A person eats food and then runs a race. Which body systems are involved and how do they interact?" This requires discriminating between systems, not just recalling facts about one.

**Social studies.** After teaching about different historical periods or civilisations, compare them. "What was similar and different about how these two civilisations governed themselves?" Comparison requires the kind of deep processing that builds understanding.

# The Mindspark Opportunity

Mindspark has a feature that most teachers haven't fully explored: the ability to create interleaved assignments. Instead of assigning a block of problems on one topic, you can create mixed problem sets that pull from multiple topics students have already learned.

This is an untapped opportunity. Most Mindspark assignments are set up as blocked practice — "do 20 problems on decimals." But the platform can mix decimals, fractions, percentages, and basic operations into a single assignment. The student has to figure out what kind of problem they're looking at before solving it — and that figuring-out step is where interleaving does its work.

For English, the same principle applies: mix reading comprehension passages on different topics, or mix grammar exercises that require identifying the relevant rule.

> [!tip]
> **Practical tip:** When setting up Mindspark assignments, try creating one interleaved assignment per week that mixes 3-4 topics students have already practised individually. Start with topics they're comfortable with. Watch how performance dips initially (because it's harder) but improves over time (because the learning is deeper).

# The Student Resistance Problem

Interleaving feels harder than blocking. Students will notice. They may complain: "This is confusing. Why can't we just do one type at a time?"

This is actually a good sign. The difficulty they're feeling is desirable difficulty — it's the kind of effort that strengthens learning. But students don't know that. They associate effort with failure, and ease with learning (the fluency trap from LS 101).

Your job is to explain why you're doing it. You might say: "I know this feels harder. That's because your brain has to decide which approach to use each time, instead of just repeating the same one. That decision-making is what helps you really learn it — so that next month, you'll still remember how to do each type."

Transparency works. When students understand why the practice feels harder, they're more likely to persist through the discomfort.

# What NOT to Interleave

Not everything benefits from interleaving. Some practical guidelines:

- **Don't interleave topics that are too similar.** If students can't tell the topics apart even with blocked practice, interleaving will just confuse them further. They need clearer understanding first.
- **Don't interleave topics where one is a prerequisite for another.** If students need to master addition before they can do multiplication, interleaving them before addition is solid will undermine both.
- **Don't interleave the format of the retrieval routine.** As we discussed in Section 2, the routine should stay consistent. Interleave the CONTENT (which topics you retrieve), not the FORMAT (how you retrieve).

## Section 5: The Release Sequence

**Estimated Minutes:** 15

### Content

> [!tip]

# The Full Sequence

In LS 201, you learned about worked examples, the expertise reversal effect, and the fading sequence. Now we put it all together into a practical design tool: the release sequence. This is how you move students from "I've never seen this" to "I can do this on my own."

The release sequence has three stages: modelling → guided practice → independent practice. Each stage serves a different purpose, and each requires specific teacher actions. The most common mistakes are rushing through the middle stage (guided practice) or skipping it entirely.

# Stage 1: Modelling

Modelling means thinking aloud while you solve a problem or complete a task. Not just showing the answer — narrating the DECISIONS. The goal is to make the invisible thinking visible.

A poor model: "First we find the common denominator. It's 12. Then we convert both fractions. 2/3 becomes 8/12 and 1/4 becomes 3/12. Then we add them: 11/12."

A good model: "I'm looking at these two fractions and I need to add them. But they have different denominators — 3 and 4 — so I can't add them directly. I need to find a common denominator. I'm thinking: what number do both 3 and 4 go into? I know 3 times 4 is 12, so 12 works. Now I need to convert each fraction. For 2/3, I ask: what did I multiply 3 by to get 12? Four. So I multiply the top by 4 too: 8/12. Same logic for 1/4..."

The difference is that the second model shows the THINKING, not just the steps. Students don't just learn what to do — they learn how to approach the problem. This is what "I do, you watch" should actually look like.

> [!info]
> **The key to good modelling:** Narrate your decision points, not your steps. Instead of "first do this, then do this," say "I'm noticing X, so I'm going to try Y because..." Students need to hear the reasoning, not just the procedure.

# Stage 2: Guided Practice

Guided practice is where students try the task with teacher support. The teacher watches, catches errors early, and provides immediate feedback. This is the most important stage — and the one most often rushed or skipped.

In a small group of 8-9 students, guided practice is especially powerful because you can see every student's work in real time. You can catch the student who's confused before they've practised the wrong approach 10 times. You can give immediate, targeted feedback.

What guided practice looks like:
- The teacher gives a problem similar to the one modelled
- Students begin working while the teacher circulates
- The teacher watches for errors and misconceptions — not just wrong answers, but wrong processes
- When a student makes an error, the teacher doesn't give the answer but redirects: "Look at this step again. What did you multiply the denominator by?"
- When most students have completed the problem, the teacher reviews it together and addresses common errors

How long should guided practice last? Until most students can do the task correctly without help. Not one problem. Not two problems if students are still making errors. As many problems as it takes for you to feel confident that students understand the process.

> [!warning]
> **The rushing trap:** The most common Phase 1 error is moving from modelling to independent practice too quickly, skipping or shortening guided practice. When students make errors during independent practice that could have been caught during guided practice, it's a sign that the release was too fast. Errors practised independently become ingrained — they're harder to fix later.

# Stage 3: Independent Practice

Independent practice is where the skill becomes automatic. Students work alone, without teacher support. But this stage should ONLY begin after guided practice has confirmed understanding. Releasing students to independent practice before they're ready means they'll practise errors, not skills.

In elementary: independent practice often happens during the work cycle. The student has had the lesson and guided practice, and now works with the material independently. The teacher observes but doesn't intervene unless the student is truly stuck.

In middle school: independent practice is the "now you try on your own" phase. Students work through problems while the teacher monitors, stepping in only when necessary. The goal is for students to build fluency and automaticity.

# The Fading Sequence

Between modelling and full independence, there's a gradual removal of scaffolding. From LS 201, recall the four-stage progression:

1. **Full worked example:** The teacher solves the entire problem while students watch
2. **Partially completed example:** The teacher completes some steps, students complete the rest
3. **Hints only:** Students attempt the full problem with strategic hints available
4. **Fully independent:** Students solve without any support

Each stage removes scaffolding as competence grows. The art is in knowing when to move from one stage to the next — and the answer is: use diagnostic questions. Before reducing support, check whether students can handle the current level reliably.

# The Expertise Reversal Danger

Here's a nuance from LS 201 that matters for design: leaving scaffolding in place too long is as harmful as removing it too soon.

When a student has mastered the basic procedure, continued worked examples become redundant. The student doesn't need to see the thinking modelled anymore — they need to do the thinking themselves. At this point, the scaffolding gets in the way. It takes up cognitive resources (the student processes the example even though they don't need it) and prevents the student from developing independence.

Use diagnostic questions to determine readiness. If a student can correctly answer "what would you do next?" and "why?" without help, they're ready for the next stage of fading.

# What Mastery Learning Actually Means

Mastery learning is a simple idea that's often misunderstood. It means: students must demonstrate competence on current material before moving to the next level. Not "everyone moves at the same pace" — "everyone reaches the same standard, at their own pace."

In a traditional school, everyone moves together regardless of whether they've understood the current material. The teacher teaches Topic A for two weeks, then moves to Topic B whether students have mastered Topic A or not. Students who didn't master Topic A now have a gap — and Topic B may build on Topic A, so the gap compounds.

Mastery learning says: check whether students have reached a sufficient level of understanding before moving on. If they haven't, provide targeted support until they do. The standard is the same for everyone; the time to reach it may differ.

In our small-group setting with 8-9 students, this is more achievable than in a class of 30. You can monitor each student's understanding and respond individually.

# Diagnosing Gaps in a Mixed-Stage Group

In any group of 8-9 students, you'll have different levels of understanding. Some students will master a concept after one lesson. Others will need multiple exposures. The same group will have different gaps on different topics — a student who excels at reading comprehension might struggle with writing structure, and vice versa.

The tool for diagnosis is the diagnostic question — a carefully designed question that reveals whether a student has understood a specific concept, not just whether they can get a right answer. From LS 202, you know how to design these: questions where each wrong answer reveals a specific misconception.

Use diagnostic questions at key transition points:
- After guided practice, before releasing to independence (Section 5)
- At the end of a lesson, to decide who needs more support
- At the start of a new topic that builds on a previous one, to check the prerequisite

When you discover gaps, don't panic. Gaps are normal. The question is: what do you do about them?

> [!info]
> **Gaps are diagnostic information, not failure.** When 3 of 8 students haven't mastered a concept, it's not a sign that your teaching failed — it's useful information about which students need what kind of support. The diagnostic question isn't a test of the student; it's a tool for you to direct your attention and effort where it's needed.

# Targeted Remediation

When you identify specific gaps, the response should be targeted, not general. Not "re-teach the whole unit" but "this student needs more guided practice on THIS specific concept."

Targeted remediation looks like this:
- Identify the specific gap (e.g., "this student can add fractions with like denominators but doesn't understand how to find a common denominator")
- Provide focused support on that specific gap — a brief re-model, a few guided practice problems, a different representation or explanation
- Check again — has the gap closed?

This is different from re-teaching. Re-teaching covers everything again, wasting time on parts the student already understands. Targeted remediation focuses on the specific point of confusion.

In a small group, you can do this efficiently. While 5 students work independently on material they've mastered, you spend 5-10 minutes with the 3 students who need support on the specific concept. This isn't "holding everyone back" — it's differentiation based on diagnostic data.

# Mindspark as a Mastery Tool

For maths and English, Mindspark already operates on mastery learning principles. The adaptive engine identifies gaps in student understanding and serves targeted practice on those specific gaps. It doesn't move students ahead until they've demonstrated competence at the current level.

This is powerful — but it only covers maths and English. For every other subject, you need to implement mastery learning manually:
- Design diagnostic questions for key concepts
- Use them to identify who has mastered what
- Provide targeted support for specific gaps
- Re-check before moving on

The principles are the same as Mindspark's algorithm, but you're the algorithm.

> [!warning]
> **The tension with curriculum pacing:** The most common objection to mastery learning is: "I can't slow down — I have to cover the curriculum." Here's the research response: covering material that students haven't mastered is wasted time. If Topic B requires understanding of Topic A, and three students haven't mastered Topic A, then teaching Topic B to those students is pointless — they can't access it. You're not saving time by moving on; you're building on an unstable foundation. Better to master less than to cover more and retain nothing.

# The Practical Balance

Mastery learning doesn't mean that every student must master every single concept before anyone moves on. That's impractical. Here's the practical balance:

1. Identify the concepts that are PREREQUISITES for what comes next. These are the ones where mastery matters most.
2. For prerequisite concepts, check for mastery and provide targeted remediation before moving on.
3. For concepts that are important but not prerequisites, note the gap and plan spaced retrieval to address it over time.
4. For enrichment or extension concepts, let students who've mastered the basics go deeper while you support those who need more time on the fundamentals.

This is differentiation based on learning needs, not ability grouping. Every student aims for the same standard — they just get different levels of support along the way.

## Section 7: Designing for Transfer

**Estimated Minutes:** 15

# The Ultimate Goal

Transfer is the ultimate goal of education: applying what you learned in one context to a new, different context. A student who learns about fractions in maths and then recognises fractions in a recipe, a science measurement, or a music rhythm is transferring their knowledge.

But transfer is much harder than most people think. In fact, it's one of the most robust findings in educational research: students consistently fail to apply what they've learned in one context to a different context, even when the underlying principle is the same. A student who can solve fraction problems perfectly in maths class may not recognise a fraction problem in science. Not because they're stupid — because knowledge tends to be "welded" to the context in which it was learned.

This means transfer must be deliberately designed. It won't just happen because you taught the concept well. You have to plan for it.

# Near Transfer vs. Far Transfer

**Near transfer:** Applying a skill to a similar context. You learned to add fractions with thirds and fourths, and now you can add fractions with fifths and sixths. The contexts are slightly different but the procedure is essentially the same. Near transfer is achievable with good teaching.

**Far transfer:** Applying a skill to a very different context. You learned about proportional reasoning in maths and now you apply it to mixing paint colours or understanding population growth rates. The surface features are completely different, but the underlying principle is the same. Far transfer is rare and unreliable — even among experts.

For practical lesson design, focus your efforts on near transfer. If you can achieve reliable near transfer across multiple contexts, that's a significant accomplishment. Far transfer is a bonus, not an expectation.

> [!info]
> **The practical implication:** Don't assume that teaching a concept in one context means students can use it everywhere. If you want students to apply fraction skills in science, you need to explicitly practise fraction skills IN a science context. Context matters more than most teachers realise.

# Why Transfer Is Hard

Transfer fails because knowledge gets "welded" to its context. When a student learns to calculate percentages in maths class — with worksheets, maths vocabulary, a teacher asking "what percentage is this?" — all of those contextual features become part of the memory. The student doesn't just learn "percentages." They learn "percentages-in-maths-class-on-worksheets."

When the same student encounters a percentage problem in science — different room, different teacher, different vocabulary ("what proportion of the sample..."), different format — they don't recognise it as the same skill. The surface features are different, and the brain retrieves knowledge based partly on surface features.

This is why "teaching it once and hoping it transfers" doesn't work. The student needs to encounter the same principle in multiple contexts before they can strip away the context-specific features and see the underlying structure.

# What Helps Transfer

Four strategies that reliably improve transfer:

**1. Varied practice — same concept, multiple contexts.** Don't just practise fractions with numbers. Practise fractions with pizza, with measuring cups, with time, with music. Each new context builds a more flexible representation of the concept.

**2. Explicit connection-making.** Don't assume students will see the connection. Say it out loud: "Where else have we seen this pattern? Remember when we talked about sharing equally in our maths lesson? This is the same idea — dividing something into equal parts."

**3. Comparing examples.** Put two examples side by side — one from maths, one from science — and ask: "What's the same about these two problems? What's different?" The comparison forces students to identify the deep structure beneath the surface features.

**4. Abstracting the principle.** After students have seen multiple examples, help them articulate the general rule: "So what's the pattern we keep seeing? Whenever we need to... we can..." This abstract principle is more transferable than any single example.

> [!tip]
> **The order matters.** Don't start with the abstract principle and then show examples (that's just telling). Start with multiple concrete examples, then help students discover the common principle. The discovery — guided by you — builds understanding that sticks and transfers.

# What Doesn't Help Transfer

Hoping it happens. This is the most common approach, and it reliably fails. "We covered percentages in maths, so students should be able to use them in science." They can't. Not without explicit practice in the new context.

Also unhelpful: teaching "general thinking skills" in isolation. Critical thinking, problem solving, and creativity are not transferable general skills — they're highly context-dependent. A student who thinks critically about literature may not think critically about science. These skills must be developed within each domain, with explicit connections drawn between domains.

# Practical Applications for Your Classroom

When teaching a concept, deliberately use examples from multiple contexts:
- Teach measurement using science experiments, cooking, art projects, and sports
- Teach persuasive writing using advertisements, opinion essays, letters, and debates
- Teach data analysis using science data, social studies data, and student-generated data

When students encounter a new problem, prompt them: "What does this remind you of? Where have we seen something like this before?" Train them to look for connections, not just wait for you to point them out.

In elementary: the Montessori emphasis on practical life applications is a transfer strategy. When a student measures ingredients for cooking after learning measurement with materials, that's deliberate near transfer.

In middle school: cross-subject connections are transfer opportunities. Coordinate with other teachers: "When you teach X in science, I'll reference the same data analysis skills in maths." Even a brief mention builds the bridge.

You know the theory. You know the strategies.

Now it's time to apply it all to your own teaching. Not to someone else's lesson plan — to yours. Not to an abstract scenario — to the unit you're actually teaching right now.

This section gives you an audit checklist. Take a real unit or lesson sequence you teach, walk it through the checklist, and identify what's working and what's missing.

# The Audit Checklist

Work through each area and honestly assess your current practice for a specific unit you teach.

**1. Phase 1 — Understanding (Cognitive Load)**

- Am I presenting new material in small steps, or dumping too much at once?
- Am I modelling my thinking aloud — narrating the decisions, not just the steps?
- Am I doing sufficient guided practice before releasing students to work independently?
- Am I checking understanding with diagnostic questions at key transition points?
- Are my scaffolds appropriate — enough for beginners, removed for students who've progressed?

**2. Phase 2 — Durability (Memory)**

- Is there retrieval practice? When — the next day? The next week? A month later?
- Is the retrieval spaced? Do I have a spacing calendar, or do I "cover and move on"?
- Is there any interleaving? Do I mix topics during practice, or block them?
- Is there cumulative review — or does each unit exist in isolation?

**3. Feedback (from LS 301)**

- Am I giving specific, actionable feedback — not just praise?
- Am I using detective feedback that makes students do the thinking?
- Am I using wise framing that builds trust alongside high standards?
- Am I giving feedback that students actually USE to improve their work?

**4. Student Ownership (from LS 301)**

- Are students planning their own work, or am I doing all the planning?
- Are students monitoring their own understanding, or waiting for me to tell them?
- Are students evaluating their own work against standards, or depending entirely on my judgment?
- Have I taught the metacognitive skills needed for self-regulation, or just expected them?

**5. Collaboration (from LS 301)**

- When students work together, is there individual accountability?
- Are collaborative tasks structured so every student must contribute, not just the confident ones?
- Have I taught students HOW to give peer feedback, or just told them to do it?

> [!info]
> **Be honest, not comprehensive.** The point of the audit isn't to feel bad about everything you're not doing. It's to identify the BIGGEST gaps — the areas where a small change would make the largest difference. Most teachers have at least one area that's reasonably strong and one area that's almost entirely missing.

# Identify Three Changes

After completing the audit, identify exactly three changes you'll make. Not ten. Not "everything." Three.

Why three? Because change is hard. If you try to overhaul your entire practice at once, you'll be overwhelmed, nothing will stick, and you'll revert to your old habits within a month. This is the same principle you apply to your students — small steps, scaffolded release, practice until automatic.

Your three changes should be:
1. **Specific:** Not "improve feedback" but "start every session with a 3-question retrieval starter covering material from the past month."
2. **Actionable:** Something you can start doing this week, not something that requires redesigning your entire curriculum.
3. **Connected to a principle:** For each change, you should be able to name the learning science principle behind it. This isn't arbitrary — you're making evidence-informed choices.

> [!warning]
> **The "I'll change everything" trap:** The most ambitious teachers want to implement every strategy from every module all at once. This approach almost always fails. It's too much cognitive load — for you. Start with one change, practise it for two weeks until it's automatic, then add the second. Then the third. Small steps. The same principle that works for your students works for you.

# Share for Accountability

Research on behaviour change — including teachers changing their practice — consistently shows that public commitment and accountability improve follow-through. Tell a colleague what your three changes are. Better yet, find a colleague who's also making changes and check in with each other.

Set a specific date to check in: two weeks from now. At that check-in, ask:
- Did you implement your first change? What happened?
- What was hard about it? What worked?
- Are you ready to add the second change?

This isn't about judgment. It's about having someone ask you the question, which makes you more likely to follow through.

# The One-Change Principle

Start with the most important change first. The one that addresses the biggest gap you identified in your audit. Practise it for two weeks. Not perfectly — just consistently. Do it every session, even when it's awkward, even when you forget and have to restart, even when it doesn't seem to be working yet.

After two weeks, it will start to feel natural. Then add the second change. Two more weeks. Then the third.

In three months, you'll have embedded three evidence-based changes into your practice — permanently. That's more than most professional development programmes achieve in a year. And it happened because you started small, practised consistently, and built gradually. Exactly the way durable learning works.

> "We are what we repeatedly do. Excellence, then, is not an act, but a habit."
> — Will Durant (paraphrasing Aristotle)

# A Final Thought

Everything in this course — retrieval routines, spacing calendars, interleaving, the release sequence, mastery learning, transfer design — is ultimately about one thing: respecting the gap between UNDERSTANDING and REMEMBERING.

Your students can understand something perfectly today and forget it completely next month. That's not a failure of understanding. It's how memory works. The entire purpose of Phase 2 — and of this course — is to bridge that gap deliberately, with tools that the science of learning has shown to work.

You now have those tools. The question is: will you use them?
