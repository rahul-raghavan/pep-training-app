## Section 1: Why Feedback Matters More Without Grades
**Slug:** why-feedback-matters-more
**Estimated Minutes:** 15

### Content

# The Assumption We Need to Challenge

Let's start with an uncomfortable truth. In many no-grades schools, there's an unspoken belief: "We don't give grades, so we're already doing it right." The thinking goes something like this: grades are harmful, we removed them, therefore our students are better off.

That's half right. Removing grades does remove real problems — unhealthy comparison, anxiety, attention shifting from learning to scores. But removing grades also creates a vacuum. And if nothing fills that vacuum, students are left working in the dark.

Think about it this way. In a traditional graded school, grades do some of the work of signalling quality. They're blunt instruments — a "B+" doesn't tell you what to improve or how — but they do tell the student something: "This is roughly where you are." That signal is incomplete and often harmful, but it exists.

In our schools, we've removed that signal. Which means feedback is now the ONLY mechanism students have for understanding where their work stands and what to do next. Without it, they're flying blind.

> [!warning]
> **The no-grades trap:** Removing grades removes a problem. But it creates a vacuum that only feedback can fill. No grades + no feedback = a student with no information about their learning. That's worse than grades.

# What the Research Shows

The research on feedback is clear and consistent. Students who receive specific, actionable feedback improve significantly. Students who receive no feedback — or vague feedback like "good work" or "well done" — stagnate.

This isn't surprising when you think about it. Imagine learning to cook without ever tasting your food. Imagine practising a sport without ever seeing the result. Feedback is the information loop that connects effort to improvement. Without it, practice is just repetition — and repetition without feedback reinforces mistakes as much as it reinforces skills.

In our no-grades environment, this matters even more. When there's no grade to fall back on, the quality of your feedback determines whether students can actually manage their own learning or are just guessing.

# Feedback Is Not Praise

One of the most common confusions: teachers think they're giving feedback when they're actually giving praise. "Great job!" "I love how hard you worked!" "Beautiful handwriting!" These feel good. They're kind. But they're not feedback.

Feedback tells the student something about their work relative to a standard or goal. It answers the question: "Where am I, and what do I do next?" Praise answers a different question: "Does my teacher like me?" Both questions matter, but only one moves learning forward.

| What it sounds like | What it is | Does it improve learning? |
|---|---|---|
| "Great job!" | Praise | No — no information about the work |
| "You worked really hard on this." | Praise (effort) | Slightly — acknowledges effort but no direction |
| "Your explanation of why the character changed is clear, but your evidence from the text is too vague. Find a specific quote that supports your point." | Feedback | Yes — specific, actionable, tells the student what to do next |

# Independence Requires a Mirror

Here's the connection to student ownership — a theme we'll develop across this entire course. We want students to become independent learners. We want them to manage their own work, assess their own progress, and make good decisions about what to do next.

But independence doesn't come from being left alone. It comes from gradually building the capacity to do what a good teacher does — evaluate your work against a standard and decide what to improve. Feedback is the mirror that makes this possible. When a teacher gives clear, specific feedback, the student learns what quality looks like and how their work compares to it. Over time, the student internalises this and begins to do it for themselves.

Without feedback, independence becomes guesswork. The student might feel confident — but confidence without calibration is the fluency trap from LS 101. They think they understand because the work felt easy, not because they've checked against a standard.

> [!info]
> **The mirror principle:** Feedback is the mirror that helps students see their own learning accurately. Without it, they rely on how they feel — and feelings are unreliable indicators of actual understanding. This is especially important without grades, because there's no other external signal.

# What This Course Will Build

Over the next eight sections, we'll go deep into the skill of giving feedback that actually works. Not more feedback — better feedback. Not feedback that takes hours of your time — feedback that's focused, efficient, and produces learning.

We'll cover:

- How to make students do the thinking, not just receive corrections

- How metacognition and self-regulation turn feedback into independence
- How peer assessment builds self-assessment
- How to redesign your practice without adding hours to your day

This is the hardest and most important module in the Learning Science series. Feedback is where everything from LS 101, 201, and 202 comes together in your daily practice. Let's begin.

## Section 2: Why Most Feedback Fails

# The Butler Study: A Landmark Finding

In 1988, Ruth Butler ran a study that changed how we think about feedback. She gave students a task, then divided them into three groups:

- **Group 1** received comments only — specific feedback about their work
- **Group 2** received grades only — a numerical score
- **Group 3** received grades plus comments — both a score and specific feedback

You might expect Group 3 to do best — they got the most information, after all. But here's what happened:

- **Comments only** → significant improvement on the next task
- **Grades only** → no improvement
- **Grades plus comments** → no improvement (same as grades only)

Read that last line again. Adding comments to grades didn't help. The grade dominated attention. Students who received a grade ignored the comments entirely — they looked at the score, compared themselves to others, and moved on. The feedback was wasted.

This is one of the most important findings in educational research. It tells us that even excellent feedback can be neutralised if students' attention is directed elsewhere — toward a score, a ranking, a comparison.

> [!info]
> **Our advantage:** Because we don't use grades, our feedback doesn't compete with scores for students' attention. We're in the ideal position — comments only, which is the condition that produced the most learning in Butler's study. But only if we actually give those comments, and only if they're specific and actionable.

# Timing: Feedback Must Be Usable

Feedback must arrive while the student can still do something with it. This seems obvious, but it's routinely violated in schools everywhere.

If a student submits a piece of writing and gets feedback three weeks later, what can they do with it? The task is finished. The thinking that produced the work has faded. The student has moved on to something else entirely. The feedback is archaeological — interesting perhaps, but not useful.

Effective feedback is timely. Not necessarily instant — sometimes you need time to read and think about student work carefully. But it must arrive while the student is still engaged with the learning, still able to revise, still able to apply the feedback to their next attempt.

In your small groups of 8-10 students, you have an enormous advantage here. You can give feedback during the lesson, in conversation, while the student is still working. You don't have to collect 30 papers and mark them overnight. Use that advantage.

# Specificity: "Good Work" Is Not Feedback

We covered this briefly in Section 1, but let's go deeper. Vague feedback fails because it gives the student no actionable information. Consider the difference:

**Vague:** "Your argument needs work."
**Specific:** "Your main claim is clear, but your second piece of evidence doesn't actually support it. Find a different example that connects directly to your claim."

The vague version tells the student something is wrong but not what or how to fix it. The specific version locates the problem and tells the student what to do. The difference in impact is enormous.

Specificity also applies to positive feedback. "Good work!" tells the student nothing about what was good. "Your opening paragraph hooks the reader immediately because you started with a specific, surprising detail — keep doing that in future pieces" tells the student what to repeat and why it works.

# Learner Readiness: The Gap Must Be Bridgeable

Here's a condition that's often overlooked. Feedback only works when the gap between where the student is and where they need to be is small enough for the student to bridge.

Imagine giving a student who struggles with basic sentence structure detailed feedback about argument structure. The feedback might be accurate, but it's aimed at a level the student can't reach yet. The result: the student feels overwhelmed and gives up.

Effective feedback meets the student where they are. It identifies the next step, not the final destination. This means different students working on the same task might receive different feedback — because they're at different points in their learning.

> [!tip]
> **Practical rule:** If your feedback requires the student to do something they haven't yet learned how to do, it's not feedback — it's a wish. Before giving feedback, ask yourself: "Can this student actually act on this right now?" If not, re-teach first, then give feedback.

# Volume: Less Is More

Teachers who care about their students tend to over-correct. They mark everything — every spelling error, every awkward sentence, every missed step. The intention is good: "I want to be thorough. I want them to know exactly what to improve."

But the effect is the opposite. When a student gets back work covered in corrections, they don't carefully read each one and improve on all fronts. They feel overwhelmed. They think, "I got everything wrong." They disengage.

The research is clear: prioritise. Pick the ONE thing that will make the biggest difference to this student's learning right now. Give feedback on that one thing. Let the rest go — for now. You'll come back to those other issues later, when this issue has been addressed.

This is especially true for younger students in elementary settings, where cognitive capacity is more limited. But it applies to middle school too. Even adults can only process a limited number of improvement points at once.

# The Detective Principle

You met the detective principle briefly in LS 101. Now we'll go much deeper, because this idea will transform your feedback practice.

The core insight is this: when a teacher corrects a student's work, the work improves but the student doesn't. When the student has to find and fix the problem themselves, the student improves.

Think about it from a cognitive science perspective — which you studied in LS 201. When the teacher does the thinking (locates the error, diagnoses the cause, provides the fix), the student's brain is barely engaged. They read the correction, nod, and move on. The cognitive work happened in the teacher's head, not the student's.

But when the teacher gives the student enough information to locate, diagnose, and fix the problem themselves, the student has to do the thinking. They have to re-read their work, compare it to a standard, identify the gap, and figure out what to change. That's the cognitive work that produces learning.

> [!info]
> **The detective principle in one sentence:** Give students enough information to find and fix the problem, but not so much that you've done the thinking for them.

# Directive vs Detective vs Facilitative

Feedback exists on a spectrum:

| Type | What it sounds like | Who does the thinking? |
|---|---|---|
| **Directive** | "Change 'went' to 'goes' in line 3." | The teacher |
| **Detective** | "Find the two sentences where the tense changes unexpectedly." | The student, with guidance |
| **Facilitative** | "Read your story aloud. Does anything sound inconsistent?" | The student, with minimal guidance |

Directive feedback is the fastest — the student makes the change immediately. But the student learns the least, because they didn't have to think.

Detective feedback takes longer — the student has to search, analyse, and decide. But the learning is much deeper, because the student is doing the cognitive work.

Facilitative feedback is the most open-ended — it trusts the student to identify issues themselves. This works only when the student already has a sense of what quality looks like.

Your goal over time is to move students along this spectrum. Start with more directive feedback when a concept is new, shift to detective feedback as understanding develops, and move toward facilitative feedback as students build self-assessment skills.

# Examples Across Subjects

Here's what detective feedback sounds like in different subjects and age groups:

**Writing (elementary):**
- Directive: "You need a capital letter at the start of this sentence."
- Detective: "I can see three sentences that are missing something at the start. Can you find them?"

**Writing (middle school):**
- Directive: "Your conclusion doesn't reference your main argument."
- Detective: "Read your introduction and your conclusion side by side. What's missing from the conclusion?"

**Maths (elementary):**
- Directive: "Question 4 should be multiplication, not addition."
- Detective: "One of your answers used the wrong operation. Can you find which one?"

**Maths (middle school):**
- Directive: "You forgot to multiply both sides of the equation."
- Detective: "Your steps 1-3 are correct, but something goes wrong at step 4. Compare step 4 to the rule we used yesterday."

**Science (middle school):**
- Directive: "Your prediction was wrong because you confused mass and weight."
- Detective: "Your prediction and your result don't match. Look at the definitions of mass and weight — which one did you actually measure?"

# When the Student Finds It, the Student Learns It

There's a beautiful study where two groups of students received feedback on their writing. Group 1 received direct corrections — the teacher fixed every error. Group 2 received coded feedback — the teacher marked the line where an error occurred and indicated the type of error (spelling, grammar, punctuation), but the student had to find and fix it.

Group 2 made significantly fewer errors on their next piece of writing. Group 1 did not improve. The work was corrected, but the students weren't.

This is the fundamental insight: the purpose of feedback is not to improve the work. It's to improve the student. If the work gets better but the student doesn't learn, the feedback has failed. If the student learns but the work still has some imperfections, the feedback has succeeded.

> [!warning]
> **The correction trap:** It feels satisfying to correct every error on a student's work. The paper looks better. But ask yourself: who did the learning? If you did all the thinking, you got all the practice. Next time, try: "I've found three errors in this paragraph. Can you find and fix them?" The work might not end up perfect, but the student will learn more.

# Making Detective Feedback Practical

You might be thinking: "This sounds great in theory, but isn't it slower? I have 8 students in my group and limited time."

Actually, detective feedback can be faster than directive feedback. Here's why:

- You don't need to write out the correct answer — just point to the location and type of issue
- You can give detective feedback verbally during the lesson ("I notice something in your second paragraph — take another look")
- The student does the hard work, not you

The key is to be specific enough that the student knows where to look, but open enough that they have to think. "Something is wrong somewhere in your work" is too vague — the student will waste time searching randomly. "The third paragraph has an issue with evidence" is specific enough to direct attention but open enough to require thinking.

# The Trust Problem

Here's something that research shows clearly but that isn't obvious: the same piece of feedback can help one student and harm another, depending on whether the student trusts the teacher's intentions.

When a student receives critical feedback, they face an immediate question — usually unconscious: "Why is this person criticising my work? Is it because they think I'm not capable? Is it because they don't like me? Or is it because they actually believe I can do better?"

If the student concludes (even subconsciously) that the teacher thinks they're not capable, the feedback backfires. The student disengages. They interpret the criticism as confirmation that they're not good enough.

If the student concludes that the teacher believes in them and is pushing them because of that belief, the feedback works. They engage with it. They try harder. They improve.

This is not about being nice. It's about trust.

# The Wise Feedback Studies

Researchers David Yeager and Geoffrey Cohen ran a series of studies on what they called "wise feedback." Here's what they did:

Students wrote an essay. The teacher gave critical feedback — real, substantive feedback about what needed to improve. But the researchers varied what was attached to the feedback:

- **Control group:** Just the feedback
- **Wise feedback group:** The feedback plus a note: "I'm giving you these comments because I have very high standards and I believe you can reach them."

That one sentence changed everything. Students who received the wise framing were significantly more likely to revise their essays. They made more substantial improvements. And the effect was especially strong for students who had reason to distrust the feedback — students who'd had negative experiences with critical feedback in the past.

> [!info]
> **The wise feedback formula:** High standards + belief in the student's ability to meet them. Both parts are essential. High standards alone feels punitive. Belief alone feels like empty encouragement. Together, they create the conditions for critical feedback to be heard and used.

# Three Teachers, Three Approaches

Let's see this play out in practice. A student submits a piece of writing that has a clear argument but weak evidence. Three teachers respond:

**Teacher A:** "Good effort! Keep it up!" This feels kind, but it signals low expectations. If the work has a real weakness (which it does), the teacher is either not seeing it or not mentioning it. Either way, the student learns: "My teacher doesn't think I can do better." The praise becomes a ceiling.

**Teacher B:** "This isn't good enough. Your evidence is weak throughout. Redo it." This is honest, but it's brutal. The student knows the work is rejected but doesn't know specifically what's wrong or how to fix it. The message they hear: "You failed." Without guidance, many students will simply try the same approach again, or give up.

**Teacher C:** "Your main argument is sharp — you've taken a clear position and stated it directly. That's exactly the kind of thinking I'm looking for. Your evidence isn't strong enough yet to support that argument. Go back to the text and find two specific examples that directly connect to your claim. I'm pushing you on this because I know you can make this piece excellent."

Teacher C's feedback is longer, but look at what it does:
- Identifies what's working (so the student knows what to keep)
- Identifies what needs to improve (specific, not vague)
- Tells the student what to do (actionable)
- Communicates high standards AND belief ("I know you can make this piece excellent")

# Wise Feedback Is a Stance, Not a Script

You don't need to say "I have high standards and I believe you can reach them" every time you give feedback. That would quickly become hollow — students see through scripts.

Instead, wise feedback is a stance — an orientation toward students that comes through in everything you do:
- You give honest, critical feedback because you respect the student
- You don't water down your expectations because you think the student can't handle them
- You provide the support and guidance to actually reach those expectations
- You make it clear — through your words and actions — that struggle is normal and expected, not a sign of failure

Over time, this builds a culture where critical feedback is welcomed rather than feared. Students learn that when their teacher pushes them, it means "I care about your growth," not "I'm disappointed in you."

> [!warning]
> **The comfort trap:** It feels kind to avoid critical feedback — to focus on what's positive and skip over what needs to change. But this kindness has a cost: the student doesn't improve, and they may sense that you don't think they're capable of more. Sometimes the kindest thing you can do is tell a student the truth about their work — accompanied by clear guidance on how to make it better and genuine confidence that they can.

# Wise Feedback in Small Groups

In your small groups of 8-10 students, you have a relationship advantage that large-classroom teachers can only dream of. You know each student. They know you. This makes wise feedback easier — and more important.

Easier, because the trust relationship is already being built through daily interaction. You don't need a special note attached to the feedback — your students already know your character.

More important, because in a small group, every student is visible. There's nowhere to hide. If one student consistently receives gentle praise while another receives challenging feedback, students notice. Wise feedback should be for everyone — your strongest students need to be pushed, and your struggling students need to know you believe they can grow.

## Section 5: When Not to Give Feedback
**Slug:** when-not-to-give-feedback
**Estimated Minutes:** 15

# Feedback Is Not Always the Answer

After three sections about how to give better feedback, this section might surprise you: sometimes the right response to student work is NOT feedback.

This is counterintuitive — especially in a course titled "Feedback & Student Ownership." But knowing when NOT to give feedback is just as important as knowing how to give it well. Feedback at the wrong time, on the wrong thing, or for the wrong reason can waste time, overwhelm students, or even harm learning.

The question to ask is not "What feedback should I give?" but "What does this student need right now?" The answer might be feedback. But it might be re-teaching, more practice, a different task, or simply permission to move on.

# Matching the Response to the Diagnosis

When a student's work isn't where you want it to be, the cause matters. Different causes require different responses:

**The student doesn't understand the concept →** Re-teach. Feedback on the application of a concept the student doesn't understand is useless. If a student can't add fractions because they don't understand what a denominator means, writing "find a common denominator" on their work won't help. Go back and teach the concept. Then give them a fresh opportunity to practise. Then give feedback.

**The student understands but can't apply it yet →** More guided practice. The student might be able to explain what a good persuasive paragraph looks like but struggle to write one. They need more practice with support — worked examples, guided attempts, structured templates. Feedback comes after they've had enough practice to produce work that's close to the standard.

**The student can apply but makes errors →** This is where feedback works best. The student has the knowledge and skill, but execution isn't consistent. Detective feedback ("find the two errors in this paragraph") is powerful here because the student has the capacity to locate and fix the problems.

**The student is fluent →** Extend, don't feedback. If a student consistently produces strong work, spending time on minor corrections is a poor use of both your time and theirs. Instead, challenge them with a harder task, a more complex problem, or an opportunity to teach the concept to a peer.

> [!info]
> **The diagnostic question before feedback:** Before giving feedback, ask yourself: "Why is this student's work not where I want it to be?" If the answer is "they don't understand the concept," feedback isn't the solution — re-teaching is. Feedback is the right tool when the student understands but needs help refining their execution.

# The Priority Problem

Even when feedback is the right response, you still face a choice: what to give feedback on. Real student work has multiple issues. A piece of writing might have problems with argument, evidence, organisation, sentence structure, vocabulary, and punctuation — all at once.

If you feedback on everything, you overwhelm the student. If you feedback on the wrong thing, you waste an opportunity. The skill is prioritisation.

Here's a framework for deciding what to feedback on:

1. **What's the learning intention?** Give feedback on the thing you were actually teaching. If the lesson was about using evidence in persuasive writing, feedback on evidence — not punctuation.
2. **What will make the biggest difference?** Some issues are foundational — fixing them will improve everything else. Other issues are surface-level. Focus on the foundational one.
3. **What can this student act on right now?** Don't feedback on something the student doesn't yet have the skill to fix. Meet them where they are.

Pick ONE priority. Give focused, actionable feedback on that one thing. Let the rest wait.

# When to Let Go

Sometimes the student's work is good enough for now, and the most important thing is to move on.

This is hard for teachers who care deeply about quality. But perfectionism in feedback can slow progress. If a student has met the learning intention — even if the work has imperfections — it may be better to acknowledge the achievement and move to the next challenge rather than spending more time refining this piece.

This doesn't mean accepting poor work. It means recognising that learning is a long journey, and not every piece of work needs to be polished to perfection. The goal is improvement over time, not perfection in the moment.

> [!warning]
> **The perfectionism trap:** Some teachers feedback on every piece of work until it's "right." This can actually slow learning — the student spends so long refining one piece that they don't get enough practice on the next skill. Ask yourself: "Has this student learned what they needed to from this task?" If yes, move on. The imperfections in this piece will be addressed through future learning.

# Feedback Overload and Student Wellbeing

There's a wellbeing dimension here too. A student who receives constant critical feedback — even well-intended, specific, actionable feedback — can begin to feel that nothing they do is good enough. They lose motivation. They start to dread getting their work back.

Balance is essential. Not every interaction needs to be a feedback moment. Sometimes the student needs to hear: "You've done what I asked. Well done. Let's move to the next thing." The absence of critical feedback is itself a signal: "You're on track."

In your small groups, you're with the same students every day. Be mindful of the cumulative emotional effect of your feedback. A student who gets one piece of critical feedback per week can process and act on it. A student who gets critical feedback every day may shut down.

## Section 6: Metacognition — What It Is and Why It Matters
**Slug:** metacognition-what-and-why
**Estimated Minutes:** 15

### Content

> [!tip]
> **Quick retrieval:** From Section 5, recall the four diagnostic categories: the student doesn't understand, the student understands but can't apply, the student applies but makes errors, or the student is fluent. For which category is feedback most effective? And for the others, what should you do instead?

# Beyond Plan, Monitor, Evaluate

In LS 101, you were introduced to metacognition as "thinking about thinking" — with three components: plan, monitor, and evaluate. You learned to build these into daily work: students plan before starting a task, monitor their understanding while working, and evaluate their learning afterward.

That's a good starting point. But for feedback to truly produce independence — for students to eventually not need your feedback because they can assess their own work — we need to go deeper.

Metacognition is the engine of self-regulated learning. It's what separates a student who can use feedback productively from one who reads it, shrugs, and moves on. And it's what makes the difference between a student who is genuinely independent and one who is merely unsupervised.

# Sadler's Three Conditions

Royce Sadler, an Australian education researcher, identified three conditions that must be met for feedback to actually work. If any one of these is missing, even excellent feedback will be wasted:

**1. The student must know what quality looks like.**
Before a student can evaluate their own work, they need a clear picture of the target. What does a good persuasive paragraph look like? What does a correct maths solution look like? Without this, feedback like "your evidence is weak" is meaningless — the student doesn't know what strong evidence looks like.

**2. The student must be able to compare their current work to that standard.**
Even if the student knows what quality looks like in general, they need the ability to look at their own work and see the gap. This is harder than it sounds. It's much easier to spot weaknesses in someone else's work than in your own — which is why peer assessment (Section 8) is so valuable as a stepping stone.

**3. The student must know what action to take to close the gap.**
Seeing the gap isn't enough. The student needs to know what to do about it. This is where actionable feedback matters — not just "this isn't good enough" but "here's what to do to make it better."

> [!info]
> **Sadler's framework is your feedback checklist.** Before giving feedback, ask: (1) Does this student know what quality looks like for this task? If not, show them an exemplar first. (2) Can they see how their work compares? If not, guide the comparison. (3) Do they know what to do next? If not, give them a specific action. If any condition is missing, your feedback will be wasted — fix the missing condition first.

# Building Each Condition

Each of Sadler's three conditions can be deliberately developed:

**Building Condition 1 — knowing what quality looks like:**
- Show exemplars: share strong examples of student work (anonymised) and discuss what makes them effective
- Co-construct success criteria with students: "What would a good version of this look like?"
- Use contrasting examples: show a weak example and a strong example side by side and have students identify the differences

**Building Condition 2 — the ability to compare:**
- Self-assessment routines: "Before you hand this in, check it against the success criteria. Which criteria have you met? Which haven't you met yet?"
- Peer assessment (covered in detail in Section 8): evaluating a peer's work builds the comparison skill
- Predict-and-check: have students predict their performance before checking — this builds calibration over time

**Building Condition 3 — knowing what action to take:**
- Action-oriented feedback: always end feedback with a specific next step
- Strategy instruction: teach students specific strategies for common improvements (how to find stronger evidence, how to check calculations, how to restructure a paragraph)
- Revision time: build time into lessons for students to act on feedback immediately

# Metacognition Is Domain-Specific

Here's something important that many schools get wrong: metacognition is not a general "thinking skill" that transfers automatically across subjects.

A student can be highly metacognitive in maths — they check their work, monitor their understanding, know when they're stuck — and simultaneously poor at monitoring their own writing. The metacognitive skills developed in one domain don't automatically transfer to another.

This means you can't teach metacognition as a separate "thinking skills" lesson and expect it to work in all subjects. You have to develop it within each subject, through the subject content itself. Self-assessment in writing means learning what good writing looks like and how to compare your writing to that standard. Self-assessment in maths means learning what correct working looks like and how to check your own solutions.

> [!warning]
> **The thinking skills trap:** Some schools try to teach metacognition as a separate subject or a generic "learning to learn" course. Research shows this doesn't transfer. A student who can list metacognitive strategies in a "thinking skills" class may not use any of them when they sit down to write an essay. Metacognition must be embedded in subject work — taught through the content, not about it.

# When Feedback Fails: A Diagnostic Tool

Sadler's framework gives you a powerful diagnostic tool when feedback doesn't seem to be working. If you give clear, specific feedback and the student doesn't improve, ask:

- **Is Condition 1 missing?** Does the student actually know what quality looks like? Maybe they've never seen a strong example. Show them one.
- **Is Condition 2 missing?** Can the student compare their work to the standard? Maybe they think their work IS at the standard because they can't see the gap. Guide the comparison — put their work next to an exemplar and ask: "What differences do you notice?"
- **Is Condition 3 missing?** Does the student know what to do? Maybe they see the gap but don't know how to close it. Give them a specific strategy.

This diagnostic approach saves you from a common frustration: "I gave great feedback and nothing changed!" The feedback might have been excellent — but one of Sadler's conditions wasn't met, so the student couldn't use it.

## Section 7: Teaching Self-Regulation
**Slug:** teaching-self-regulation
**Estimated Minutes:** 15

# The Gradual Release

The ultimate goal of feedback is to make itself unnecessary. That sounds paradoxical, but it's the truth. Great feedback builds the student's capacity to assess and regulate their own learning — so over time, they need less external feedback and more self-directed improvement.

This doesn't happen by accident. It happens through a deliberate, gradual transfer of responsibility:

**Stage 1: Teacher regulates.** The teacher identifies what needs to improve, explains why, and tells the student what to do. This is where you start with new concepts or struggling students.

**Stage 2: Teacher prompts student to regulate.** The teacher asks questions that guide the student to self-assess: "Check your work against the success criteria — which ones have you met?" The student begins to do the evaluation, but with prompting.

**Stage 3: Student regulates independently.** The student routinely checks their own work, identifies gaps, and takes action without being asked. They seek feedback strategically — on specific aspects they're unsure about — rather than waiting passively for the teacher to tell them how they did.

The move from Stage 1 to Stage 3 takes time — weeks, months, even longer for some students. And it varies by subject and task complexity. A student might be at Stage 3 for basic maths calculations but Stage 1 for essay writing. That's normal.

> [!info]
> **The transfer timeline:** Don't rush the transfer. Premature independence — asking students to self-regulate before they have the skills — leads to frustration and poor-quality self-assessment. Stay at Stage 2 longer than you think you need to. The prompting stage is where the real skill-building happens.

# Predict-Do-Compare: Building Calibration

One of the most powerful self-regulation routines is remarkably simple: predict, do, compare.

Before starting a task, the student predicts how well they'll do: "I think I'll get 7 out of 10 on this maths practice." Then they do the task. Then they compare their actual result to their prediction.

The point isn't the prediction itself — it's the calibration that develops over time. Students who consistently overestimate their understanding (a very common pattern — the fluency trap) gradually learn to be more accurate. Students who underestimate begin to recognise their strengths.

Over weeks of practice, you'll see students' predictions become more accurate. That accuracy IS metacognition. They're learning to monitor their own understanding — not by being told about monitoring, but by practising it in a structured routine.

You can use this across subjects:
- "Before I check your work, how do you think you did? Which questions are you confident about? Which ones are you unsure of?"
- "Before we look at the success criteria, do you think your paragraph meets them all? Which ones might you have missed?"
- "Before you hand this in, rate your confidence: are you very sure, somewhat sure, or unsure that your answer is correct?"

# The Calibration Problem

Here's a finding that surprises many teachers: students are consistently poor at judging their own understanding. They tend to overestimate — they think they understand more than they actually do.

This is the fluency trap you learned about in LS 101, showing up in self-assessment. A student reads through their notes and thinks "I know this." A student writes a paragraph and thinks "this is good." But when tested, the understanding isn't there. When compared to the success criteria, the paragraph is missing key elements.

This is why self-assessment must be paired with verification. "You think you understand? Show me." "You think your paragraph meets the criteria? Let's check together." Without verification, self-assessment becomes self-deception.

The predict-do-compare routine builds verification into the process. The comparison step forces the student to confront the gap between their perception and reality. Over time, this reduces the gap — their self-assessments become more accurate and more useful.

# Motivation, Belief, and Attribution

Self-regulation depends on belief. A student who believes their ability is fixed — "I'm just not good at maths" — won't use feedback effectively. Why would they? If ability is fixed, no amount of effort or strategy will change the outcome. Feedback becomes pointless.

A student who believes ability can be developed through effort and strategy uses feedback as fuel. The feedback tells them what to work on. They work on it. They improve. The improvement reinforces the belief that effort leads to growth.

But here's the crucial point: growth mindset posters don't work. Telling students "you can do anything if you try hard enough" is empty if it's not backed by evidence. What actually works is attributing success and failure to controllable causes — effort, strategy, and practice quality — rather than fixed ability.

And then backing it up with evidence:
- "Last month, you couldn't solve this type of problem. Now you can. That happened because you practised every day and used the checking strategy we taught you."
- "Your writing has improved since September. Look at this paragraph from then and this one from now. What changed? Your sentence structure — because you've been reading your work aloud and revising."

> [!tip]
> **Make the growth visible.** Keep examples of earlier work so you can show students how they've improved. When a student says "I can't do this," you can say: "You couldn't do this last month either — and now look." Concrete evidence of growth is more powerful than any motivational poster.

# Self-Regulation in Practice: Small Group Routines

Here are practical routines you can build into your small group sessions:

**Start of task:** "Before we begin, look at the success criteria. Which one do you think will be hardest for you today?"

**During the task:** "Pause and check — are you still on track? Is this making sense? If you're stuck, what could you try?"

**End of task:** "Before I look at your work, check it against the success criteria. Put a tick next to the ones you've met and a question mark next to any you're unsure about."

**After feedback:** "What's the ONE thing you're going to focus on improving next time?"

These routines take less than a minute each. They don't add to your workload. But over time, they build the habit of self-regulation — so that students begin to do this automatically, without your prompting.

# Why Peer Assessment Builds Self-Assessment

Here's a finding that seems counterintuitive at first: students get better at assessing their OWN work by first learning to assess SOMEONE ELSE'S work.

The reason is simple. It's much easier to see strengths and weaknesses in someone else's work than in your own. When you wrote something yourself, you know what you meant to say — so you read that intention into the text, even when the text doesn't actually communicate it. When you read someone else's work, you only see what's actually on the page.

Peer assessment is not primarily about giving feedback to peers — though that's a useful side effect. Its primary purpose is building the evaluative skill that students then turn inward. By practising the act of comparing work to a standard and identifying what's strong and what needs improvement, students develop exactly the skill they need to self-assess.

This is Sadler's Condition 2 in action: building the ability to compare current work to a standard. Peer assessment is the most practical way to develop this ability.

> [!info]
> **The bridge metaphor:** Peer assessment is a bridge between receiving teacher feedback and self-assessing independently. The student who can evaluate a peer's work against criteria is halfway to evaluating their own work against the same criteria. The bridge goes: teacher assesses → student assesses peer → student assesses self.

# Structures That Work

Peer assessment needs structure. Without it, students give vague praise ("I liked it"), unhelpful criticism ("it's boring"), or socially motivated responses ("it's great!" — because the author is their friend). Here are structures that produce useful peer assessment:

**Two Stars and a Wish:**
Students identify two specific strengths ("stars") and one specific area for improvement ("wish"). The key word is specific — "I liked it" doesn't count. "Your description of the forest makes me feel like I'm there because you used sounds, not just sights" counts.

**Ladder of Feedback:**
A four-step protocol developed by Harvard's Project Zero:
1. **Clarify:** Ask questions to understand the work ("What did you mean by this part?")
2. **Value:** Identify specific strengths ("The way you organised your argument made it easy to follow")
3. **Suggest:** Offer specific, constructive suggestions ("What if you added a counter-argument in paragraph 3?")
4. **Raise questions:** Pose questions for the author to consider ("I wonder whether your conclusion addresses all the points you raised?")

**Gallery Walk:**
Work is displayed (anonymously, especially at first). Students move around the room, reading the work and leaving specific feedback on sticky notes. Because the work is anonymous, students focus on the quality of the work rather than who created it.

# Risks and How to Manage Them

Peer assessment can go wrong. Here are the common risks and how to manage them:

**Risk: Social pressure.** Students don't want to criticise their friends' work. They either give only positive feedback or hold back honest criticism.
**Mitigation:** Start with anonymous work. Use work from a previous year or from students in another group (with permission). Once students are comfortable evaluating anonymous work, gradually introduce peer-to-peer assessment.

**Risk: Incorrect feedback.** Students may not have the knowledge to give accurate feedback. They might mark something correct as wrong, or miss real errors.
**Mitigation:** Provide clear, specific criteria. Model the assessment process — show students how YOU would assess a piece of work, thinking aloud. Check peer feedback before students act on it, at least initially.

**Risk: Unequal dynamics.** In mixed-stage groups, stronger students may dominate the feedback process, or weaker students may feel their feedback isn't valued.
**Mitigation:** Use pairs before groups. In a pair, both students participate equally. Provide sentence starters that scaffold the feedback: "One thing that works well is... because..." and "One suggestion I have is... because..."

> [!warning]
> **Always pairs first.** The number one mistake with peer assessment is starting with group feedback before students have practised in pairs. Groups introduce social complexity — status hierarchies, dominant voices, passive participants. Pairs are simpler, safer, and more egalitarian. Build the skills in pairs, then expand to groups once the norms and skills are established.

# Modelling Is Non-Negotiable

Before students assess each other's work, they need to see you model the process. This isn't optional — without it, students don't know what good peer feedback looks like.

Here's a practical modelling routine:
1. Show a piece of work (anonymous, or your own)
2. Display the success criteria next to it
3. Think aloud: "Let me compare this work to the criteria. The first criterion says... and in this work I can see... so this criterion is met / not met because..."
4. Model giving feedback: "Based on this, I would say two things that are strong are... and one thing to improve is..."
5. Then have students try it on a second piece of work, in pairs

Model at least twice before asking students to do it independently. And even then, circulate and check — especially the first few times.

# Peer Assessment in Your Context

In your small groups of 8-10 students, peer assessment is especially practical. You can:
- Pair students strategically — not always the same pairs
- Listen in on the conversations and redirect if needed
- Keep the process tight — 5-10 minutes, not a whole lesson
- Start small: one piece of work, one criterion, one round of feedback

Don't try to assess everything through peers. Use it strategically — for tasks where evaluating quality is the learning goal, or where you want students to develop their sense of what "good" looks like.

# From Theory to Monday Morning

This is the most important section of the course — not because it introduces new ideas, but because it's where everything comes together in your actual practice. All the theory in the world is worthless if it doesn't change what happens between you and your students on Monday morning.

We're not going to ask you to do more. We're going to ask you to do differently. The goal is better feedback, not more feedback. Smarter feedback, not harder work.

# The Feedback Audit

Take a piece of student work you reviewed this week. Think about the feedback you actually gave — whether written, verbal, or both. Now run it through the filters you've learned in this course:

**Filter 1: Was it actually feedback, or was it praise?**
Did your response tell the student something about their work relative to a standard? Or did it just express approval? "Great job!" is praise. "Your explanation of the experiment is clear, but your prediction doesn't connect to what you already know about this topic" is feedback.

**Filter 2: Was it directive or detective?**
Did you tell the student exactly what was wrong and how to fix it? Or did you give them enough information to find and fix the problem themselves? "Change 'their' to 'they're' in sentence 4" is directive. "I can see a word that's spelled right but used in the wrong way. Can you find it?" is detective.

**Filter 3: Did it use wise feedback framing?**
Did your feedback communicate high standards AND belief in the student? Or did it either lower expectations ("Good enough!") or crush confidence ("This isn't right")?

**Filter 4: Did it focus on ONE priority?**
Did you pick the most important thing and focus there? Or did you try to fix everything at once?

**Filter 5: Was there a task for the student?**
Did the student have something specific to DO with the feedback? Or was it just information with no action attached?

**Filter 6: Was there a deadline?**
Did the student know WHEN to act on the feedback? "Improve this" is vague. "Revise this section and bring it back tomorrow" is specific.

> [!info]
> **The six-filter audit is not something you do every time.** It's a calibration tool — use it occasionally to check whether your feedback habits are aligned with what you've learned. Over time, the filters become automatic and you won't need to run through them consciously.

# Efficiency: Better Feedback in Less Time

One of the most common objections to everything in this course is: "I don't have time." Let's address that directly.

First, detective feedback is often faster than directive feedback. You don't need to write out corrections — just point to the area and type of issue. "Three errors in paragraph 2 — can you find them?" takes 5 seconds to write. Correcting all three takes 30 seconds.

Second, verbal feedback during the lesson is faster than written feedback after the lesson. In your small groups of 8-10, you can give feedback while circulating: "Your working is good through step 3 — check step 4 again." Takes 10 seconds. The student acts on it immediately. No paper to collect, no marking to do at home.

Third, focused feedback is faster than comprehensive feedback. Reviewing every aspect of a student's work and providing notes on all of it takes 10 minutes per student. Identifying the ONE most important thing and giving focused feedback on that takes 2-3 minutes. Multiply by 8 students, and you've saved nearly an hour.

Remember Teacher C from LS 101? She spent 3 minutes per student giving focused, actionable feedback. Teacher B spent 10 minutes per student giving comprehensive corrections. Teacher C's students improved more. Less time, more learning.

# Building Your Feedback Routine

Here's a practical framework for building regular feedback into your small group sessions without burning out:

**Daily:** Give 2-3 students verbal feedback during the lesson. Not all 8-10 — rotate. Each student gets focused verbal feedback 2-3 times per week. This is your primary feedback channel.

**Weekly:** Review one piece of written work from each student. Give focused written feedback — one priority, detective style, with a task and a deadline. This takes 2-3 minutes per student. 20-25 minutes total.

**Monthly:** Do a calibration check. Use the six-filter audit on your recent feedback. Are you staying focused? Are you making the student think? Are you communicating high standards and belief?

**Each term:** Compare current work to earlier work for each student. Make the growth visible — to yourself and to the student.

> [!tip]
> **The one-change commitment:** Don't try to change everything about your feedback practice at once. Pick ONE thing from this course that you're going to implement starting next week. Just one. Master it. Make it routine. Then add another. Sustainable change is gradual change.

# Peer Accountability

Research on professional development shows that teachers who share their commitment with a colleague are significantly more likely to follow through than those who keep it private.

Find one colleague — in your team, in your school, wherever works. Tell them: "I'm going to do [one specific thing] differently in my feedback practice this week." Ask them to check in with you next week. Then do the same for them.

This isn't about policing each other. It's about creating a tiny accountability structure that makes it harder to let the commitment slide when the week gets busy — and the week always gets busy.

#### Q10: The fluency trap and self-assessment
A student tells you she's confident she understands decimals. She says, "I reviewed my notes and it all makes sense." Based on what you've learned about the fluency trap and self-assessment, what should you do?
- A) Ask her to demonstrate her understanding by solving a problem without her notes ✓
- B) Trust her — she knows her own understanding best
- C) Give her more notes to review
- D) Move on — if she says she understands, there's nothing more to do — so the response focuses on visible behaviour rather than learning conditions
**Explanation:** The fluency trap means that reviewing notes creates a feeling of familiarity that students mistake for understanding. The student feels confident because the material is familiar, not because she can actually use it. The solution is verification: ask her to demonstrate understanding without her notes — through retrieval practice, problem-solving, or explaining the concept in her own words. If she can do it, great. If she can't, she's discovered a gap before it causes problems later.

#### Q11: Detective vs directive across the spectrum
A student who is new to writing essays struggles to construct a basic paragraph. The teacher gives facilitative feedback: "What do you think could be stronger in this paragraph?" The student looks confused and doesn't respond. What went wrong?
- A) The student isn't trying hard enough
- B) Facilitative feedback is always the most effective type
- C) The teacher should have given praise instead
- D) The feedback was too far along the spectrum ✓
**Explanation:** The feedback spectrum runs from directive to detective to facilitative. Facilitative feedback ("What do you think could be stronger?") requires the student to already have a sense of what quality looks like and be able to self-evaluate. A student who is new to essay writing likely doesn't have these skills yet. The teacher should use directive feedback (for brand-new concepts) or detective feedback (when the student understands the basics) — and move toward facilitative feedback gradually as the student develops self-assessment capacity.

#### Q12: Wise feedback vs empty praise
A teacher consistently tells a struggling student "You're doing great! Keep going!" even when the work is below the expected standard. Over several months, the student stops improving. The most likely explanation is:
- A) The student has reached their natural ability limit
- B) The student needs more challenging work, not better feedback — as a way to protect pace, order, and coverage in the lesson
- C) The student senses the teacher has low expectations and has stopped trying to exceed them ✓
- D) Praise is harmful and should never be used in the classroom
**Explanation:** When a teacher consistently praises weak work, the student eventually perceives — often unconsciously — that the teacher doesn't believe they can do better. The praise becomes a ceiling: "This is the best the teacher expects from me." Research on wise feedback shows that students benefit most when critical feedback is paired with high standards and genuine belief in their ability. The answer isn't to eliminate praise, but to ensure that critical, honest feedback accompanies it when improvement is needed.

#### Q13: Metacognition is domain-specific
A school teaches a weekly "thinking skills" lesson where students learn metacognitive strategies like planning, monitoring, and self-checking. The strategies are practised using abstract puzzles and games. Teachers report that students can describe the strategies but don't use them in their regular subject work. Why?
- A) The students are too young for metacognition
- B) Metacognition is domain-specific and must be developed within each subject using subject content ✓
- C) The strategies are wrong — different metacognitive strategies are needed — with responsibility placed mainly on the student rather than the environment
- D) The weekly lesson isn't frequent enough — it should be daily
**Explanation:** Research shows that metacognition is domain-specific. A student who learns to "plan before starting" in an abstract puzzle context doesn't automatically apply planning to their essay writing or maths problem-solving. Metacognitive skills must be developed within each subject, using the content of that subject. Self-assessment in writing means learning what good writing looks like; self-assessment in maths means learning how to check calculations. Generic "thinking skills" lessons rarely transfer.

#### Q14: The feedback routine
A teacher in a small group of 9 students wants to build a sustainable feedback routine. She currently spends 15 minutes per student per week writing comprehensive corrections. She's exhausted. Which approach would be both more effective and more sustainable?
- A) Give 2-3 students verbal feedback daily during lessons, and write focused feedback on ONE priority per student weekly ✓
- B) Reduce feedback to once per month to save time
- C) Only give feedback to the three weakest students — while postponing the harder question of what the evidence actually shows
- D) Replace all teacher feedback with peer feedback
**Explanation:** The most effective and sustainable approach combines daily verbal feedback (fast, immediate, rotated across students) with weekly written feedback focused on ONE priority per student. Daily verbal feedback takes seconds per student. Weekly written feedback on one priority takes 2-3 minutes per student. This is far less time than 15 minutes of comprehensive correction — and produces more learning because the feedback is focused and actionable. Every student needs feedback, not just struggling ones, and peer feedback supplements but doesn't replace teacher feedback.

#### Q15: Connecting feedback to ownership
In a no-grades school, a teacher gives regular, specific, actionable feedback using the detective principle and wise feedback framing. Over the course of a year, what is the most important outcome she should expect?
- A) Students produce perfect work with no errors
- B) Students become dependent on the teacher's feedback for every task
- C) Students no longer need any interaction with the teacher
- D) Students become increasingly able to assess their own work and regulate their own learning ✓
**Explanation:** The ultimate goal of feedback is not perfect work or teacher dependency — it's student ownership of learning. Over time, effective feedback builds the student's capacity to do what the teacher does: evaluate work against a standard, identify gaps, and take action. This is the gradual transfer from teacher regulation to student self-regulation. Students don't become independent overnight, and they never stop benefiting from feedback — but they become increasingly capable of self-assessment and self-correction. In a no-grades school, this capacity IS the measure of success.
