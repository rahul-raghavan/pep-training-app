## Section 1: The Prepared Environment
**Slug:** the-prepared-environment
**Estimated Minutes:** 20

### Content

**Handbook anchor:** Elementary Handbook, Chapter 10 — *The Prepared Environment*.

# The Environment as Third Teacher

> "The first aim of the prepared environment is, as far as it is possible, to render the growing child independent of the adult."
> — Maria Montessori, The Secret of Childhood

In a Montessori classroom, the environment is not just a backdrop. It is a living, breathing participant in the child's education — what many Montessori educators call the **third teacher** (the first being the child themselves, the second being the adult guide).

Everything in the room — every shelf, every material, every piece of art on the wall — either supports the child's development or gets in the way. There is no neutral. A cluttered shelf communicates chaos. A beautiful, ordered shelf communicates: *This is a place where important work happens. You belong here.*

The prepared environment is your most powerful tool. Get this right, and much of the rest follows. Get it wrong, and no amount of brilliant storytelling or clever presentations will make up for it.

# The Physical Environment: Order and Beauty

Walk into a well-prepared Montessori elementary classroom and you will notice something immediately: it is **beautiful**. Not decorated — beautiful. There is a difference.

Beauty in a Montessori classroom means:

- **Natural materials** — wood, glass, ceramic, metal. These communicate respect. Plastic communicates disposability.
- **Plants and living things** — a classroom aquarium, potted plants that children tend, a nature table that changes with the seasons.
- **Art** — real art, not cartoon posters. Reproductions of great paintings, children's own best work mounted with care.
- **Order** — every material has a place, every place has a purpose. Children know where things live and return them there.

Order is not rigidity. It is the foundation of freedom. When a child knows exactly where the geometry materials are, they can choose them independently. When the shelves are cluttered and disorganised, the child depends on the adult to find what they need.

> [!tip]
> Walk through your classroom with fresh eyes. Kneel down to the child's height. What do you see? Is it inviting? Is it clear? Could a new child find what they need without asking an adult?

# Accessibility and Movement

The elementary classroom is designed for **independent access and free movement**:

- Shelves are open and at child height — nothing is locked away or placed out of reach
- Tables and chairs can be rearranged for individual, pair, or group work
- Floor space is available for spreading out large work — timelines, bead chains, maps
- Children move freely between areas without asking permission
- Materials for practical life — cleaning supplies, repair tools — are accessible so children can care for their own space

Movement is not a problem to manage. It is a **developmental need**. The second-plane child thinks better when their body is engaged. Sitting still for long periods is not concentration — it is compliance.

| Traditional Classroom | Montessori Prepared Environment |
|---|---|
| Desks in rows facing the teacher | Tables in clusters, floor mats, varied seating |
| Materials stored in teacher's cupboard | Materials on open shelves, accessible to all |
| Movement requires permission | Movement is free within community agreements |
| Walls covered in commercial posters | Walls show real art and children's meaningful work |
| One-size-fits-all workspace | Multiple work areas for different kinds of activity |
| Teacher's desk dominates the room | Guide has a small, unobtrusive workspace |

# The Intellectual Environment: A Landscape of Ideas

The physical layout is only half the story. The **intellectual environment** is the landscape of ideas, questions, and provocations that fill the room.

In a well-prepared elementary classroom, you will find:

- **Reference materials** — dictionaries, atlases, encyclopaedias, field guides, and age-appropriate non-fiction. These are not decorations. They are working tools that children use daily.
- **Montessori materials** — the golden bead material, geometry cabinets, grammar boxes, history timelines, puzzle maps. Each one embodies an abstract idea in concrete form.
- **Research tools** — magnifying glasses, microscopes, measuring instruments, compasses, rulers, scales.
- **Provocation displays** — a collection of rocks with a question card ("How old do you think these are?"), a timeline with gaps ("What happened here?"), a mystery object on the science shelf.

The intellectual environment should say to the child: *There is so much to discover here. Where will you begin?*

# The Social Environment: A Multi-Age Community

The mixed-age grouping (6-9 or 9-12) is not a convenience — it is a **deliberate design choice** that creates a natural social fabric.

**Younger children** watch older ones and see what is possible. They absorb vocabulary, work habits, and social skills simply by being in the room. They have mentors who are not adults.

**Older children** teach what they know, which deepens their own understanding. They develop leadership, patience, and empathy. When a nine-year-old explains long division to a seven-year-old, both children learn.

This community runs on **cooperation, not competition**. There are no grades to compare. No class rankings. Children are not measured against each other — they are measured against their own growth.

Within this community, conflict is inevitable and welcome. The elementary child is building a moral sense, and conflict is the raw material. When two children disagree about how to share a material, they are practising the skills of negotiation, compromise, and justice.

**The Peace Corner** is a dedicated space — sometimes a small table with a flower, a calming object, and a set of conflict resolution steps — where children can go to settle disputes or simply find calm. It is not a punishment corner. It is a tool for self-regulation and peacemaking that children learn to use independently.

# The Emotional and Spiritual Environment

Beyond the physical, intellectual, and social, there is a dimension that is harder to describe but easy to feel when you walk into a room: the **emotional and spiritual atmosphere**.

**Emotional safety** means:

- Children can take risks without fear of ridicule
- Mistakes are treated as part of learning, never as failures
- Every child feels seen and known — not just the loud ones, not just the high-achievers
- The guide's tone is warm, calm, and respectful — even on difficult days

**Wonder and reverence** mean:

- The guide approaches knowledge with genuine awe — not as information to be transmitted, but as something magnificent to be shared
- There is space for silence, for looking closely, for saying "I don't know — let's find out"
- Nature is treated with respect — the classroom garden, the animals, the changing seasons are not add-ons but central to the experience

> "The environment must be rich in motives which lend interest to activity and invite the child to conduct his own experiences."
> — Maria Montessori, The Absorbent Mind

# The Guide's Role in Maintaining the Environment

The prepared environment is not something you set up in August and forget about. It is a **living system** that requires constant attention.

**Daily:** Scan the shelves. Is everything in its place? Are materials complete and in good repair? Is the room clean and inviting? Are the plants watered? Is the nature table current?

**Weekly:** Rotate materials based on what children are working on. Remove what is no longer being used. Add new provocations. Check that reference materials are accessible and current.

**Seasonally:** Rethink the layout. Are traffic patterns working? Do children have enough floor space? Are the shelves reflecting the current interests and developmental levels of the children? Bring in seasonal elements — autumn leaves, spring seedlings, winter light.

The guide's own **presence** is part of the environment. Your calm, your preparation, your genuine interest in the children's work — these set the emotional tone of the room more powerfully than any material on any shelf.

# Extending the Environment Through Going Out

The prepared environment does not end at the classroom walls. For the second-plane child, the world outside is an essential extension of the classroom.

Going Out — which you will study in depth in Section 4 — is how the environment expands to include libraries, museums, parks, workplaces, and the wider community. The classroom should be deliberately incomplete, creating a need for children to go beyond it to find answers, meet experts, and experience the real world.

> [!info]
> **Vignette (6-9):** A seven-year-old notices that the classroom fish tank is looking cloudy. Without being asked, she gets the cleaning supplies from the practical life shelf, follows the illustrated care guide posted nearby, and cleans the tank. She has never been assigned this job — but the environment made it possible for her to take it on.

> [!info]
> **Vignette (9-12):** A group of ten-year-olds is researching biomes. They have used every reference book on the shelf and want more. One child suggests writing to a marine biologist whose book they have been reading. The guide helps them draft the letter, but the idea, the motivation, and the follow-through are theirs. The "incomplete" classroom created the need; the children filled it.

# What Children Actually Do All Day

> "Play is the work of the child."
> — Maria Montessori

Walk into a Montessori elementary classroom during a three-hour work cycle and you will see children doing things that look very different from what happens in a traditional school. Here is what you might observe:

- A child lying on the floor with the Timeline of Life, sketching organisms in a notebook
- Two children working together at a table with the stamp game, solving long multiplication problems
- A small group researching volcanoes, surrounded by books, with one child writing notes and another illustrating
- A child alone at a desk, deeply absorbed in writing a story
- Three children rehearsing a presentation they will give to the class on Friday
- A child watering the classroom plants and checking on the fish
- Two children in a quiet disagreement at the Peace Corner, working through a conflict protocol
- A child reading silently in a corner, legs tucked under her

This is not chaos. It is a room full of children engaged in **meaningful, self-chosen work**. Some are working alone. Some are in pairs or groups. Some are quiet. Some are talking. All of them are building themselves.

# How Children Choose Work

In a traditional school, the teacher decides what every child does and when. In a Montessori classroom, children make real choices. But this freedom is not random — it is supported by several structures:

**Interest.** The child is drawn to a topic, a material, or a question. This is the most powerful engine of deep work. When interest is genuine, concentration follows naturally.

**Presentations.** The guide gives short, precise presentations (called lessons) that introduce children to new materials and ideas. A child cannot choose work they have never seen. Presentations open doors.

**The environment.** The shelves, the displays, the provocations — all of these invite the child to engage. A new fossil on the science shelf. A challenging puzzle on the maths table. A beautiful book about ancient civilisations left open to an intriguing page.

**The work plan.** Many elementary classrooms use a work plan — a simple record where the child notes what they plan to work on and what they actually did. This is not a to-do list imposed by the adult. It is a tool for the child to develop **planning, self-awareness, and follow-through**.

**Social connection.** Children often choose work based on who they want to work with. This is not a problem — it is the social tendency at work. The guide watches to ensure that social connection leads to productive collaboration, not just socialising.

# What About Children Who "Won't Work"?

Every guide has known the child who wanders, the child who avoids, the child who seems to float through the day without settling into anything meaningful. The temptation is to intervene immediately — to assign work, to redirect, to correct.

Montessori's counsel is clear: **observe first, don't correct.**

Before you act, ask yourself:

- Has this child had enough presentations? Do they know what is available to them?
- Is the environment offering enough at the right level — challenging enough to be interesting, accessible enough to be achievable?
- Is something happening outside school — tiredness, hunger, family stress — that is affecting their capacity?
- Is this a temporary phase, or a pattern?

The Montessori approach to a child who won't work is not punishment or pressure. It is **seduction** — making the work so inviting, so interesting, so alive that the child cannot resist.

This might mean:

- Giving a particularly captivating presentation to a small group, with the wandering child nearby
- Inviting the child to help prepare materials for a younger child
- Offering a Going Out opportunity connected to something you know the child cares about
- Simply sitting near the child and doing interesting work yourself — modelling engagement

> [!warning]
> Forcing a child to work defeats the purpose. If a child is completing tasks only because an adult told them to, they are practising compliance, not building the will. The goal is for the child to choose work because they are genuinely drawn to it.

# Great Work

Beyond daily tasks and follow-up activities, the elementary classroom makes space for what we call **Great Work** — extended projects that integrate multiple subjects, require sustained effort, and result in something the child is genuinely proud of.

Great Work might look like:

- A research project on the Indus Valley civilisation that involves reading, writing, timeline work, map-making, and a presentation to the class
- Building a working model of a water purification system after studying the water cycle
- Writing, illustrating, and binding a book about the solar system
- Organising a class market that involves budgeting, advertising, production, and customer service

Great Work is not assigned. It emerges from the child's interest, supported by the guide's presentations and the environment's provocations. It is the ultimate expression of self construction — the child choosing meaningful, challenging, integrated work and carrying it through to completion.

# The Case Against Worksheets

> [!warning]
> Greg MacDonald, whose handbook informs this course, is direct on this point: **worksheets are not Montessori work.**

Here is why:

- Worksheets are **someone else's questions**. The child fills in blanks decided by an adult. There is no ownership, no curiosity, no personal investment.
- Worksheets are **closed-ended**. There is one right answer. The child either gets it or doesn't. There is no room for exploration, wonder, or going deeper.
- Worksheets keep the child **dependent on the adult** — waiting for the next sheet, the next instruction, the next check mark.
- Worksheets feel productive to adults because they produce a **visible paper trail**. But visible output is not the same as meaningful learning.

What should you use instead? **Charts, nomenclature cards, activity cards, and research tasks** — tools that invite the child to engage actively with knowledge rather than passively receive it.

- **Charts** — large, beautiful reference materials that children consult and copy, building their own reference library
- **Nomenclature cards** — three-part cards (picture, label, definition) that children match, sort, and use for self-study
- **Activity cards** — task cards that guide independent investigation: "Collect five different leaves. Compare their shapes. What patterns do you notice?"
- **Research tasks** — open-ended assignments that require the child to find information, organise it, and present it in their own way

# How Work Looks Different: 6-9 vs. 9-12

| Aspect | Lower Elementary (6-9) | Upper Elementary (9-12) |
|---|---|---|
| Work choices | Guided by presentations and interest; shorter tasks | Self-directed research; longer, multi-day projects |
| Materials | Heavy use of concrete Montessori materials | Transitioning to abstraction; more books and research tools |
| Group work | Learning to collaborate; guided by the adult | Peer-led collaboration; self-organised groups |
| Recording | Simple drawings, labels, short sentences | Detailed notes, essays, charts, presentations |
| Work plan | Simple daily plan, often with guide support | Weekly plan managed increasingly by the child |
| Great Work | Emerges with guide encouragement | Initiated and driven by the child |
| Follow-up | Closely connected to the original presentation | Branches widely; child makes their own connections |

> [!info]
> **Vignette (6-9):** After a presentation on the parts of a flower, a group of seven-year-olds dissects real flowers, labels the parts using nomenclature cards, and paints detailed botanical illustrations. One child decides to count the petals of every flower in the school garden and makes a chart. The guide gave a ten-minute presentation. The children's work lasted a week.

> [!info]
> **Vignette (9-12):** A ten-year-old reads about the Great Fire of London in a history book. She decides to research how cities have dealt with fire throughout history — from Rome to Chicago to modern building codes. She interviews a local firefighter (Going Out), builds a timeline, writes an essay comparing ancient and modern fire safety, and presents her findings to the class. The guide's role? One conversation and two resource suggestions.

# Tell, Don't Read

> "Oh mother... she told it from herself."
> — Baker & Greene, Storytelling: Art and Technique

This is perhaps the single most important principle in Montessori storytelling: **tell the story. Do not read it.**

When you read from a page, your eyes are on the book. Your voice flattens. The connection between you and the children is broken. The story becomes words on paper, not a living experience.

When you tell the story, it comes from **inside you**. Your eyes are on the children. Your voice rises and falls naturally. The story becomes a gift you are sharing — something alive between you and them.

Children know the difference instantly. A child who has heard a story told from the heart will say, as that child said: "She told it from herself." That is what you are aiming for.

# Two Kinds of Delivery: Story vs. Presentation

In the Montessori elementary classroom, you will deliver content in two distinct ways. Both are essential. Both require preparation. But they are **fundamentally different** in purpose and technique.

| Aspect | Story | Presentation |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | To inspire wonder and imagination | To demonstrate a skill or concept |
| Tone | Dramatic, sweeping, emotional | Clear, precise, methodical |
| Materials | Charts, props, experiments | Montessori materials, tools, models |
| Audience | Usually the whole class or a large group | Usually a small group or individual |
| Length | 15-40 minutes | 5-15 minutes |
| Follow-up | Children explore freely; many directions | Children practise the specific skill shown |
| Examples | Great Stories, historical narratives, origin tales | How to use the stamp game, how to parse a sentence, how to read a map |
| The guide's role | Storyteller, performer, artist | Demonstrator, model, coach |

Both stories and presentations share one rule: **less is more.** Say what needs to be said. Show what needs to be shown. Then stop. The child's own work is where the real learning happens.

# Preparing a Story: Eight Steps

Preparing a story well is an act of respect — for the story, for the children, and for yourself. Here are eight steps that will serve you for every story you ever tell.

**Step 1: Read for pleasure.** Before you do anything else, read the story simply to enjoy it. Let it move you. If it doesn't move you, it won't move the children.

**Step 2: Outline the plot.** What are the key events? What is the arc? Write a simple outline — beginning, middle, end — with the major turning points. This is your skeleton.

**Step 3: Make it your own.** You are not performing someone else's script. You are telling a story that has passed through you. Change words that feel awkward in your mouth. Add details that make it vivid for your particular children. The story must feel like yours.

**Step 4: Don't memorise word for word — but DO memorise openings and closings.** The opening sets the tone. The closing is what the children carry away. These two moments deserve exact, practised language. Everything in between can be told in your own words.

**Step 5: Use memory strategies.** Visualise the story as a series of scenes. See the locations, the characters, the objects. Walk through the story in your mind as if you were watching a film. This is far more powerful than trying to memorise a text.

**Step 6: See the story.** Close your eyes. Can you see it? Can you see the dark void before the universe began? Can you see the first spark of light? Can you see the first tiny organism floating in the ancient ocean? If you can see it, you can tell it.

**Step 7: Choreograph.** Plan your movements. When will you unroll the timeline? When will you light the candle? When will you drop the food colouring into the water? Your body and your props are part of the story.

**Step 8: Test yourself.** Tell the story out loud — to a mirror, to a friend, to your dog. The first time you tell a story should never be in front of children. Practise until it flows. Then practise once more.

> [!tip]
> You do not need to be a professional actor. You need to be a person who has prepared well and who cares about what they are sharing. Sincerity is more powerful than performance.

# Telling the Story

**Before you begin:**
- The room should be ready. Materials laid out. Charts in position. Lights dimmed if appropriate.
- The children should be settled and expectant. Build a brief moment of silence before you start.
- Take a breath. Centre yourself. You are about to give a gift.

**Voice — pitch, pace, volume:**
- **Vary your pitch.** A monotone kills a story. Let your voice rise with excitement, drop with suspense, soften with tenderness.
- **Control your pace.** Slow down at important moments. Speed up during action. Pause before a revelation. Silence is your most powerful tool.
- **Manage your volume.** Whisper to draw children in. Speak firmly for important ideas. Never shout — if you need to be louder, project from your diaphragm, not your throat.

**Eyes:**
- Look at the children. Not at the ceiling, not at your notes, not at the chart. Your eyes are the connection. When you look at a child during a story, that child feels the story is for them.
- Move your gaze around the group naturally. Don't stare at one child. Don't look over their heads.

**Body:**
- Stand if the story calls for it. Sit if it calls for closeness. Move if movement serves the story.
- Use your hands deliberately — pointing to a chart, miming an action, holding up an object.
- Be still when stillness matters. Constant movement is distracting.

# Delivering a Presentation

A presentation is different from a story. It is shorter, more precise, and focused on demonstrating a specific skill or concept with materials.

**Preparation:**
- Know the material intimately. Practise the steps until your hands move smoothly without hesitation.
- Gather everything you need before you call the children. Nothing kills a presentation like getting up to find a missing piece.
- Know your key point. What is the ONE thing this presentation is about? Everything else is secondary.

**During:**
- Speak slowly and clearly. Use fewer words than you think you need.
- Let the materials do the talking. Your hands and the materials are the show — your voice is the narration.
- Work from left to right (reading direction). Move precisely. Children are watching your hands, not just listening to your words.
- Invite the child to try. The moment of transfer — when the child's hands take over from yours — is the purpose of the whole presentation.

**Ending:**
- End cleanly. "Now it's your turn" or "I'll leave this here for you to work with."
- Do not hover. Give the presentation, transfer it, and step away. Your continued presence communicates: "I don't trust you to do this on your own."

**After:**
- Observe from a distance. Is the child engaged? Confused? Experimenting?
- Record the presentation in your notes so you know what each child has been shown.

**When it goes wrong:**
- You forget a step mid-presentation: pause, take a breath, and continue. Children are forgiving.
- A child is not paying attention: finish the presentation for the others and invite this child another time. Do not scold.
- You realise you are unprepared: it is better to stop gracefully and come back tomorrow than to fumble through. Children lose trust in sloppy presentations.

# Storytelling as Daily Practice

Storytelling is not reserved for Great Stories at the start of the year. It is a **daily practice** that runs through everything you do.

- When you introduce a new area of the classroom, tell the story of why it matters.
- When a conflict arises, tell a story about a similar situation and how it was resolved.
- When a child makes a discovery, help them tell the story of what they found.
- When you give a history lesson, tell it as a story — with characters, tension, and resolution — not as a list of facts and dates.

The elementary child's imagination is fed by stories. Every time you tell one well, you model language, build connection, and light a fire.

> "The teacher, when she begins work in our schools, must have a kind of faith that the child will reveal himself through work."
> — Maria Montessori, The Absorbent Mind

## Section 4: Going Out

**Estimated Minutes:** 20

# Not a Field Trip

> "To let the child go out... is the way to build the man."
> — Maria Montessori, From Childhood to Adolescence

Going Out is one of the most distinctive — and most misunderstood — features of Montessori elementary education. It is **not** a field trip. The difference matters.

| Field Trip | Going Out |
|---|---|
| Planned and organised by adults | Initiated by children based on their work |
| Whole class goes together | Small group (2-4 children) goes independently |
| Destination chosen by the teacher | Destination chosen by the children to answer their question |
| Adults supervise and guide the experience | Children manage the outing with minimal adult presence |
| Happens a few times a year | Happens regularly — weekly or more |
| Follow-up is optional | The outing IS the follow-up to classroom work |
| Purpose: exposure and enrichment | Purpose: answering a real question, meeting a real need |

A field trip says: "We are all going to the museum because the teacher thinks it will be educational."

Going Out says: "We are studying rivers and we need to measure the water flow at the stream behind the library. We called the library to ask if we could come. We planned our route. We are taking our notebooks and measuring tools."

# Why Going Out Matters

Going Out serves five essential purposes in the elementary child's development:

**1. Building the will.** Planning an outing, making phone calls, navigating public spaces, dealing with unexpected problems — all of this builds the executive function and willpower that the second-plane child needs to develop.

**2. Feeding the mind.** The classroom, no matter how well prepared, is finite. The world outside has experts, primary sources, living specimens, real workplaces, and experiences that no classroom can replicate.

**3. Preparing for society.** The 6-12 child is building social skills and civic awareness. Going Out teaches them how to interact with adults who are not their parents or teachers, how to behave in public spaces, how to ask for help politely, and how to represent their school community.

**4. Developing independence.** Going Out is one of the most powerful ways the elementary child moves from dependence to independence. They plan, they execute, they problem-solve — with the adult stepping further and further back as the child grows.

**5. Making Great Work possible.** Many of the most meaningful projects in a Montessori elementary classroom require resources that exist outside the classroom walls. Going Out makes these projects possible.

# Preparing Children: Skills by Age

Going Out is not something you launch on the first day of school. Children need to be prepared — and the level of preparation changes as they grow.

**Ages 6-7: Foundation stage**
- Children go out in small groups, always with an adult
- Focus is on learning the basics: how to walk in public, how to speak to adults politely, how to stay together, how to observe and record
- Outings are short and close — the school garden, a nearby shop, the library next door

**Ages 8-9: Growing independence**
- Children begin to plan more of the outing themselves — choosing the destination, preparing questions
- The adult may accompany but steps back, letting children lead
- Outings extend further — a nearby park, a local business, a community centre
- Children learn to make phone calls, write letters of inquiry, and follow up with thank-you notes

**Ages 10-12: Real independence**
- Children plan and execute outings with minimal adult presence (within the bounds of local regulations and school policy)
- They manage their own transportation (walking routes, public transit in some settings), time, and materials
- Outings may be longer and more ambitious — visiting a university lab, interviewing a professional, attending a council meeting
- Children debrief and integrate what they learned into their ongoing projects

**The stages of Going Out:**
1. The guide models Going Out with the whole class (early days)
2. The guide accompanies small groups and coaches the process
3. Children plan with guidance and go with an accompanying adult
4. Children plan independently and go with minimal adult presence
5. Children plan, execute, and report back autonomously (upper elementary ideal)

# The Two Agendas

Every Going Out has **two agendas** — and the wise guide keeps both in mind.

**The children's agenda:** To answer their question, meet the expert, collect the data, see the thing, get the resource. This is the visible purpose — the reason they planned the outing.

**The adult's agenda:** To develop independence, build social skills, strengthen planning and problem-solving, and give children the experience of navigating the real world. This is the invisible purpose — the deeper developmental work happening beneath the surface.

The children may think they went to the library to find a book about ancient Rome. The guide knows they also practised planning, time management, polite interaction with a librarian, collaborative decision-making, and wayfinding. Both agendas matter.

# Creating the Need: The Deliberately Incomplete Classroom

Here is a powerful idea: **the classroom should be deliberately incomplete.**

If the classroom has everything a child could ever need, there is no reason to go out. But if the guide intentionally leaves gaps — a question that can't be answered by any book on the shelf, a project that requires a real expert, a specimen that can only be found outdoors — then Going Out becomes a natural, necessary part of learning.

This is not negligence. It is design. The guide knows what is missing and why. The gaps are invitations.

> [!tip]
> Look at your shelves. Is there a question your students have been asking that cannot be answered by anything in the classroom? That is your invitation for a Going Out.

# Safety Essentials

Going Out involves real responsibility. Safety is not optional.

**Permissions:** Have a standing permission system in place. Many Montessori schools use a blanket permission form at the start of the year that covers regular Going Out within a defined radius, plus specific permission for longer or unusual outings.

**Child information cards:** Each child who goes out should carry a card with the school's contact information, the child's name, emergency contacts, and any medical information. These cards travel with the group.

**Ratios:** Follow your school's and local regulations for adult-to-child ratios. For young children, this may be 1:4 or 1:6. For older children who have been well-prepared, the ratio may relax — but never below what regulations require.

**Buddy system:** Children go out in pairs or small groups, never alone. The buddy system is both a safety measure and a social learning tool.

**First aid:** At least one person in every Going Out group should know where the nearest first aid resources are. For outings beyond the immediate neighbourhood, a basic first aid kit should travel with the group.

**Roll call:** Before leaving and upon returning, count heads. This sounds obvious. Do it anyway. Every time.

**Emergency training:** Children should know what to do if they get separated from the group, if someone is hurt, or if plans change. Practise these scenarios before they happen.

**Chaperone briefing:** If parents or other adults accompany a Going Out, brief them clearly. Their role is to **ensure safety, not to lead the experience**. The children lead. The adult watches, supports, and intervenes only for safety.

> [!warning]
> Going Out that is poorly planned or unsupervised is not Montessori — it is negligence. Freedom and responsibility go together. Prepare children thoroughly, manage safety carefully, and build independence gradually.

# When Going Out Goes Wrong

Sometimes it will. A child gets lost for a moment. The expert they planned to visit is unavailable. The bus is late. It rains.

These are not failures. They are **learning opportunities** — often the most powerful of all. How the group responds to the unexpected teaches resilience, flexibility, and problem-solving in ways no classroom exercise can.

The guide's role when things go wrong:

- Stay calm. Your calm is their anchor.
- Help the children problem-solve: "What can we do now?"
- Debrief afterwards: "What happened? What did we learn? What would we do differently?"
- Distinguish between problems caused by poor planning (address these) and problems caused by the unpredictable world (celebrate the learning).

> [!info]
> **Vignette (6-9):** Two seven-year-olds are studying birds. Their classroom has a bird identification book but only three species from their local area are pictured. The guide suggests they visit the park with a parent volunteer and their binoculars. They spot four species not in their book, draw them from life, and return to the classroom determined to make their own field guide. The deliberately incomplete classroom created the need.

> [!info]
> **Vignette (9-12):** A group of eleven-year-olds researching local government wants to understand how city budgets work. They write a letter to a city councillor, receive an invitation to observe a budget meeting, plan their visit (transportation, questions to ask, dress code, timing), attend the meeting, take notes, and return to create a presentation for the class explaining what they learned. The guide's role was to help them draft the initial letter. Everything else was theirs.

## Section 5: Learning Journals and Planning

# Making Learning Visible

> "What the hand does the mind remembers."
> — Maria Montessori

In the Montessori elementary classroom, much of the child's most important work is invisible to the outside eye. Concentration, problem-solving, moral growth, social learning — none of these show up on a test. So how do we make learning visible — to the child, to the guide, and to the family?

The answer is the **learning journal**.

A learning journal is not a diary. It is not a workbook. It is the child's own record of what they have explored, discovered, created, and thought about. Over time, it becomes a rich, personal map of the child's intellectual journey.

# What Goes in a Learning Journal

**Minimum entries** (especially for younger or newer children):
- What I worked on today
- What I learned or noticed
- What I want to explore next

**Richer entries** (as children grow and the practice deepens):
- Detailed notes from research and reading
- Sketches, diagrams, and labelled illustrations
- Questions that arose during work — both answered and unanswered
- Reflections on group work — what went well, what was hard
- Plans for Great Work, including timelines and steps
- Records of Going Out — what they did, whom they met, what they found
- Personal responses to stories, experiments, and presentations
- Connections noticed between different subjects or topics

The journal grows richer as the child grows. A seven-year-old's journal might contain simple sentences and drawings. An eleven-year-old's journal might read like a researcher's notebook — detailed, reflective, and full of questions.

# Starting the Journal Tradition

**For the youngest children (new to the classroom or age 6-7):**
- Begin with simple prompts: "Draw what you worked on. Write one sentence about it."
- Model journal entries by sharing your own — show children what a thoughtful entry looks like
- Make journal time a predictable part of the day — same time, same routine
- Celebrate the practice, not the product — the act of reflection matters more than the quality of the entry

**For older or experienced children (ages 8-12):**
- Journals become more detailed and self-directed
- Children begin to use them as planning tools — mapping out projects, tracking progress, recording sources
- Guide may introduce specific reflection prompts occasionally: "What surprised you this week?" or "What was the hardest thing you worked on?"

**Key principles for learning journals:**

- **Consistency.** Journals work because they are a habit. A journal written once a week is valuable. A journal written once a month is forgotten.
- **Specificity.** "I did maths" is not a journal entry. "I worked on long division with the stamp game and got stuck when there was a remainder. I figured out that I needed to exchange" — that is a journal entry.
- **Don't check like police.** The journal is the child's. It is not homework to be graded. The guide reads journals to understand the child's journey, not to correct spelling or give marks.
- **Use in conferences.** The journal is a powerful tool for child-guide conferences (see below) and for parent meetings. It shows, in the child's own words and pictures, what they have been learning.

# The Child-Guide Conference

The conference is a regular, one-on-one conversation between the guide and the child about the child's work, growth, and plans. The learning journal is often the starting point.

**In lower elementary (6-9):**
- Conferences happen **frequently** — weekly or even more often, but briefly (5-10 minutes)
- Focus is on: What have you been working on? What do you enjoy? What feels hard? What do you want to try next?
- The guide gently steers, makes suggestions, and gives new presentations based on what the conference reveals
- Tone is warm, encouraging, and curious

**In upper elementary (9-12):**
- Conferences happen **less frequently** but go **deeper** — biweekly or monthly, 15-20 minutes
- Focus shifts to: How is your Great Work progressing? Are you challenging yourself? What goals do you have? Where are you stuck?
- The child takes more ownership of the conference — reviewing their own journal, identifying patterns, setting goals
- The guide acts as coach and consultant, asking probing questions rather than giving answers

# Planning and Records for the Guide

While the child keeps a learning journal, the guide keeps their own records. These serve a different purpose — they are the guide's tool for ensuring that every child is seen, reached, and supported.

**Yearly planning:** At the start of the year, the guide maps the major presentations, Great Stories, and Key Lessons across the curriculum. This is a framework, not a rigid schedule. It ensures nothing essential is forgotten while leaving room for children's interests to lead.

**Monthly check-in:** Each month, review your records. Which children have received many presentations? Which have received few? Which areas of the curriculum are thriving? Which are being neglected? Adjust your plans accordingly.

**Weekly planning:** Each week, plan the presentations you will give based on your observations, conferences, and the children's expressed interests. Identify which children need individual attention.

**Daily question:** At the end of each day, ask yourself: **"Am I reaching each child?"**

This is the question that keeps the guide honest. It is easy for certain children to become invisible — the quiet ones, the ones who manage themselves, the ones who don't demand attention. The daily question forces you to notice them.

> [!tip]
> Keep a simple grid with children's names on one axis and curriculum areas on the other. Each time you give a presentation or have a conference, make a mark. Patterns will emerge quickly — and the gaps will tell you where to focus.

# Testing and Transition

Where do tests fit in a Montessori classroom? Greg MacDonald's view is nuanced and practical: **tests have a place, particularly in upper elementary (9-12).**

Here is why:

- Many children will transition to middle schools that use tests. Preparing them for that reality is an act of care, not a betrayal of Montessori principles.
- Tests, when used well, can help children practise the skill of demonstrating what they know under pressure — a real-world skill.
- Tests should never be the primary form of assessment. They are one data point among many — alongside the journal, the conference, the guide's observations, and the child's own self-assessment.

The key is context. A test given as a **supportive checkpoint** — "Let's see what you know so far" — is very different from a test given as a **judgment** — "This is your grade." The first supports growth. The second creates anxiety and dependence on external validation.

# Freedom and the Journal Connection

There is a beautiful paradox at the heart of Montessori education: **structure enables freedom.**

The learning journal is a perfect example. The discipline of reflecting daily — writing down what you worked on, what you noticed, what you wonder — gives the child a structure within which freedom thrives. Without the journal, the child's work can float, disconnected and forgotten. With the journal, the child can see their own path, notice their own patterns, and take ownership of their own learning.

Freedom without reflection is chaos. Reflection without freedom is compliance. The learning journal holds both together.

> "The child who concentrates is immensely happy."
> — Maria Montessori, The Absorbent Mind

> [!info]
> **Vignette (6-9):** A seven-year-old opens her journal at the end of the morning work cycle. She draws a picture of the leaf she examined under the magnifying glass and writes: "I saw the veins. They look like rivers. I want to know why." The guide reads this during the afternoon and plans a presentation on plant vascular systems for the following week. The journal became a bridge between the child's curiosity and the guide's response.

> [!info]
> **Vignette (9-12):** An eleven-year-old reviews his journal during a conference with the guide. He notices he has spent three weeks on his ancient Egypt project but hasn't done any mathematics. He says, "I think I need to get back to fractions." The guide asks, "What would help you get started?" He decides to ask a classmate who is good at fractions to work with him. The journal made his own pattern visible — and he took action.
