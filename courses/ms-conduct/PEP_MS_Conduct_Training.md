## Section 1: Why We're Doing This
**Slug:** why-this-matters
**Estimated Minutes:** 15

### Content

**Handbook anchor:** Middle School Handbook, Chapter 11 — *When Things Go Hard*.

# The Problem We're Solving

Right now, one student can do the same thing on Monday and Friday — and get a completely different response depending on which adult is nearby.

That's not a teacher problem. It's a systems problem. Without a shared framework, every adult improvises based on their own instincts, energy levels, and thresholds. Some of us are stricter. Some of us let things go. Neither is wrong in isolation — but the inconsistency is the problem.

**What students learn from inconsistency:**
- They learn to read the adult, not the expectation
- They learn that norms are negotiable depending on who's watching
- They lose trust in the system — because the system is unpredictable

**What we lose:**
- Authority that comes from consistency, not intensity
- The ability to spot patterns (because we're not tracking)
- Each other's support (because we don't know what the last adult did)

> [!info]
> **This framework does not replace your judgement.** It gives you a shared starting point so that when you respond, the next adult can continue from the same place. The framework is the default; the room is the reality. Trust the room — then tell the team.

## What Progressive Schools Get Right

Research across progressive schools globally — Montessori Erdkinder, Acton Academy, High Tech High, and Indian schools like Riverside, Rishi Valley, Shibumi, Aarohi, and Prakriti — converges on the same finding:

**Progressive schools reject punitive discipline as the primary lever, but they do not abandon consequences.**

Instead, they build clear consequence ladders rooted in repair, peer accountability, and community responsibility. And they invest heavily in adult consistency before asking anything of the children.

> "The real teeth in a progressive framework is not punishment. It is responsibility — the demand that a young person face the harm they caused, understand it, and fix it. That is harder than detention."
> — Synthesis from the IIRP, Positive Discipline, and Acton Academy literature

## Six Principles Behind Every Choice

Every card, every escalation threshold, every notification trigger in this framework is built on these six principles. If a specific consequence ever feels off, return to these and judge whether the response honours them.

| Principle | What it means in practice |
|-----------|--------------------------|
| Developmental, not punitive | The point of every consequence is that the child learns something — not that they suffer |
| Real consequences, not vague disapproval | "Be respectful" is not a consequence. "Apologise to Ms K in person before the next session" is |
| Related, respectful, reasonable, revealed | If a consequence isn't all four, we're drifting toward punishment (from Positive Discipline) |
| Repair before exclusion | The default for harm is restoration, not removal. Removal happens when restoration cannot keep the community safe |
| Adults consistent, then children clear | Children cannot follow norms we ourselves apply differently |
| Structure first, individual second | If 8+ children struggle with the same expectation, the design is the problem, not the children |

> [!tip]
> **The Four R's test (Positive Discipline):** Before applying any consequence, check — Is it Related to the behaviour? Respectful (no shame)? Reasonable (proportionate)? Revealed in advance (the student knew this would happen)? If any of the four is missing, you've crossed into punishment. The predictable result is Resentment, Revenge, Rebellion, or Retreat.

**Explanation:** Consistency is about the starting point, not rigid uniformity. Teachers still use judgement — but they judge from the same baseline. The goal is that students learn to read the expectations, not the adult.

A parent complains that their child was asked to clean up a mess they didn't make as a consequence for littering yesterday. Using the Four R's test, what's the problem?
- A) It's not Reasonable — cleaning is too harsh
- B) It's not Respectful — the child is being shamed
- C) It's not Related ✓
- D) It's not Revealed — the child didn't know this would happen
**Explanation:** The consequence must be related to the specific behaviour. Cleaning up their own mess is related; cleaning someone else's mess is group punishment in disguise. - D) Pause the session and bring it to the team as a design problem ✓

## Section 2: The Four Cards

# The Four Cards

We use football language because students already understand it. Whistle, Yellow Card, Red Card are the student-facing vocabulary. Structural Review is what we use internally for the fourth tier.

Students learn the card names. They should know what card they were given and why — no mystery, no translation needed.

> [!info]
> **Important:** The first three cards (Whistle, Yellow Card, Red Card) are shared with students. They hear these names. "Structural Review" is internal terminology for adults only.

## Whistle — Coaching

**When:** First instances, minor disruption, developmental struggles.

**What you do:** Name what you see, state the norm, give a choice, follow up privately. Under 90 seconds. Then return to the activity without lingering or shaming.

**Examples:** Side conversations, lateness, leaving spaces messy, dismissing feedback, phone glance, forgetting materials.

**What it sounds like:**
- "That's a side chat. Listening now — we'll come back to ideas in a minute."
- "You're 7 minutes late. What happened?" (private, one line)
- "Look around — what does this space need right now?"

**Documentation:** Mental note or one-line in your personal log. No formal entry unless it recurs in the same week.

**Escalate to Yellow Card when:** The same behaviour returns a third time in the week, or the student ignores the redirect.

> [!tip]
> **Most of what happens in a normal week should be Whistles.** If you're giving Yellow Cards daily, either the expectations need re-teaching or the structure needs redesign.

## Yellow Card — Pause & Reset

**When:** Behaviour prevents the group from functioning, or the student needs a reset. Also for repeated Whistles.

**What you do:** Step the student out briefly. Have a short restorative conversation. Agree on re-entry conditions before they rejoin. Log it in the shared behaviour tracker within 24 hours.

**Examples:** Disruption derailing a session, dismissive talk to a peer, public confrontation, repeated missed deadlines, continuing after someone says "stop."

**What it sounds like:**
- "Take five minutes. I'll come check in."
- Three questions: "What happened? Who was affected? What needs to shift?"
- "Here's how you rejoin: [specific condition]."

**Documentation:** One row in the shared behaviour tracker within 24 hours. The advisor reviews the tracker weekly.

**Parent loop:** If this is a repeat pattern (not a single incident), the advisor sends a short Friday note home.

**Escalate to Red Card when:** The reset doesn't hold, harm has been done to another person, or the student refuses re-entry.

## Red Card — Immediate Removal & Repair

**When:** Harm to identity, dignity, or safety. The non-negotiables.

**What you do:** Stop the behaviour immediately. Support the harmed student first. Remove the offender from the space — calmly, without public escalation. The advisor or Head of Middle School Program takes the student; another adult holds the class. Restorative conference within 24–48 hours. Written repair plan. Parents of both students called the same day.

**Examples:** Identity-based mockery (religion, caste, language, body, gender), deliberate physical harm, retaliation against someone who reported a problem, substance use, deliberate property damage, leaving campus without permission.

**What it sounds like:**
- To the group: "We're stopping. [Student], come with me please."
- To the harmed student (first): "Are you okay? I saw what happened. We're taking this seriously."
- To the offender (separately): "What happened is serious. We're going to talk this through properly — not right now, but today."

**Documentation:** Full write-up in the student file. Shared with all staff who interact with the student.

**Parent loop:** Same-day phone call, always. For both families when another student was harmed.

> [!warning]
> **Identity-based harm is always a Red Card from the first instance.** It never starts at Whistle or Yellow Card, regardless of whether the student "meant it as a joke." This is the single non-negotiable across every sound progressive framework globally.

## Structural Review — Internal Only

**When:** Many students show the same struggle (8+ is the threshold), OR one student's pattern of repeated Red Cards points to a design problem, not just an individual behaviour problem.

**What you do:** The Head of Middle School Program convenes the team, parents, and (where appropriate) the student. Options include: behavioural contract with explicit exit criteria, schedule redesign, in-school or at-home suspension (1–5 days), external support referral, or — rarely — withdrawal. A written agreement is signed by school and family. Review in 4–6 weeks.

**Examples:**
- Widespread passive resistance during a planned session → redesign group size, timing, or format
- Recurring aggression from one student despite good-faith repair attempts → behavioural contract with family
- A safety concern the current setup cannot hold → structural change to the environment

**Documentation:** Full documentation in the student file. Shared with any staff who interact with the student.

**Parent loop:** Always a sit-down meeting with the family. Never just a phone call.

> [!info]
> **Structural Review is a pause for planning, not a punishment.** When suspension (days off) is part of the plan, the return plan is drafted before the suspension begins. The student knows exactly what they're working toward.

- Stop the activity immediately

- Not ask Student B to explain how they feel in front of the group
- Signal to the group that this is being handled seriously
- Not try to resolve the situation in the moment — a restorative conference comes later
**AI Prompt:** Evaluate whether the response: (1) prioritises the harmed student first, (2) removes the offender calmly without public humiliation, (3) does NOT try to have a full restorative conversation in the moment — this comes later, (4) does NOT minimise the harm ("I'm sure they didn't mean it"), (5) signals seriousness to the group. This is a Red Card scenario — the response should reflect that gravity.

## Section 3: The Decision Flow

**Estimated Minutes:** 15

**Handbook anchor:** Middle School Handbook, Chapter 11 — *When Things Go Hard*.

# When You Have 30 Seconds to Decide

Something happens in front of you. The group is watching. You need to act. Walk this sequence in your head:

**Step 1: Is anyone unsafe right now?**
If yes → Remove the student or the risk first. Everything else waits. This is a Red Card minimum.

**Step 2: Has harm been done to another person's identity, dignity, or safety?**
If yes → Red Card. Support the harmed student first. Remove the offender. Restorative conference within 48 hours. Parents same day.

**Step 3: Is the group no longer functioning?**
If yes → Yellow Card. Pause the activity. Name the problem. Reset or end early.

**Step 4: Is this a first instance or a repeat?**
First time → Start at the card the behaviour warrants (usually a Whistle).
Third time this week → Move up one card and loop the advisor.

**Step 5: Are 8+ students struggling with this same thing?**
If yes → This is a Structural Review. Bring it to the team. Redesign before adding individual consequences.

> [!warning]
> **If the decision flow points one way and your read of the room points the other — your read wins.** Then tell the team, so we can adjust the default if the framework is off.

> [!tip]
> **When in doubt about whether something is a Yellow or Red Card, default UP, not down.** It is much easier to de-escalate ("we treated this seriously and on reflection it was a Yellow Card situation — here's what we did") than to under-react and discover later that a child was harmed and we missed it.

## The Principle Behind the Flow

The consequence should be **as small as possible — and as consistent as possible.** If in doubt, do the smaller thing, but do the same smaller thing every time.

Consistency over intensity. A mild consequence applied every time beats a harsh one applied sometimes.

- A) Ask both students what happened
- B) Give the first student a Yellow Card — with responsibility placed mainly on the student rather than the environment
- C) Separate both students immediately

**Explanation:** Step 1 of the decision flow is always: "Is anyone unsafe right now?" In this case, one student is standing up angrily and there was a physical action. Ensure safety first — position yourself between them if needed. Then move to Step 2 (was someone harmed?) and respond accordingly.

Confiscating for an entire term would fail the "Reasonable" test.

**AI Prompt:** Evaluate whether the response: (1) addresses the behaviour (disruption) rather than assuming intent (mocking), (2) is proportionate to a first-instance minor disruption, (3) doesn't publicly shame or accuse, (4) includes a plan to follow up privately. Flag if the response jumps to Yellow or Red Card without first trying a Whistle-level redirect.

# How to Talk About Behaviour

Four frameworks shape the language we use. Each is best in a different moment:

| Framework | Best for | Core question |
|-----------|----------|---------------|
| NVC (Nonviolent Communication) | One-on-one conversations, de-escalation | "What need is this behaviour trying to meet?" |
| Restorative Questions | After harm has occurred, repair conversations | "Who was harmed and what do they need?" |
| Positive Discipline (Mistaken Goals) | Understanding persistent patterns | "What mistaken belief about belonging is driving this?" |
| Coaching language | In-the-moment redirects (Whistle tier) | "What do I need the student to do right now?" |

## NVC Script — For Private Follow-Ups

The four-step NVC process separates fact from judgement:

**1. Observation** (what you saw, not your interpretation):
"I noticed you turned in a blank worksheet."

**2. Feeling** (yours, briefly):
"I'm concerned..."

**3. Need** (what matters and why):
"...because I need to understand whether you're following the material, and I want you to feel supported."

**4. Empathy + Request:**
"It sounds like something about this assignment isn't working for you. What's going on? Would you be willing to tell me what would help — more time, a different approach, a check-in?"

> [!tip]
> **NVC is NOT about being soft.** It's about being clear and direct without triggering defensiveness. "I noticed you arrived 10 minutes late three times this week" is an observation. "You clearly don't care about this class" is a judgement. The first opens a conversation. The second closes it.

## Restorative Questions — For Repair Conversations

After harm has occurred, use this sequence (can be a 5-minute hallway conversation or a full restorative conference):

1. **What happened?** (Let the student tell their version without interruption)
2. **What were you thinking at the time?**
3. **Who has been affected by this?** (Help them see beyond themselves)
4. **What do they need from you?**
5. **What will help repair this?** (The student proposes the repair, not you)

For the harmed student, ask:
1. **What happened from your perspective?**
2. **How has this affected you?**
3. **What do you need to feel okay here again?**

> [!warning]
> **Never force a harmed student into a restorative conversation.** They get to choose whether they want to sit with the other student. If they don't, the offender still does the repair work — a written reflection, a plan for changed behaviour — but the harmed student's consent is required for face-to-face.

## Mistaken Goals — For Persistent Patterns

When the same student keeps repeating the same behaviour despite coaching, Positive Discipline suggests four "mistaken goals" — mistaken beliefs about how to belong:

| What you see | What the student believes | What makes it worse | What helps |
|-------------|--------------------------|--------------------|-----------|
| Attention-seeking (constant interruptions, clowning) | "I count only when I'm being noticed" | Scolding, hovering, giving constant attention | Give attention for contribution, not disruption. Redirect without audience |
| Power struggles (defiance, "you can't make me") | "I count only when I'm in control" | Engaging in the power struggle, warnings, threats | Offer limited choices ("A or B — which one?"). Disengage from the battle |
| Revenge (deliberate harm, cruelty) | "I count by hurting back — because I've been hurt" | Harsh punishment, shaming | Acknowledge the hurt underneath. Repair the relationship before the behaviour |
| Assumed inadequacy (withdrawal, "I can't") | "I count when people expect nothing of me" | Criticism, doing things for them, giving up | Small wins. Break tasks into manageable steps. Express faith in their ability |

> [!info]
> **How to use this in practice:** When you notice a pattern — the same child, the same kind of behaviour, again and again — ask yourself: which of these four goals might be driving this? Then test your hypothesis: if you change your response and the behaviour shifts, you've found the right lens. Bring your read to the weekly team huddle.

## Coaching Language — For In-the-Moment Redirects

These are your Whistle-tier scripts. Brief, warm, non-shaming:

**For off-task behaviour:**
- "What should you be doing right now?"
- "Look around — what do you notice about the room?"

**For choices:**
- "You can do A or B. Which one?"
- "I need you to [specific action]. You can choose how."

**For when you need to defer:**
- "I noticed something. We'll talk about it after the session."
- "I'm not going to address this now — but I will."

**For when a student is escalating:**
- "I can see you're frustrated. Take five minutes. I'll come find you."
- "This is important. And right now isn't the moment. Let's talk at [time]."

### Exercises

#### Multiple Choice: Choosing the right approach
A student has refused to participate in group work for the third session in a row. They sit quietly but don't contribute. Other students are frustrated. Which framework is most useful here?
- A) Mistaken Goals ✓
- B) NVC — have a private conversation about what you've observed
- C) Restorative Questions — ask who was affected
- D) Coaching language — redirect them back to the group
**Explanation:** A persistent pattern of withdrawal — especially quiet non-participation — often points to the "assumed inadequacy" mistaken goal. The student may believe they have nothing to contribute, or that their efforts will be criticised. Coaching language (redirecting) hasn't worked after three sessions. NVC is useful for the conversation, but the Mistaken Goals lens helps you understand what to address. Try small wins: give them a defined, achievable role in the group.
#### Voice Exercise: NVC follow-up conversation
**Scenario:** A student was dismissive to you during a session this morning — they said "this is pointless" when you asked them to put their phone away. You didn't respond in the moment (good — you avoided a public power struggle). Now it's after lunch and you're sitting down with them privately. Have the conversation using the NVC framework.
**Guidance:** A good response should:
- Start with an observation, not a judgement ("I noticed you said 'this is pointless' when I asked you to put your phone away" — NOT "You were rude to me")
- Share a brief feeling ("I was surprised / concerned")
- Name the need ("I need us to be able to work together respectfully, even when something feels boring")
- Ask with genuine curiosity what was going on for them
- End with a clear, specific request (not "be more respectful" but something concrete)
**AI Prompt:** Evaluate whether the response: (1) uses observation language rather than judgement/accusation, (2) shares a feeling without being dramatic or guilt-tripping, (3) asks with genuine curiosity what was going on — not as a rhetorical device, (4) ends with a specific request or agreement, not a vague "do better." Flag if the response includes sarcasm, lectures, references to "respect your elders," or attempts to make the student feel guilty.

#### Voice Exercise: Restorative conversation after harm
**Scenario:** Student A excluded Student B from their lunch group, saying "we don't want you here." Student B told a teacher. You're now sitting with Student A for a restorative conversation. Student B has said they're willing to join later if they feel ready. Walk through the conversation with Student A.
**Guidance:** A good response should:
- Ask what happened without leading ("Tell me what happened at lunch" — not "Why did you exclude B?")
- Ask what they were thinking at the time
- Help them see the impact without lecturing ("How do you think B felt?")
- Ask them to propose a repair ("What could help make this right?")
- Be clear that this matters — exclusion is a Yellow Card behaviour
- Not force an apology — the student needs to understand what they're apologising for first
**AI Prompt:** Evaluate whether the response: (1) follows the restorative question sequence rather than jumping to punishment or forced apology, (2) lets the student do most of the talking, (3) helps the student identify the impact on the other person, (4) has the student propose the repair rather than dictating it, (5) is firm about the seriousness without being punitive. Flag if the response includes "how would you feel if someone did that to you" (this is a guilt device, not genuine empathy-building) or if it forces an apology.

# Real Scenarios: Respect for Self

These are drawn from the full consequence matrix. For each scenario, notice the default card, the escalation path, and who handles it. The goal is calibration — so that what one teacher does, every teacher would do.

## Lateness

| Occurrence | Card | Response |
|-----------|------|----------|
| 1st time | Whistle | Brief, private, one-line: "You're late. What happened?" Note in your log |
| 3rd time in a month | Yellow Card | Short reflection: "How did my lateness affect the group?" Student makes up missed work in their own time |
| 5th+ / chronic | Yellow/Red Card | Advisor + parent meeting. Possible temporary loss of elective-choice privilege for 1–2 weeks |

## Not Following Through on Commitments

A student signed up for an enterprise role or community responsibility and isn't doing it.

| Occurrence | Card | Response |
|-----------|------|----------|
| 1st time | Whistle | "What's your plan?" — the student sets their own deadline for catching up |
| Repeated | Whistle/Yellow | Completion happens on the student's personal time, not during preferred activities. Reflection on how non-completion affected the community |
| Chronic | Yellow Card | Written contract with specific, small, measurable next steps. Weekly advisor check-ins. Possible loss of role in enterprise until reliability is rebuilt |

## Using Freedom of Movement to Avoid Learning

A student uses the freedom to move between spaces not to learn in a different way, but to avoid learning altogether.

| Occurrence | Card | Response |
|-----------|------|----------|
| 1st time | Whistle | Redirect: "Where are you supposed to be? What work are you returning to?" |
| Repeated | Yellow Card | 3–5 day reduced-freedom period: student works in a designated spot within adult sight-line |
| Chronic | Yellow Card | Earn-back plan with visible milestones. Full freedom restored after 2 weeks of on-task observation. Advisor + parent meeting |

## Substance Use

> [!warning]
> **Substance use (tobacco, alcohol, drugs, vapes) is always a Red Card from the first instance.** There is no Whistle for this.

| Occurrence | Card | Response |
|-----------|------|----------|
| 1st time | Red Card | Immediate removal. Parent notification same day. Counsellor engagement. 1–3 day in-school suspension with structured reflection. Restorative conference on return |
| Repeated | Red/Structural Review | At-home suspension (2–5 days). Written behavioural contract with explicit exit criteria. Mandated counselling. Possible exclusion from next residential trip |
| Chronic | Structural Review | Placement review with family. Referral to external support |

## Plagiarism / Unattributed AI Use

| Occurrence | Card | Response |
|-----------|------|----------|
| 1st time | Yellow Card | Work is not accepted. Student redoes it with full transparency and a written reflection on why honest attribution matters. Advisor and parents informed |
| Repeated | Yellow/Red Card | Formal integrity hearing with advisor + Head of Program. Loss of related privileges (e.g., independent presentation slots) until trust rebuilt |
| Chronic | Red Card | Written contract. Possible suspension from high-trust activities (exhibitions, external trips) for a defined period |

### Exercises

#### Multiple Choice: Freedom of movement
A student has been wandering between spaces for the third time this week. Each time, when redirected, they said "I was just going to the washroom" or "I was looking for my notebook." You suspect they're avoiding their independent work. What's the right response?
- A) Trust their explanation each time — you can't prove they're avoiding work
- B) Whistle — redirect them one more time and see if it stops
- C) Yellow Card ✓
- D) Red Card — they've been dishonest about their reasons
**Explanation:** Third occurrence this week triggers escalation from Whistle to Yellow Card. The response is specific and time-bound: work in a designated spot for 3–5 days. This isn't punishment — it's scaffolding. The student hasn't shown they can use freedom of movement for learning yet. They earn it back with visible milestones.
#### Voice Exercise: Commitment follow-up

## Section 6: Scenarios — Relationships & Identity
**Slug:** scenarios-relationships
**Estimated Minutes:** 25

### Content

**Handbook anchor:** Middle School Handbook, Chapter 11 — *When Things Go Hard*.

# Real Scenarios: Respect for Others

These are the hardest scenarios — and the ones where consistency matters most. A student who experiences identity-based harm needs to know that every adult will respond the same way.

## Identity-Based Mockery

This is the clearest Red Card in the matrix. Targeting someone's religion, caste, language, accent, appearance, body, gender, or sexuality is a Red Card from the first instance, every time, regardless of context.

| Step | Action |
|------|--------|
| 1 | STOP everything |
| 2 | Support the harmed student first. Check in. Affirm that this is being taken seriously |
| 3 | Separate the offender — calmly, without public shaming |
| 4 | Loop the Head of Middle School Program today |
| 5 | Within 24–48 hours: restorative conference with facilitator, harmed student (if they consent), offender, and supporters |
| 6 | Written repair plan |
| 7 | Parents of both students informed same day |

> [!warning]
> **"They didn't mean it" / "It was just a joke" is not a mitigating factor.** The card is determined by impact, not intent. A student who mockingly imitates another student's accent "as a joke" has still targeted their identity. Red Card.

**If repeated:** Loss of free-movement privileges for 1–2 weeks. Structured peer group reassignment. Intensified family partnership.

**If chronic:** Behavioural contract with defined exit criteria. Possible suspension. Placement review if unresolved.

## Exclusion & Social Cruelty

"You can't sit with us." Freezing someone out. Forming groups that deliberately exclude.

| Occurrence | Card | Response |
|-----------|------|----------|
| 1st time | Yellow Card | Name what you saw. Invite the excluding group into perspective-taking. Check in privately with the excluded student |
| Repeated | Yellow Card | Community circle addressing patterns of inclusion. Structured grouping for a defined period to interrupt cliques |
| Chronic / targeted | Red Card | Treat as targeted behaviour. Restorative conference. Parent partnership |

## Physical Harm

| Occurrence | Card | Response |
|-----------|------|----------|
| Rough play escalating | Yellow Card | Immediate stop. Both students heard separately, then brought together for a brief restorative check-in. Check on anyone who felt unsafe |
| Deliberate harm (hitting, kicking, throwing objects) | Red Card | Immediate removal. Harmed student supported first. Parent contact same day (both families). 1–2 day in-school suspension. Restorative conference before return |
| Repeated | Structural Review | At-home suspension. Mandated counselling. Behavioural contract. Possible trip exclusion |

## Retaliation Against a Reporter

> [!warning]
> **Retaliation is always a Red Card, treated as a separate harm.** If Student A reported Student B, and Student B retaliates against Student A — the retaliation is its own Red Card, regardless of what the original issue was. The reporter is protected and reassured. If students learn that reporting leads to retaliation, they stop reporting.

## Disrespect Toward Support Staff

Disrespectful behaviour toward drivers, nannies, admin, or cleaning staff is a **Yellow Card by default** — one tier higher than the same behaviour toward a teacher. This is a deliberate design choice: it signals that every adult in the community is treated with equal respect.

| Occurrence | Card | Response |
|-----------|------|----------|
| 1st time | Yellow Card | Named as a core value violation. Apology in person to the staff member. Advisor and parents informed |
| Repeated | Red Card | Restorative conversation with the affected staff member. Written reflection. Parent meeting |

## Online Behaviour

What happens on group chats and social media directed at a peer is treated as if it happened in person.

| Occurrence | Card | Response |
|-----------|------|----------|
| 1st time | Yellow/Red Card (depending on severity) | Screenshot evidence reviewed. Offender removes content. Restorative conference. Parents of both informed |
| Repeated | Red Card | Extended phone/device restrictions at school. Digital-citizenship reflection. Parent partnership |

## Section 7: Documentation & Parent Communication
**Slug:** documentation-parents
**Estimated Minutes:** 20

### Content

**Handbook anchor:** Middle School Handbook, Chapter 11 — *When Things Go Hard*; Chapter 12 — *The Parent Partnership*.

# Documentation That Actually Gets Read

We document so we can act, not so we can prove we acted. The test: can the next adult read the file and know what's happening in 60 seconds?

## Three Layers

| Layer | What | Where | Who | When |
|-------|------|-------|-----|------|
| Whistle layer | One-line note: "10 Apr — R. late again, 3rd this fortnight" | Teacher's own notebook or class doc | Teacher | Same day, 30 seconds |
| Yellow Card layer | One row per incident: date, student, teacher, what happened, card applied, re-entry plan, parent looped (y/n) | Shared behaviour tracker | Teacher writes, advisor reviews weekly | Within 24 hours |
| Red Card / Structural Review layer | Full write-up: incident, response, repair plan, signatures | Student file (permanent). Shared with all staff who interact with the student | Advisor curates | Same day |

> [!tip]
> **The Whistle layer is private and takes 30 seconds.** Don't overthink it. "R. late again, 3rd this fortnight" is a perfectly good log entry. The point is that when it becomes a Yellow Card, you can say "this is the 4th time" with confidence, not from memory.

> [!info]
> **The shared behaviour tracker is the heartbeat of this system.** If teachers don't fill it in within 24 hours, the advisor can't see patterns. If the advisor doesn't review it weekly, teachers are carrying patterns alone. Both sides of this discipline matter.

## Weekly Team Huddle (15 minutes)

Every week, 15 minutes. The advisor leads. Three questions:

1. **Who's on a contract right now?** (Status check — is it working?)
2. **Who's in a pattern this week?** (Names from the tracker)
3. **Does anything need to move up or across?** (Escalation decisions, handoffs between teachers)

That's it. Not a meeting about philosophy. A meeting about names.

# Parent Communication — Three Triggers

The biggest shift in this framework: we don't wait until Red Card to talk to parents.

## Trigger 1: SAFETY — Same Day, Any Card

If physical harm (to or from the student), substances, leaving campus, or serious emotional distress are involved — parents are called before the student goes home. Regardless of what card it was.

These are rare. But non-negotiable.

## Trigger 2: PATTERN — Friday Note, Yellow Card and Up

If a student has received any Yellow Card or higher in the week, OR three Whistles for the same behaviour, the advisor sends a short Friday note home.

**What the note says:**
- What we observed this week (specific, not vague)
- What we've been working on with the student
- What we agreed together

**What the note does NOT say:**
- It's not an accusation
- It's not a request for the parent to "handle it at home"
- It's not dramatic

> [!info]
> **This is the single biggest change from current practice.** The Friday note is what prevents the Red Card phone call from being the first time a parent hears about a pattern. It turns parent communication from adversarial to collaborative.

## Trigger 3: PROACTIVE — Monthly, Every Student

A regular short note about every student, even when nothing is on fire.

"She was curious about three things this month" counts. "He took real ownership of his enterprise role this week" counts.

**Why this matters:** When the safety call eventually comes — and for some families it will — it lands as a concern from someone who knows and cares about their child, not as a complaint from a stranger.

## Section 8: The Adult Agreement
**Slug:** adult-agreement
**Estimated Minutes:** 15

### Content

**Handbook anchor:** Middle School Handbook, Chapter 10 — *What You Are — and What You’re Not*; Chapter 11 — *When Things Go Hard*.

# What We Ask of Each Other

This framework only works if we hold it together. Six commitments from every adult:

## 1. Hold the tier

When another teacher has placed a student at a tier, uphold it. Don't re-negotiate it in front of the student. If you think the call was wrong, talk to the colleague privately — never in front of students or parents.

## 2. Loop early

If you're wondering whether to involve the advisor, the answer is yes. Early looping is strength, not weakness. Yellow Card and above always involves the advisor. Whistle can too — especially when you're unsure.

## 3. Document on the same day

Memory fades. A two-line entry within 24 hours is worth more than a polished write-up a week later. The tracker is only as good as what's in it.

## 4. Bring the hard calls to the Head

Red Card and Structural Review are never solo decisions. You will be supported — but you have to surface it. Carrying a serious incident alone is not heroism; it's a system failure.

## 5. Disagree in private

If you think a colleague made the wrong call, say so — to them, not in front of students or parents. Then bring it to the team huddle if needed. We calibrate together.

## 6. Repair ourselves too

If we lose our temper — and we will, because we're human — we model the apology. The same way we ask the students to. "I raised my voice earlier and that wasn't okay. I'm sorry." This is the most powerful teaching moment in the entire framework.

> "What one of us does, any of us would do."

## What Is NOT on the Table

Some clarity for ourselves and for parents. These practices are inconsistent with what PEP is and will never be used:

- Corporal discipline of any kind
- Public shaming or public dress-downs
- Withholding food, recess, or lunch as a consequence
- Using grades or academic assessment as a behavioural lever
- Sarcasm, mockery, or imitating the student's behaviour back at them
- Group punishment for one student's behaviour
- Indefinite "sit in the corner" with no repair pathway
- Making parent communication a threat — we involve parents to help, not to punish

> [!tip]
> **Naming what's NOT on the table protects you.** When a parent pushes for harsher consequences ("can't you just punish him properly?"), you can point to a published commitment. When you're exhausted and tempted to use sarcasm, the line is clear. When a colleague crosses it, you have standing to say something.

## When You're Exhausted

Some days you genuinely cannot do patient coaching. That's real. Here's the protocol:

**Use a holding response:** "I'm noticing something. We'll talk about it after lunch with [advisor]."

**Then hand it off:** Tell a colleague or the advisor, "I need someone else to take this one today." This is professional, not weak.

**The principle:** Restorative work done while you're depleted is worse than no response at all. A clumsy, angry Whistle does more damage than a deferred, calm Yellow Card.
